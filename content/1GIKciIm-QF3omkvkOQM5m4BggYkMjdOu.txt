WHAT IS THE PRACTICE?
On a quarterly basis, network leadership and campus leaders collaboratively conduct
observations and review data against a multi-phase rubric to evaluate the effectiveness of
program implementation and develop prioritized goals for improvement.

WHY IS IT IMPORTANT?
• Networks with multiple schools need systems and processes to ensure fidelity and
quality of program implementation, particularly for programs like Anchor.
• Across a network, outcomes can vary for students based on the way programs are
implemented. This variation in outcomes may be even greater for students with
disabilities and those in emotional support programs.
• Networks need ways to identify positive examples of programs and determine how
to allocate resources to ensure students are well served. Networks can measure
programs against these exemplars and use them to devise action plans for program
improvement.
• Importantly, program improvement using a variety of data sources on an ongoing basis
helps improve the academic, social-emotional, and behavioral learning that students
experience.
• Networks can use data from program evaluation to inform network-level support,
coaching, and professional development.

The Challenge
Ensuring High-Quality Programming
Across the Mastery Network
Mastery Schools created the Anchor program to provide an educational environment where students
with emotional and behavioral disabilities can explicitly learn skills that meet their IEP goals. Mastery
created Anchor to be a supportive setting that can flexibly increase the amount of time students
receive education in general education classrooms. To accomplish this, Anchor has multiple levels of
programmatic support and intervention, ranging from students receiving direct support for 20% of their
day to others receiving support for more than half the day when needed. Mastery now offers Anchor
programming at each of their secondary campuses.
School-level program directors need to ensure that the skills students are working to master align with
their IEP goals, that staffing is adequate to provide various levels of support and education for those
skills, and that students have the necessary reinforcement and opportunities to master them. Mastery’s
network-level director for Anchor needs to oversee program implementation fidelity and student
outcomes across campuses so that all students in Anchor programs receive a high-quality program. To
make this possible, Mastery needed a valid and reliable way to monitor program effectiveness, provide
feedback on strengths and growth areas, and provide targeted support for each school’s Anchor
program.

The Practice
Using a Program-Specific Rubric
to Identify Areas of Strength
and Need
To keep the Anchor program consistent in implementation
and outcomes for students, the Mastery network team
developed a “Must Haves” rubric. The rubric is aligned
with the Anchor program components that are essential
for students to experience the program’s full benefits.
The Must Haves show that the program is implementing
each component as intended. The rubric also pushes
each school’s program to progress from meeting minimum
expectations — referred to as Phase 1 on the rubric —
to a higher level of implementation that can result in the
best outcomes for students — the rubric’s Phase 2. The
table on the following page presents each Must Haves
component and what it shows about the implementation.

Schedule &

There is intentionality given to both student and staff schedules; staffing and schedules

Student programming is data-driven and aligned. Students’ present levels of need drive

SAM Skills

SAM skills are the foundation of the Anchor program and visible to all stakeholders. SAM

Data Collection
& Point Card

Student data is front and center, and Kickboard/Point Card is the system to ensure
strong data collection. The Kickboard/Point data allows staff/student to reflect on student

behavior through a token economy. Archived data provides longitudinal data to analyze
trends over time.

Behavioral
Feedback

Behavioral Feedback is the evidence-based intervention that we use to change student
behavior. We use the intervention with fidelity across all settings.

Behavioral
Escalation
Response Plan

Trained professionals are available and skilled at supporting our students during all
phases of the Crisis Development Model. Those trained professionals have a schoolnormed process and designated area(s) where this support occurs. Professionals will
manage students’ surface behaviors in order to maximize their time spent learning in the
classroom.

Level System

Multiple data points are consistently collected, analyzed, and responded to in an effort to
provide appropriate support and reinforcement to students based on trends in their skill
acquisition. Student’s level is a reflection of their current performance and demonstrates
the level of adult support and therapeutic programming that is required for the student to
be successful.

Safety Planning
& Therapeutic
Holds

In the rare instance when a student in the Anchor program is displaying significant risk
behavior(s), members of the Anchor team have both the skills and resources to ensure
student safety 100% of the time.

Daily Skills
Class

Students in the Anchor program receive social and emotional skill development through
a daily skills class in order to promote the transition of skill acquisition across multiple
settings. Curriculum should be aligned with SAM skills and adjusted to meet trends and
students’ skill development needs. Participation is based on the student performance
data (student Level and behavioral goal growth).

Individual
Counseling

Students receive quality school-based counseling as a related service based on their
individual needs. Counseling goals are aligned with the students’ IEP goals and should
be determined by the IEP team by referencing assessment data, observation data, and
performance data.

Small
Therapeutic
Groups

Small therapeutic groups (STG) are readily available to all students within the Anchor
program and are strategically and effectively used to address targeted student needs.

At Mastery’s Simon Gratz High School in Philadelphia, Veronica Koff manages all of these pieces with
expertise. Her network-level manager, Ann McKetta, helps her keep the program improving through
regular meetings to evaluate the program’s health and determine what types of campus support and
ongoing professional development are needed. Ann and Veronica meet quarterly to document artifacts
and analyze data demonstrating how Anchor is being implemented using the Must Haves rubric.
This routine allows Veronica and Ann to set goals for Gratz’s program together and ensure the best
outcomes for students.

Collecting Data Across the Program
The quarterly meetings provide the school and network leaders opportunities to gather data across
Anchor’s whole program for measurement against the rubric’s criteria. To gather evidence on the
school’s performance on the program components, Veronica leads Ann through various Anchor
program spaces to observe and collect data together.
For example, during a recent third-quarter visit to the school, Veronica and Ann focused on Gratz’s
current performance on Goal Alignment & Progress Monitoring. Ann could see that Gratz had not yet
set up systems to conduct ongoing progress monitoring three or more times per quarter and therefore
was performing at Phase 1 of Goal Alignment (see the table on the following page for rubric details).
In their walkthrough debrief, Ann and Veronica used their observation data to develop a plan for how
Gratz could progress toward Phase 2 on the program component.
As Gratz implements the plan, Veronica can ask Ann to come back at any time to observe the changes
they have made, rather than waiting for the next quarter’s formal observations.

Looking for Areas of Growth
During each quarterly meeting, Ann and Veronica look for
places where there are opportunities for growth or for greater
focus on a component of the program. First, they look for
program fidelity, that is, whether the Must Haves are present
(Phase 1). Then they work toward program quality (Phase
2). Gratz’s program has already met Phase 1 expectations
across most of the program components, so when Veronica
and Ann meet, they look down and across the entire rubric to
find components where the program is not yet at Phase 2.

Making Plans for Improvement
After analyzing the data at each visit, Ann and Veronica devise a plan to reach their improvement goals,
and they discuss how they will measure improvement.
In the example visit, for the goal of improving the behavioral feedback students receive, the pair came
up with a few specific action steps that Veronica could take going forward:
• Train staff on the quality of discrete skill feedback.
• Have staff implement discrete skill feedback.
• Monitor data at the staff and student levels in Kickboard.

The Impact
Using Data to Drive Program Improvement Across Schools
The Anchor Must Haves rubric drives goal setting and focus areas for progress for program staff. While
the data helps identify areas for program improvement at each school, it can also be analyzed at the
network level to ensure programmatic alignment across Anchor campuses in the Mastery network. Over
time, Mastery has been able to improve the Anchor program design, fidelity, and quality across their
network to better educate students with emotional and behavioral disabilities.

Mastery Schools is a part of the Marshall Street Networked
Improvement Community, a three-year intentional
community of 10 public charter school networks nationwide

networks identified over 20 best practices that improve
learning outcomes and school culture for students positioned
