Literature Review
ABSTRACT
Collaboration between general and special educators across all levels of the school
system has long been seen as a best practice when considering how to serve students
with disabilities effectively. There is evidence in the research base that stronger
collaboration is correlated with better results for both educators and students. One
particular form of collaboration, co-teaching, has emerged as a leading approach to
collaboration at the classroom level. Co-teaching approaches are buoyed by a strong body
of research and have the potential to serve students with learning and thinking differences.
This literature review synthesizes the existing research on educator collaboration broadly
as well as the specific practice of co-teaching, focusing on both defining the practices
and explaining their various uses. Research selected for inclusion in this literature review
includes seminal texts as well as literature published from 2008 to 2023. This review is
designed for school district administrators considering adopting or improving collaborative
practices in their districts.

Introduction
In this literature review, we share a concise overview of collaboration practices and methods with a
particular focus on co-teaching, an emerging best practice and service delivery model grounded in
the philosophy of inclusive education. Our purpose is to equip school district staff who are considering
adopting or improving collaborative practices between general educators and special educators with a
summary of the research on collaboration so that staff can make informed and thoughtful implementation
decisions for their districts. The literature review was conducted by Marshall Street, a coalition of
educators working to systematically improve opportunities for students across the United States.
This literature review is part of a larger collection of improvement tools for districts. The Networked
Improvement Community for Students with Disabilities (NIC), a group of 10 public charter school
networks serving more than 75,000 students nationwide, launched in 2019 to make dramatic gains in

What is educator collaboration?
Collaboration in education has been defined as a “mutual effort to plan, implement, and evaluate the
educational program for a given student” (Correa et al. 2005 in Hernandez 2013) and as “individuals
with diverse expertise working together to achieve mutually agreed upon objectives” (Snell and Janney
2000 in Hernandez 2013). When collaboration focuses on supporting students with disabilities, it takes
on many forms, including both direct and indirect collaboration. In direct collaboration, a specialist
works directly with a child. In contrast, in indirect collaboration, a specialist works directly with another
teacher or teachers, taking on an advisory, consultative, inspirer, or sparring partner role (Hansen et al.
2020), including sometimes working in a co-teaching capacity.
Sometimes, collaboration in schools happens within larger teams, where general and special educators
work together to make decisions about students (Friend et al. 2010; Blackwell and Rossetti 2014;
Education Resource Strategies 2021) during dedicated collaboration times. Ripley (1997) describes
how collaborative planning must happen at the district, building, and classroom levels for schools to
achieve inclusive practices. However, much of the literature about collaboration in education eventually
hones in on co-teaching as a best practice service-delivery model (Friend 2008; Villa et al. 2005;
Ronfeldt et al. 2015; Scanlon and Baker 2012). As the most discussed service delivery model situated
within collaboration, literature on co-teaching is covered in more detail later in this review.

Why is Special and General Educator Collaboration
Important?
In the literature, the most discussed reason for educators to collaborate is that it leads to improved
teacher practice and knowledge. As teachers collaborate, they can share specialist approaches,
forms of knowledge, and various skills and strategies (Hansen et al. 2020; Winn and Blanton 2005).
Additionally, as educators spend time working with one another, they begin to develop shared beliefs
about students, teaching, and learning (Winn and Blanton 2005). In a study of over 8,000 teachers,
Ronfeldt et al. (2015) found almost 90% of teachers surveyed viewed being on an instructional team
as either helpful or very helpful and specifically saw collaborating on the development of instructional
strategies to be the most useful. Teachers in another study reported that collaborative planning allowed
them to include instructional accommodations in their lessons by balancing the content-area expertise
of general educators with pedagogical knowledge and specialized supports from special educators
(Scanlon and Baker 2012). There is even evidence that when teachers change schools, moving to
a school with more collaborative practices, the rate at which their teaching skill improves increases
(Ronfeldt et al. 2015).
There is also evidence that stronger educator collaboration leads to improved student outcomes.
Ronfeldt et al. (2015) found that schools with instructional teams that engaged in better collaboration
had higher achievement gains in both math and reading than schools that did not collaborate as well.
This supports previous research showing that schools with higher levels of collaboration also have
higher levels of student achievement (Goddard et al. 2007; Goddard et al. 2010 in Ronfeldt et al. 2015).
Collaboration between educators often leads to greater inclusion of students with disabilities in the
general education classroom (Bausela 2003, Boavida and da Ponte 2011, and Lozano et al. 2010 in

What is Co-Teaching?
A widely used form of collaboration, co-teaching is a service-delivery option that supports the
philosophy of inclusive education (Alnasser 2020; Anderson 2008). The most commonly cited definition
of co-teaching in the literature is that of Friend (2008, 9): “Co-teaching occurs when a general education
teacher and a specialist — often a special education teacher, reading specialist, speech/language
therapist, or bilingual teacher — work as partners to teach a diverse group of students.” Further, coteaching presumes:
• Instruction for diverse learners primarily occurs concurrently and within the same
classroom space. (Basso and McCoy 2007 in Anderson 2008; Friend 2008; Friend and Cook
2003 in Simmons and Magiera 2007; Gately and Gately 2001; Cook and Friend 1995 in Murawski
and Swanson 2001).
• Teaching partners are both two equally licensed adults. One is a generalist with expertise
in the grade band or content area and the other is a specialist who is licensed as a specialist
education teacher, reading specialist, or the like, whose background is in adapting and modifying
instruction for students with special needs and learning disabilities (Basso and McCoy 2007 in
Anderson 2008; Friend 2008; Friend and Cook 2003 in Simmons and Magiera 2007; Cook and
Friend 1995 in Murawski and Swanson 2001).
• Both teachers are jointly responsible for the academic outcomes of all the students in the
classroom. (Basso and McCoy 2007 in Anderson 2008; Friend 2008; Gately and Gately 2001;
Keefe and Moore 2004; Murawski and Swanson 2001).
• Both teachers engage equitably in joint collaborative planning, instruction, assessment,
and reflection. (Dove and Honigsfeld 2018; Friend 2008; Gately and Gately 2001; Murawski and
Lochner 2010).
Importantly, Dove and Honigsfeld (2018) and Anderson (2008) note that co-teaching is NOT:
• The simple placement of two teachers in a classroom such as:
– one person teaching while another person has a prep period,
– one teacher conducting a lesson while the other watches,
– one teacher dictating over the other what is to be taught and how it is to be taught, or
– one person being assigned as a tutor.
• The combining of two different content classrooms (e.g., ELA and History).
Co-teaching typically refers to the service-delivery model of collaboratively educating students with
disabilities alongside their general education peers, though co-teaching is also being implemented
for multilingual learners and gifted and talented students (Friend et al. 2010). Some students with

Why is Co-Teaching Important?
For decades, the public education system in the United States has failed to effectively serve students
with a diverse set of needs, particularly students with disabilities. Inclusion classrooms — classrooms in
which students with and without disabilities are served together — are both an educational requirement
and a best practice for serving students with disabilities due to governmental mandates such as No
Child Left Behind (NCLB), the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA), and the Every Student
Succeeds Act (ESSA) and research showing academic gains from inclusion classrooms (Alnasser
2020; Brendle, Lock, and Piazza 2017; Cole et al. n.d.; Cramer et al. 2010; Weiss, Glaser, and Lloyd
2020). Yet, too often, schools integrate students rather than fully including them. Integrated education
places students with diverse needs in general education classrooms without providing them with
additional support to open access to learning. Inclusive education, on the other hand, is the systemic
change of an educational institution to provide an equitable and participatory learning experience for all
students (Hehir, Pascucci, and Pascucci 2016).
According to a recent nationwide survey of education data, 67% of students in special education
receive instruction in general education classrooms for at least 80% of the school day (National
Center for Education Statistics 2023). The large number of students with special needs integrated in
general education classrooms — who, due to the disproportionate rates of Black and Latinx students
being referred for special education services, are often Black and Latinx (National Center for Learning
Disabilities 2020) — leaves educators with the important question of how to implement inclusion
classrooms well (Alnasser 2020; Soslau, Gallo-Fox, and Scantlebury 2019 in Alnasser 2020).
Co-teaching has emerged as a leading strategy for effectively meeting the needs of students with
disabilities in an inclusive environment. While co-teaching is not required as a method of instruction by
governmental mandates (Cramer et al. 2010; DeMartino and Specht 2018), schools across the United
States have embraced co-teaching due to:
• A wide research base on co-teaching

Limitations of Co-Teaching
Few mentions were made in the literature regarding limitations (limitations are defined as
shortcomings or weaknesses of the approach) of co-teaching, or of collaborative practices in
general. One such mention was that students with disabilities received less specially designed
instruction (ASCD 2015). Additionally, anecdotally, schools transitioning to co-teaching noticed an
increase in academic outcomes for students with disabilities after introducing co-teaching, and
then experienced a leveling off of academic gains after a period of time (ASCD 2015). Notably, for
multilingual co-teaching classrooms, ESOL teachers “felt restricted from using their greatest assets
in the mainstream classroom. In particular, the use of native language and the ability to focus on the
complexities of language development were not leveraged to the highest potential” (McClure and
Cahnman-Taylor 2010 in Bauler and Kang 2020, 4).

While limitations were less frequently discussed in the literature, implementation challenges were
frequently cited. The main challenges of co-teaching include a lack of co-planning time, lack of
administrative support, need for ongoing training/lack of training support, relationship difficulties
between co-teachers, and increased workload for general education and specialist teachers (Alnasser
2020; Anderson 2008; Brendle, Lock, and Piazza 2017; Winn and Blanton 2005). Importantly, these
challenges are significant enough to prevent many schools from implementing co-teaching with fidelity.
Because these challenges to implementing co-teaching are significant, they are examined more
thoroughly in the “Necessary Conditions for Successful Implementation” section of this literature review.

Common Implementation Frameworks
The six models of co-teaching as defined by Friend et al. (2010) is the most commonly cited framework
for co-teaching in the literature. An additional framework is Dove and Honigsfeld’s seven models of coteaching for multilingual learners (2018).
Friend et al.’s (2010, 12) six models of co-teaching are listed below. Of the six models, Friend et al.
(2010) notes that models 1, 2, and 3 are far more beneficial to students than models 4, 5, and 6:
1. Station Teaching involves dividing students into three groups and rotating the groups from
station to station, where they are taught by the co-teachers at two stations and working
independently at the third;
2. Parallel Teaching requires each of the co-teachers to instruct half of the students, presenting
the same lesson to provide instructional differentiation and increased student participation;
3. Alternative Teaching involves one teacher providing instruction to the majority of students
while the other teacher works with a small group for remediation, enrichment, or assessment;
4. Teaming requires that co-teachers lead large-group instruction, with both teachers lecturing,
representing different viewpoints and multiple methods of solving problems;
5. One Teach, One Assist, also identified as Supportive Teaching, involves one co-teacher
leading instruction, while the other teacher circulates among the students providing individual
assistance;
6. One Teach, One Observe involves one of the co-teachers leading large-group instruction
while the other teacher gathers academic, behavioral, or social data on specific students or the
class group.
Dove and Honigsfeld’s (2018) seven models of co-teaching for multilingual learners closely map
to Friend et al.’s (2010) six models of co-teaching. The chart that follows is a crosswalk of the two
frameworks, combined with corresponding benefits and challenges compiled by Anderson (2008, 39).

The literature notes that the best results for students occur when teaching teams use a variety of
models (Scruggs, Mastropieri, and McDuffie 2007 in Alnasser 2020; Solis et al. 2012 in Alnasser
2020), in particular, station teaching, parallel teaching, and alternative teaching. Despite this guidance,
supportive teaching is the most commonly used co-teaching model, followed by parallel teaching,
because they require less co-planning time (Alnasser 2020; Friend et al. 2010; Keefe and Moore 2004;
King-Sears and Strogilos 2020; Scruggs, Mastropieri, and McDuffie 2007 in Alnasser 2020; Solis et al.
2012 in Alnasser 2020).
Additionally, the literature notes that over-reliance on supportive teaching can have the disadvantage
of placing one teacher in an assistant role, negatively affecting co-teaching efficacy and teacher morale
(Basso and McCoy 2007 in Anderson 2008; Dove and Honigsfeld 2018; Scruggs, Mastropieri, and
McDuffie 2007 in Alnasser 2020) and can lead to student views that students with disabilities are a
separate group and not an actual part of the general education classroom (Embury and Kroeger 2012).

Efficacy of Co-Teaching on Academic Outcomes
and For Whom
There is definitive evidence that inclusion classrooms (either solo with pull-out and push-in services
or co-taught) increase academic outcomes for students with disabilities (Cole et al. n.d.). Because of
the wide variability of implementation, there is inconclusive data regarding the extent to which teacher
practices, including co-teaching, cause or are correlated with the increase (King-Sears et al. 2021;
Hanover Research 2012; Murawski and Swanson 2001). Primarily, this is due to datasets that do not
lend themselves to large-scale standardized research. For example, large datasets reviewed in the
Murawski and Swanson (2001) meta-analysis lacked disaggregated data for co-taught vs. non-cotaught classrooms as well as disaggregated data for students with disabilities compared to students
without disabilities. In addition, the definitions of co-teaching, implementation structures, and fidelity
of co-teaching vary wildly, not only from district to district, but also across individual classrooms within
schools (Hanover Research 2012). Further, many studies lack specific descriptions of the types of
co-teaching approaches used (King-Sears et al. 2021). These study limitations have so far precluded
conclusive results about the efficacy of co-teaching.
In the most recent meta-analysis on co-teaching, King-Sears et al. (2021) compared the academic
achievement of students with disabilities in co-taught classes to the achievement of students with
disabilities in special education settings such as resource rooms or self-contained classrooms. The
study authors conducted a comprehensive search of published and gray literature, which resulted in
26 eligible studies for a total of 3,714 students with disabilities. The analysis found a positive moderate
effect size (g = 0.47) for students in co-taught settings compared to students in special education
settings. Yet, crucially, the studies used for the meta-analysis lacked information about co-teaching
instructional practices and equivalency of students assigned to co-taught or special education settings.
Thus, it is unclear how much of the positive moderate effect size is due to co-teaching, as opposed to
the positive effect inclusion classrooms have on academic outcomes. More research is needed that
compares the academic performance of students with disabilities in solo-taught inclusion classrooms to
those in co-taught inclusion classrooms.

The lack of conclusive evidence of efficacy means there is also a lack of evidence on sub-group
performance, particularly for target populations of Black and Latinx students with disabilities
experiencing poverty. No literature surveyed had subgroup data for Black students, Black students
experiencing poverty, or Black students with disabilities experiencing poverty. Similarly, no literature
surveyed had subgroup data for Latinx students, Latinx students experiencing poverty, or Latinx
students with disabilities experiencing poverty. However, a 2020 study on the effects of co-teaching for
multilingual learners in K–4 classrooms in New York found that language proficiency scores increased
over two years of co-teaching instruction (Bauler and Kang 2020). As 78% of the United States’
multilingual learners are Hispanic (National Center for Education Statistics 2021), this study may be
used as a partial proxy, though the study did not report on disability or income status.
Although the meta-analyses did not provide conclusive evidence of the efficacy of co-teaching, they
also did not provide conclusive evidence that co-teaching, regardless of fidelity, has negative effects on
academic outcomes (Murawski and Swanson 2001; King-Sears et al. 2021). Thus, co-teaching appears
to be a strategy for effectively implementing inclusion classrooms with limited downsides and the
potential for positive academic gains when executed with integrity, though more research is needed.
Given the limited downsides and potential benefits of co-teaching, it is important to note that Black
and Latinx students in special education programs are more likely to be removed from inclusive general
education classrooms than their white peers (de Valenzuela et al. 2006, Fierros and Conroy 2002,
and Sullivan 2011 in Waitoller and Artiles 2013), leading to situations where Black and Latinx students
with disabilities are less likely to see the benefits of collaboration and co-teaching. This is especially
troubling because co-taught classrooms often implement aspects of Universal Design for Learning
(UDL), which helps to make them inclusive not just of students of varying abilities, but also of different
races and socioeconomic statuses as well (Sullivan and Thorius 2010). By removing Black and Latinx
students with disabilities from these co-taught classrooms, schools are pushing students of color further
from opportunity.

Necessary Conditions for Successful
Implementation
Successful collaboration requires a school- and district-level belief in the value of true inclusion. With
this vision, districts and schools can create the policies, staffing, structures, schedules, professional
development, and culture changes that undergird effective collaborative relationships and processes,
including but not limited to co-teaching.
High-quality, high-impact co-teaching and other forms of collaboration can be achieved through
thoughtful strategic planning on the part of districts, school administrators, and teachers (Ripley 1997).
When time and intention are used to purposefully plan for the structure of multiple adults working
together across a school community, excellence is possible. Yet implementing collaborative practices
with fidelity can be difficult, especially because of the traditional siloing of general and special educators,
particularly in teacher education (Winn and Blanton 2005). The literature specifically highlighted several
challenges to implementing co-teaching effectively. When these challenges were not addressed,

Conclusion
In conclusion, collaboration between general and special educators is crucially important for schools
that are working to become more inclusive of all learners. Within the broad landscape of collaborative
practices, co-teaching is currently the best strategy for effectively meeting the needs of all students
within classrooms. Co-teaching can be effective for a myriad of students with learning and thinking
differences, including students with disabilities and multilingual learners; thus, creating a district culture
of co-teaching can benefit many subgroups of diverse learners. District staff looking to adopt or improve
their collaborative practices must recognize that many system-level changes — including scheduling,
professional development, culture, and staffing — will be necessary to implement co-teaching, coplanning, and other collaborative teams effectively.
While the literature has not yet been able to provide conclusive evidence of co-teaching efficacy on
academic scores, the literature confirms the efficacy of inclusion (solo and co-taught) classrooms
and suggests collaboration has positive benefits on classroom culture, student sense of belonging,
and teacher job satisfaction, which are all foundational elements to increasing outcomes for diverse
learners. In short, collaboration between educators traditionally tasked with serving students in silos
has the potential, if the necessary conditions are met, to dramatically increase academic outcomes for
diverse learners. If nothing else, co-teaching is an approach to meeting the inclusion requirements of
IDEA and ESSA with a whole host of benefits for both students and teachers.
Best practices for collaboration in schools and networks are discussed in the companion collection,
Opening Doors to Collaboration.
