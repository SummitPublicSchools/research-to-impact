Enabling Conditions: Repeated Reading
A Guide for Leaders
This guide is a companion to the Repeated Reading practice in the Marshall
Research-to-Impact Practices, addressing common needs and barriers at a systems and
site-leader level.

Why School Leaders Matter
School and system leaders play a decisive role in whether Repeated Reading takes hold and
endures. Their responsibility is to:
● reinforce a culture where reading growth is both expected and celebrated and promote
literacy as a schoolwide responsibility (Culture and Knowledge);
● build infrastructure, including reliable assessment systems, and sufficient staffing
(Infrastructure and Staff Commitment); and
● invest in tools for tracking progress, appropriate materials, time to deliver the practice
consistently, and teacher development (Time Allocation, Materials, and Tools).

Enabling Conditions for Repeated Reading
Culture and Knowledge
● School leaders communicate and cultivate in all teachers the belief that supporting
foundational reading skills is their role
● Teachers understand the relationship between reading fluency and overall reading
proficiency
● Teachers and support staff have autonomy to plan for literacy interventions and to
determine when and where to schedule those interventions within the site model the
school selects
● Students understand the purpose of fluency practice and set and track their own growth
goals

Infrastructure and Staff Commitment
● School leaders select a site model (whole-class, small group, or intensive 1:1) and align
staffing, data, and time to that model
● Assessment structures — universal screeners and diagnostic assessments (as needed
for your model)— to identify students and track progress
● Dedicated staff members to enact Repeated Reading practice
● Instructional coach to provide ongoing coaching and implementation support
● Designated team to monitor the effectiveness of the practice

Time Allocation, Materials, and Tools
● School leaders need time to develop and deliver training, and time to support
data-based instructional decision making
● Teachers need time to build their capacity, implement the practice with students, and to
participate in data-based instructional decision making
● Educators have ready access to the materials and tools needed to run the practice
See below for resources that strengthen these enabling conditions and offer solutions to
common barriers.

Culture and Knowledge
Building Culture and Cultivating Knowledge
Implementation thrives when teachers see foundational reading as part of their role — and have
the autonomy to flexibly embed Repeated Reading into their classrooms.

Key Strategies:
● Build shared understanding across staff of how fluency supports comprehension and
access to complex texts
● Emphasize that all secondary teachers — ELA, content area, intervention — play a role
in foundational reading for all students
● Empower teachers to decide when and where fluency fits in their daily schedule
● Use staff PD to model and reinforce the purpose and structures of the routine
● Provide training in foundational reading components
○ Ensure teachers understand how accuracy, rate, and prosody develop, and how to
coach students through each element during practice.
○ Train students and educators on how to give partner feedback on accuracy, rate, and
prosody, using modeling, sentence stems, and rotating reader and listener roles.
● Connect data to classroom practice
○ Train teachers to interpret students’ reading levels and translate those data into
embedded supports—such as selecting appropriate texts, pairing students
strategically, and integrating fluency routines within content instruction.

Building Student Ownership and Motivation as Readers
Middle and high school students invest in Repeated Reading when they see it as a path to
grade-level learning. Leaders build that investment by helping educators share purpose, support
goal setting, celebrate growth, and choose texts that matter to students.

Key Strategies:
● Share the purpose of fluency practice so students see it as a path to grade-level
learning, not remediation
● Create systems or activities for students to set their own goals for fluency, as well as
review their progress data
● Celebrate growth publicly and regularly with students and families (e.g., literacy award
nights, family phone calls)
● Train educators to embed authentic, grade-level texts connected to student interests and
course content

Infrastructure and Staff Commitment
Developing Strong Infrastructure
Teachers need assessment systems to identify which students may benefit most and track
whether the Repeated Reading practice or targeted intervention is working.

Key Strategies:
● Ensure universal screeners are in place and accessible for educators and leaders (e.g.,
Lexile-based tools) to monitor site and student level trends, as well as identify students
who may benefit from intervention
● Provide guidance on diagnostic tools to separate fluency needs from decoding needs
● Align data collection with data analysis and intervention decision-making cycles (e.g.,
every 6–8 weeks)
● Use dashboards or trackers that simplify cold/hot read analysis

Secure and Scale Staff Commitment
Implementation sticks when teams—not just individuals—own the work. Dedicated staff, shared
roles, and regular coaching ensure that Repeated Reading becomes a consistent part of
instruction, not an add-on or short-term fix.

Key Strategies:
● Select a site model (whole-class, small group, or intensive) and align staffing, data, and
time to that model
● Identify and support lead implementers (e.g., content teachers, interventionists, aides)
based on the variation used
● Use team time to norm on expectations and adjust the plan based on student progress
● Designate a coach to monitor implementation and support educators (see Key Moves for
Coaches below)
● Plan ahead for scale: build fluency work into preservice, coaching cycles, and schedules

Key Moves for Coaches:
● Model the routine with educators before they launch it with students
● Observe student pairs and give feedback on the quality of partner feedback (accuracy,
rate, prosody)
● Review cold/hot read data with teachers during fluency data huddles and adjust
groupings, texts, or pacing
● Differentiate support. For new implementers, focus on routine fidelity and timing. For
experienced implementers, focus on text selection and feedback quality
● Support paraeducators and support staff using the Protocol for Staff Supporting
Repeated Reading

Time Allocation, Materials, and Tools
Tend to Time Allocation
Fluency routines may be brief—but without dedicated time, they quickly fall off the schedule.
Implementation requires leaders and teachers to proactively plan when, where, and how fluency
fits in.

Key Strategies:
● Protect time for fluency in core instruction—aim for 3 times per week, 5–10 minutes per
session
● Help teachers map fluency into the flow of existing classes (e.g., warm-up activities,
small group sessions, content preview)
● Provide guidance for adapting the routine during short weeks or testing windows
● Include planning and data review time in team meetings to reflect on student progress
● Consider using scope-and-sequence calendars to keep the routine visible and on track

Ensure Access to Effective Tools and Materials
Simple, accessible materials make it easier for teachers to implement fluency routines
consistently—and for students to track their growth over time.

Key Strategies:
● Use short, content-based texts (200–350 words) from grade-level curriculum
● Convert passages into curriculum-based measurement (CBM) format using tools like the
Oral Reading Fluency Passage Generator or use an AI tool to create customized,
topic-aligned passages
● Equip classrooms with timers, calculators, and graphing sheets for student tracking
● Create shared folders with reusable tools: pairing templates, feedback sentence stems,
error-tracking sheets
● Train students and staff to use tools independently over time
