Set Your Vision for Routine Data Cycles
Leverage this resource to audit your current state and set a vision for your data practice.
This activity supports you in defining a clear vision for routine data cycles at your school — where the practice is headed and what it will make possible for your students and educators.
Directions: Use Part 1 to audit your current state and name your leverage point. In Part 2, visualize the future state and write a clear, concise, and inspirational vision statement you can share with stakeholders. Use the Optional section to workshop your vision before you finalize it.
Companion Resource: Implementing Routine Data Cycles to Rapidly Address Student Needs: Executive Summary
Part 1: Audit Your Current State
What is the biggest obstacle to data-driven instructional decision-making at our school?
Base your answer on your observations and reflections.

What is a bright spot we can build on?
What's already working that we can do more of?

What is our leverage point — one shift that would unlock the most progress?
This may be your most fragile enabling condition, or something else.

Part 2: Visualize the Future State
Looking ahead to the end of your first full year of implementation, what do you want to be true about routine data cycles at your school?
Think about your leverage point above — what will be different once you've moved it?

What do you want routine data cycles to look like, sound like, and feel like for students and adults when it is working well?
Look like
routines, behaviors, structures

Sound like
language, questions, collaboration

Feel like
confidence, clarity, belonging

How will students in your priority population, particularly students with disabilities, be better served by routine data cycles a year from now than they are today?

Write your vision statement.
It should be clear, concise, and inspirational — written so it can be shared with staff, families, and other stakeholders.

Who will you share this vision with first?
Make a plan to communicate it to others and build momentum for the practice to launch.

OPTIONAL Sentence Stems: Writing a Vision Statement
* Option 1: By [end of school year], students with disabilities at [my school] will __________ because educators __________.
* Option 2: By [end of school year], routine data cycles [describe it] because educators ___________. In turn, students will ______________.
Example: By 2028, routine data cycles are a core part of how we learn and improve together. Educators use data routinely to celebrate progress, identify needs, and make timely shifts that accelerate outcomes for Black and Latino students with disabilities, both on their own and with their teams.
OPTIONAL: Strengthening the Vision — Support & Scaffolds
These optional questions can be used to refine, test, and strengthen your vision by considering what effective spread, depth of quality, sustainability, and ownership of routine data cycles look like at your school.
Directions: Pick the questions that feel most useful. Use the “Think about” ideas as starting points, then go back to your vision statement and revise it. Focus on where you’re headed, not where you are today. Planning next steps comes after vision setting.

REACH: When routine data cycles are working well, which classrooms and students are served?
Think about: How many teams or classrooms? Why these classrooms? Are students with the greatest needs included? What would be true by the end of your first full year?
QUALITY: What would you see in lessons and classrooms that shows routine data cycles are making a difference?
Think about: How lessons change when teachers plan using data. What students with disabilities can now access, or what teachers now know about student performance.
SUSTAINABILITY: What would keep routine data cycles going over time, even if staff or leaders change?
Think about: Structures, habits and routines in place to ensure it lasts. What could get in the way (no need to solve it now). What routine data cycles look like in a normal week.
OWNERSHIP: Who leads routine data cycles at your school, and how do they keep improving it?
Think about: How teachers and leaders share the work. Which roles naturally hold it. How the practice grows without outside support.
