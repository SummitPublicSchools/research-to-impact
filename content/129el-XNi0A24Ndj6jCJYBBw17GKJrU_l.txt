A Guide for Leaders
This guide is a companion to the Co-Planning practice in the Marshall Research-to-Impact Practices,
addressing common needs and barriers at a systems and site-leader level.

Why School Leaders Matter
School and system leaders play a decisive role in whether co-planning takes hold and endures. Their
responsibility is not only to provide resources or training, but also to:
● Set clear priorities for co-planning (aligned vision and mindset)
● Protect collaboration time and the structures around it (infrastructure)
● Invest in ongoing coaching that builds teachers’ confidence and capacity to co-plan (staff
development)
● Provide materials and data teams need to plan (resource allocation)
By reinforcing a culture of shared responsibility, leaders ensure co-planning becomes a consistent,
scalable, and lasting part of a school’s work.

Enabling Conditions for Co-Planning
These enabling conditions ensure regular, protected collaboration occurs between team members,
anchoring the co-planning meeting in a broader ecosystem of collaboration. Additional collaboration
structures—namely quick, informal check-ins between meetings and longer planning sessions (e.g.,
quarterly unit planning days, data dives)—strengthen and extend the co-planning meeting throughout
the year. They do not replace it.

Aligned Vision and Mindset
● School leaders continuously communicate beliefs and expectations that all educators are
responsible for all students.
● School leaders share a clear expectation for co-planning, including an explicit definition of
roles and responsibilities.

Infrastructure
● The school-wide schedule contains
specific, dedicated time for regular
co-planning by teaching teams.
● Teams use an outcomes-aligned agenda
for co-planning with a common protocol for
student work and data analysis (see
Co-Planning Agenda Templates).
● High level lesson plans for the coming
week are shared before the meeting.
● Network or school data systems make
student data tracking transparent.

Staff Development
● Partnership launch meetings take place during summer training, focusing on relationship
building alongside establishing roles and norms.
● Teachers receive ongoing coaching to build their collaboration skills and mindsets.
● Differentiated support for new and returning co-planning teams (e.g., more frequent coaching
for new teams, leadership roles for strong, existing teams).

Resource Allocation
● Educators are provided high-quality instructional materials.
● Student progress data, learning profiles, and accommodations are used during planning.

Clarifying Roles and Responsibilities
Without clearly defined and shared roles, co-planning can quickly become unbalanced, inefficient, or
superficial. Establishing strong, evolving roles helps ensure every educator contributes meaningfully
and grows in their practice.
BARRIERS

SOLUTIONS

Unclear or misaligned
expectations between
co-planning partners

Use the Roles and Responsibilities checklist to clarify General
Educator and Special Educator contributions

Newer educators are
unsure of their role

Use team formation time and coaching to build understanding and
confidence

Roles are assumed
rather than defined

Co-create roles during the team formation and launch meetings, then
revisit them at set checkpoints (e.g., each quarter) as the partnership
grows. (Tool: Educator Logistics & Design Planning & Reflection)

Lack of shared
ownership or
collaboration

Establish norms, shared tools, and coaching arcs that support true
collaboration (Team Systems for Collaboration Toolkit)

Infrastructure
Protecting and Prioritizing Co-Planning Time
Even the best co-planning protocols won’t work without dedicated, protected time. Ensuring this time
is scheduled and preserved communicates that collaboration is core to instructional quality.
BARRIERS

SOLUTIONS

Co-planning time is not
scheduled or preserved

Dedicate co-planning time directly into the master schedule: at least
weekly for co-teaching teams and biweekly for other co-planning
teams, ideally 30 to 45 minutes per meeting. (Note: novice teams
typically need more time than experienced teams.)
Add calendar invites to the educator and coach calendar to keep the
time visible and sacred.

Time is repurposed for
IEP or department
meetings, or coverage

Build a campus culture where co-planning time is protected and
prioritized. Train all staff at the start of the year on its purpose and the
expectation

Meetings aren’t focused
on instructional planning

Establish accountability routines, such as leadership check-ins,
protocol observations, and collaborative reflections.

Inconsistent options for
planning blocks

Use the TIES Center’s 5-15-45 tool to design a mix of collaboration
structures (quick check-ins, regular meetings, and longer planning
sessions) that fit your schedule.

School planning blocks
are constrained by
network or district
calendars.

Work with district leadership to build protected collaboration time into
system calendars and master scheduling guidance.
If teams are attending district-wide training, have teams attend the
same training and tailor the materials to their co-planning needs.

Using Agendas and Models Effectively
A co-planning meeting is only as effective as the structure that guides it. Consistent use of agendas
and the right planning model allows teachers to make the most of limited time and ensure student
needs stay central.

BARRIERS

Co-planning agendas are
used inconsistently, or
not at all

The team is using the
same planning
structures, regardless of
need, time, and
experience

SOLUTIONS
Personalize the agenda template. Identify which agenda works best
for the team by exploring different Co-Planning Agenda
Templates—from basic to guided.
Rather than force a template, focus the team’s efforts on the four key
components of effective co-planning: alignment, reflection, removal,
and selection or planning of instructional next steps.

Use the How to Leverage Different Co-Planning Structures tool to
understand each co-planning structure’s purpose, flow, and function
to match your collaboration approach to the intended impact

Staff Development
Coaching That Makes Co-Planning Stick
Co-planning thrives with consistent, strategic coaching support. Coaches build team skill, provide
feedback, and model high-impact planning.

Key Moves for Coaches:
● Observe or facilitate co-planning meetings monthly using tools like:
○ Blue Engine’s Co-Planning Checklist
○ KIPP NorCal’s Co-Planning Observation Tool
● Provide planning support to general educators and content coaching to special educators
● Use exit tickets and student work as focal points during coaching cycles
● Help teams personalize their co-planning process to make use of longer planning sessions or
available professional development days (e.g., curriculum training, data dives)

Implementation Tips:
● Schedule 45-minute coaching blocks aligned with co-planning cycles
● Differentiate support based on teaching pair experience (e.g., new and returning co-planning
teams)
● Layer in skills over time through coaching, cross-team huddles, and quarterly professional
learning so teams get started with the practice and increase the quality of their co-planning
skills and routines as they grow.

Reinforce Roles and Responsibilities
Strong co-planning relationships continuously evolve so teaching teams can grow in their practice and
meaningfully contribute to their collective success for students. It is important to not just define roles and
responsibilities, but to check on their clarity between educators and reinforce them when they are
unbalanced or superficial. Refer to the established roles and responsibilities guidance to ensure
meaningful contributions and growth by co-planning educators.

Resource Allocation
Preparing and Using Data Strategically
Planning must be rooted in student needs. If teachers arrive without data or materials, co-planning
becomes generic and disconnected from learning.
BARRIERS

Teachers arrive at
co-planning meetings
unprepared

Data is not used to drive
instructional decisions

Planning focuses on
work completion, not
learning or mastery

SOLUTIONS
Assign role-specific pre-work and reinforce expectations about being
prepared (see pg. 3.15 in the co-planning chapter). Build a calendar
reminder for pre-work completion ahead of the planning meeting.
Have the team write out their pre-work for their next planning meeting
at the end of their current meeting. When will we meet next, and what
will we need to bring to that meeting to be ready to plan together?
Data drives effective instructional planning. Revisit A Collaborative
Approach to Student Work Analysis, specifically Green Dot’s Student
Work Analysis Protocol.
Diagnose if the barrier is analysis, goal setting, or decision- making
with the information gathered. Consider tools from the Routine Data
Cycles practice to guide coaching moves.
Focus on moving from work completion to content mastery (see the
Reflect prompts in the co-planning chapter).
Review the Using Exit Tickets to Drive Mastery tool.

Allocating Materials and Staff
Co-planning works best when teams have access to high-quality materials and consistent
partnerships. When leaders plan for both in advance, teams can focus on designing accessible
instruction instead of working around missing resources or stretched schedules.
BARRIERS

SOLUTIONS

Teams lack access to
high-quality instructional
materials

Ensure both educators have access to curriculum and unit plans
before meetings so planning time is used for adapting materials, not
creating them.
Focus co-planning on one or two teammates or subject areas per
special educator, rather than all subject areas.

Special educators are
spread across too many
co-planning partners

Have special education teachers attend a department meeting to
consult on removal of barriers and material adaptation by content
area to streamline time and resources.
Stagger co-planning meetings so each team meets in the same block
on alternating weeks (e.g., Team A on week 1, Team B on week 2) or
on specified week days if planning weekly.
