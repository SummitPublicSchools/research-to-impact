Literature Review
ABSTRACT
Multi-Tiered System of Support (MTSS) is a framework for schools and districts to address
barriers to learning for students. This literature review synthesizes the existing research
base on MTSS; namely what MTSS is, why it is important, the benefits and challenges
of the framework, an overview of common implementation strategies, and what is
currently known about the efficacy of MTSS. This review is designed for school district
administrators considering adopting or improving MTSS practices at their district.

Introduction
This literature review was written for an audience of educators, schools, and districts seeking to
improve outcomes for secondary students with a focus on Black and Latinx students with disabilities
living in poverty. In this literature review, we share a concise overview of MTSS, an adaptive organizing
framework for data-informed academic and behavioral instruction and support that meets student
needs in a timely manner. The purpose of this review is to equip district- and school-level staff who
are considering adopting or improving MTSS practices with a summary of the research on MTSS so
that staff can make informed, thoughtful implementation decisions for their districts. The literature
review was conducted by Marshall Street, a coalition of educators working to systematically improve
opportunities for students across the United States.
This literature review is part of a larger collection of improvement tools for districts. The Networked
Improvement Community for Students with Disabilities (NIC), a group of 10 public charter school
networks serving more than 75,000 students nationwide, launched in 2019 to make dramatic gains in
the learning experiences, environments, and outcomes for Black and Latinx students with disabilities
experiencing poverty. Led by Marshall Street at Summit Public Schools in partnership with technical
assistance providers NIRN, RTI International, SRI International, and SWIFT Education Center, NIC
participants used continuous improvement methods, supported by this literature review of leading

What is MTSS?
A Multi-Tiered System of Support is a framework for educators for organizing, aligning, and
implementing a system of academic, behavioral, social-emotional learning, and mental health support
for all students (California Department of Education, n.d.; Choi, McCart, and Sailor, “Achievement of
Students With IEPs,” 2020; Colorado Department of Education 2017; Drury et al. 2021; Horner and
Halle 2020; McCart and Miller 2020; McIntosh and Goodman 2016; Thurlow et al. 2020). MTSS not
only integrates the service delivery of two existing support models — response-to-intervention (RTI)
for academics and schoolwide positive behavioral intervention and support (PBIS) for behavior —
but it also incorporates social-emotional learning and mental health support service delivery in order
to increase the efficiency and efficacy of support (California Department of Education, n.d.; McCart
and Miller 2020; McIntosh and Goodman 2016). The hallmarks of MTSS include multiple tiers of
student support, high-quality instruction utilizing evidence-based practices, universal screening,
decision guidelines, progress monitoring, collaborative teacher teams, and the use data for continuous
improvement practices to improve implementation (McCart and Miller 2020; McIntosh and Goodman
2016; ESSA, Sec 8101(33) in Thurlow et al. 2020; McMaster and Wagner 2007 in Drury et al. 2021).
MTSS is a prevention-based framework for systematically addressing barriers to learning in pursuit of
improved academic and social outcomes for all learners (Averill and Rinaldi, n.d.; Drury et al. 2021;
Horner and Halle 2020; Leonard, Coyne, Oldham, Burns, and Gillis 2019).

The multiple tiers offer differentiated and increasingly intensive support for academic, behavioral, and
social learning (IRIS Center, n.d.). As McCart and Miller (2020, 4) state “MTSS is a comprehensive
set of academic, behavioral, and social supports that rely on data to mobilize and coordinate diverse
resources for students.”

Why is MTSS Important?
For decades, the public education system in the United States has struggled to effectively serve
students with a diverse set of needs, particularly students with disabilities. Inclusive classrooms — that
is, classrooms in which both students with and without disabilities are served together — are both an
educational requirement and a best practice for serving students with disabilities due to governmental
mandates such as No Child Left Behind (NCLB), the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA),
and the Every Student Succeeds Act (ESSA), as well as evidence-based research showing academic
gains from such classrooms (Alnasser 2020; Brendle, Lock, and Piazza 2017; Cole et al., n.d.; Cramer
et al. 2010; Weiss, Glaser, and Lloyd 2020). Yet, many schools struggle with the school-wide cultural
shift that is required to actualize inclusive education (Sailor 2015).
MTSS is a leading strategy for advancing the school-wide cultural shift to inclusion through efficiently
and equitably allocating resources to meet the needs of all students, including students with disabilities
and traditionally marginalized students. Schools across the United States have embraced MTSS due to
multiple factors:
• The potential for all students to receive timely support for their unique student needs,
including academic, behavioral, and social-emotional needs (Gage et al. 2015; Nelson et al.
2004 in Scott et al. 2019; McIntosh and Goodman 2016; McCart and Miller 2020; Sailor 2015),
particularly those students who typically are not served well by the existing education
system, including students with disabilities and students “who live in poverty, who
experience high mobility, who benefit from an accelerated curriculum, or who struggle to
learn for other reasons” (Choi, McCart, and Sailor, “Reshaping Educational Systems,” 2020, 8).
• The potential to increase the efficiency of teachers
by reducing the time it takes to regularly identify
student needs and prepare individualized support
for those needs (Lane, Oakes, and Menzies 2019
in Choi, McCart, and Sailor, “Reshaping Educational
Systems,” 2020; McIntosh and Goodman 2016; McCart
and Miller 2020; Sailor 2015).
• The ability to use federal and local funds to
implement MTSS activities via the Individuals with
Disabilities Act and the Every Student Succeeds Act
(ESSA, Sec 2103(b)(3)(F) in Thurlow et al. 2020; IDEA
2004 in Drury et al. 2021; Sugai and Simonsen 2012).
Today, all 50 states have adopted some form of MTSS
(Zhang et al. 2023).

Limitations of MTSS
No limitations to the MTSS framework were raised in the literature surveyed, however, multiple
challenges to implementation were mentioned. Those challenges include:
• initiative change fatigue on the part of educators (McIntosh and Goodman 2016);
• lack of MTSS knowledge and training for teachers, administrators, and district staff (Drury et al. 2021);
• difficulties actually using data to inform tiered support (Drury et al. 2021; Leonard et al. 2019);
• lack of access to or funding for tools such as progress monitoring tools and aligned curriculum based
on evidence-based practices (Mason et al. 2019; Minnesota Department of Education 2019);
• limited time for implementation of tiered support (Mason et al. 2019);
• difficulties serving students with the most significant cognitive disabilities (Thurlow et al. 2020); and
• lack of systems, structures, and policies (Drury et al. 2021; Leonard et al. 2019).
Because these challenges to implementation can forestall the adoption of MTSS in schools, the
Necessary Conditions for Implementation section of this literature review examines them more
thoroughly.

Common Implementation Frameworks
The MTSS model is based on a public health approach to intervention (Brown-Chidsey and Bickford 2016
in Drury et al. 2021) and combines three, or sometimes more, tiers of student services:
• Universal Support / Tier 1: high-quality instruction and support available to all students,
• Additional Support / Tier 2: different instruction and support for students who need more in addition
to Universal Support
• Intensified Support / Tier 3: instruction and support provided with variations in intensity (such as
greater frequency and duration) in addition to Universal and Additional Supports
(Averill and Rinaldi, n.d.; Batsche et al. 2005 in Averill and Rinaldi, n.d.; Drury et al. 2021; Horner and
Halle 2020; McIntosh and Goodman 2016; McCart and Miller 2020; Thurlow et al. 2020; National Center
on Response to Intervention 2010 in Thurlow et al. 2020).
To support the appropriate use of services within the tiers, most MTSS models incorporate the elements
of teaming structures, data for decision-making, evidence-based curriculum instruction and support
materials, and continuous improvement processes (McCart and Miller 2020, 10). There are many names
for the different elements used in the research including universal screening, progress monitoring, and
problem-solving models (a term based on RTI practices) (Batsche et al. 2005; Drury et al. 2021; Fuchs
and Fuchs 2006, Gresham 2007 in Averill and Rinaldi, n.d.; Horner and Halle 2020; Hughes and Dexter
2011 in Drury et al. 2021; Leonard et al. 2019; Mason et al. 2019; McIntosh and Goodman 2016).
A commonly used visual depiction of the tiers is a triangle, “representing levels of prevention (primary,
secondary, or tertiary), types of support for students (universal, targeted, or intensive), curricula (core,
strategic, individualized), or tiers of instruction (Tier 1, Tier 2, Tier 3)” (McIntosh and Goodman 2016, 13).
Each layer is additive, meaning that all students receive Universal Support / Tier 1 services, some students
will then receive Universal Support / Tier 1 and Additional Support / Tier 2 services, and few students

Common misperceptions of the tiers include the following:
• The perception that students need to move through all tiers in succession before receiving the
level of service that they need (Fuchs et al. 2003 in McIntosh and Goodman 2016). Thurlow et al.
(2020, 6) notes that no student should only receive Tier 3 support, as doing so would ignore the
other instruction and support in Tiers 1 and 2.
• The perception that the tiers are only applicable to some domains, such as early literacy and
behavior. In contrast, the tiers can be applied to nearly any topic in education (McIntosh and
Goodman 2016).
• The perception that the tiers are a way to label students. In contrast, the tiers are used to describe
supports that a student needs in the moment; the tiers are not a permanent descriptor of a student
(McCart and Miller 2020; McIntosh and Goodman 2016).
• The perception that MTSS is only for some students, not all students. In contrast, MTSS is
designed to provide services and resources to all students based on their needs (McCart and
Miller 2020).
• The perception that Tier 3 is the “special education” tier. In contrast, this tier is for any student who
needs intensive interventions in a particular domain (Thurlow et al. 2020).

Efficacy of MTSS and for Whom
When viewed in sum, the literature on the effect of MTSS on academic outcomes shows varied results.
Researchers typically attribute the variation in outcomes to the degree of fidelity of implementation
(Coyne et al. 2016; Fuchs, Fuchs, and Malone 2017 in Coyne et al. 2018). Generally, researchers have
found that MTSS, when implemented with fidelity, can have a positive impact on math performance
and mixed results on reading performance (Gersten et al. 2009; Montague and Jitendra 2012 in Choi,
McCart, and Sailor “Achievement of Students With IEPs,” 2020; Choi et al. 2016; Choi, McCart, and
Sailor, “Achievement of Students With IEPs,” 2020; Scott et al. 2019). One study hypothesized that
because the schools in their study had so many existing supports for reading, MTSS’s ability to impact
reading was limited as compared to math (Choi, McCart, and Sailor, “Achievement of Students With
IEPs,” 2020).
Recently, researchers have been able to more precisely measure the fidelity of MTSS implementation
to draw more definitive conclusions on the efficacy of MTSS on academic performance. Scott et al.
(2019) obtained fidelity measurements from 29 elementary, middle, and high schools in the United
States over four years. They found that schools with higher MTSS fidelity in the reading domain
were positively associated with more students at or above proficient in language mechanics and
mathematics, but not in reading. Conversely, higher MTSS fidelity in the math domain was associated
with more students at or above proficient in language mechanics, but not mathematics nor reading.
Additionally, higher MTSS implementation fidelity in the behavioral domain resulted in significantly fewer
suspension events, as compared to matched comparison schools (Scott et al. 2019, 308). As relates
to the Networked Improvement Community’s target population, schools with higher MTSS fidelity in
this study had student demographics of 14% Black, 7% Latinx, and 63% free/reduced lunch status,
though the study did not report on specific outcomes by sub-groups. The study also did not report the
percentage of students with disabilities in the sample (310).

Choi, McCart, and Sailor (“Achievement of Students With IEPs,” 2020) were also able to more precisely
measure MTSS implementation in order to draw conclusions on academic performance. Their study
examined impacts on academic performance specifically for students with disabilities (as measured
by students with Individualized Education Plans (IEPs)) at 14 schools in the same large, urban
school district serving students in Pre-K–5th grade and Pre-K–8th grade (3). Similar to Scott et al.
(2019), this study found that students with disabilities in high-fidelity implementation schools showed
increased math state assessment scores, as compared to comparison schools; however, reading
score growth was equivalent to comparison schools (1, 6). The study did not report on race/ethnicity or
socioeconomic demographics, nor on combined race/ethnicity, socioeconomic, and disability status.
Importantly, researchers in both of these studies noted the intensive support the sample schools
received, including coaching and targeted technical assistance, in order to achieve high fidelity of
implementation. Coyne et al. (2018, 15) observed that “there are many difficulties and challenges
associated with implementing MTSS in schools under real-world conditions (Arden et al. 2017; Coyne
et al. 2016)” and though sample schools may demonstrate consistent implementation as recommended
by research, “that level of implementation may not mirror current practice in the field (Balu et al. 2015),”
which can result in uneven academic performance.

Necessary Conditions for
Implementation
As MTSS is a schoolwide system of support and services for students, much of the research suggested
addressing the implementation challenges and preparing for successful adoption by focusing on
system-wide levers. As a brief review, the implementation challenges raised in the literature include the
following:
• initiative change fatigue on the part of educators leading to a loss of enthusiasm and willingness to
try new educational approaches (McIntosh and Goodman 2016);
• lack of MTSS knowledge and training for teachers, administrators, and district staff, particularly
around data-driven problem-solving (Drury et al. 2021);
• lack of systems, structures, and policies that support MTSS implementation and remove barriers to
implementation (Drury et al. 2021, Leonard et al. 2019);
• difficulties actually using the data that is collected to inform tiered support in a dynamic fashion
(Drury et al. 2021, Leonard et al. 2019);
• lack of access to/funding for tools such as progress monitoring tools, and aligned curriculum
and intervention programs based on evidence-based practices (Mason et al. 2019; Minnesota
Department of Education 2019);
• limited time for implementation of tiered support, particularly Additional Support / Tier 2 and
Intensified Support / Tier 3 (Mason et al. 2019); and
• difficulties serving students with the most significant cognitive disabilities (Thurlow et al. 2020).
Networks in the NIC that focused on improving
MTSS implementation found that they needed to
first find ways to create accessible data systems
that would allow educators to understand and
respond to the needs of all learners. Once that
infrastructure was built, they could focus on
training network and school leaders as well as
educators in the new data tools.
The SWIFT Education Center (2024) identifies
the system-wide levers as the MTSS framework,
administrative leadership, integrated educational
framework, family and community engagement,
and inclusive policy structure and practice. A
synthesized list of system-wide levers found in
the literature are listed on the following page as

EXPLANATION

Build
administrative
leadership and
support

The literature noted that administrators need to build consensus to commit to
implementing MTSS (Batsche et al. 2005 and Castillo et al. 2010 in Averill and
Rinaldi, n.d.); build a shared vision and supportive data-driven problemsolving culture school-wide (Batsche et al. 2005 and Castillo, et al. 2010 in
Averill and Rinaldi, n.d.; Colorado Department of Education 2017); cultivate
their instructional leadership skills in MTSS (Mellard, Prewett, and Deshler
2012 in Drury et al. 2021); foster inclusive decision-making (SWIFT Education
Center 2024); and deliver targeted professional development for staff (SWIFT
Education Center 2024).

Build a schoolwide MTSS
infrastructure

In collaboration with their districts, schools need to build a school-wide MTSS
infrastructure (Batsche et al. 2005 and Castillo, et al. 2010 in Averill and Rinaldi,
n.d.) that includes data collection methods (McIntosh and Goodman 2016); a
variety of collaborative teaming structures across systems (general education,
special education, administration, and school- and district-level support staff)
(Averill and Rinaldi, n.d.; McIntosh and Goodman 2016) to utilize the data for
problem-solving (Gersten et al. 2008; Sugai and Horner 2009 in McIntosh and
Goodman 2016); aligning and organizing the tiered support, including those
for special education services (SWIFT Education Center 2024; Thurlow et al.
2020); incorporating aligned curriculum and intervention programs based
on Universal Design for Learning and other evidence-based practices (SWIFT
Education Center 2024); and building knowledge on continuous improvement
cycles (SWIFT Education Center 2021).

Integrate the
school’s resources
and organizational
structure

Integrating resources and organizational structures includes actions such
as acquiring assessment and intervention programs (Mason et al. 2019;
Minnesota Department of Education 2019), redefining roles for paraeducators
and specialists (SWIFT Education Center 2024); hiring and training MTSS
coaches (Coyne et al. 2018); ensuring every student has a primary general
education classroom placement (SWIFT Education Center 2024); scheduling
appropriate time in the school week for data analysis and problem-solving,
planning, and implementing tiered support (Mason et al. 2019); ensuring the use
of language and practices that are based on service needs as opposed to
disability labels (SWIFT Education Center 2024) and creating a positive school
culture where all students have access to extracurricular activities and all staff are
contributing to student outcomes (SWIFT Education Center 2024).

Include families
and community
members as
partners

MTSS is a whole school transformation that requires the partnership of families and
community members. As part of the MTSS adoption process, schools and districts
should engage authentically with families for input and feedback (SWIFT
Education Center 2024), provide opportunities for families and community
members to participate in school and the education of the students (Fan and
Chen 2001 and Jeynes 2012 in Gross, Choi, and Francis 2018; SWIFT Education
Center 2024) and provide families with information needed to support their
students (Fan and Chen 2001 and Jeynes 2012 in Gross, Choi, and Francis 2018;
SWIFT Education Center 2024).

Evaluate and
adapt district
policies and
practices for MTSS

A variety of district changes are needed to support MTSS adoption and
implementation. Those changes include recalibrating district office roles
to cross-functionally support MTSS implementation (Averill and Rinaldi, n.d.),
building knowledge of district staff (including the superintendent) in MTSS
(Drury et al. 2021; SWIFT Education Center 2024), incorporating opportunities for
district staff to attend school-level meetings (SWIFT Education Center 2024),
building a regular process for reviewing and revising policies that impact
MTSS implementation (SWIFT Education Center 2024), and removing barriers to
implementing MTSS (SWIFT Education Center 2024).

Schools and districts can use tools such as the Schoolwide Integrated Framework for Transformation
Fidelity of Implementation Tool (Pollitt et al. 2019) or the Academic and Behavior Response to
Intervention School Assessment (ASA) fidelity tool (Scott et al. 2019) to track their MTSS adoption
progress.
Further, the literature noted that the order in which schools and districts implement the above
system changes matters for successful MTSS adoption. A study by Choi et al. (2018, 10) found that
administrative leadership played a critical role in school transformation and that “meaningful schoolwide change might not occur without the mediation of high-quality school leadership.” This suggests
that schools and districts in early stages of adoption focus on first building administrative leadership in
order to propel the other system changes.

Conclusion
In conclusion, MTSS is truly a school-wide approach to meeting students’ needs. When implemented
with fidelity, MTSS can be an effective approach to meeting all students’ needs, not just students with
disabilities. For successful implementation, schools and districts must understand they are committing
to a whole-school transformation and subsequently invest significant time to continuously align systemwide resources, professional development, policies, structures, and systems.
Schools implementing MTSS with high fidelity have seen strong gains in math and limited gains in
reading for all students, in addition to reduced suspensions. For staff, high-fidelity implementation
provides organizational clarity on educational practices and policies; accessible data systems;
increased whole-school collaboration; and more sustainable, efficient, and effective implementation
of student services. In short, MTSS is a framework for meeting student needs that, if implemented
with high fidelity, has the potential to improve academic and behavioral outcomes for Black and Latinx
students with disabilities experiencing poverty.
Best practices for implementing MTSS are discussed in Navigating Data for Multi-Tiered Systems of
Support: Equity-based practices for using data at the district, school, and classroom levels.
