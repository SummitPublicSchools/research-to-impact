Practice Overview
What is the Practice?
A summer program that provides students with targeted, 1:1 transition coaching to support them in
completing the final steps needed to meet their transition goals when they have not met them at the
time of graduation.

Why is Summer Transition Support Important?
Students with disabilities do not just need to graduate from high school. They also need to successfully
complete their transition plans so that they can follow their chosen path after graduation. Recent
government statistics showed that within the first eight years after graduation from high school, people
with disabilities made a dollar less per hour ($10.40 vs. $11.40), were less likely to both enroll in (60%
vs. 67%) and complete (41% vs. 52%) postsecondary education programs, and were less likely to live
independently (45% vs. 59%) than people without disabilities.1 This practice addresses the need to
support students, even after graduation, to finish essential steps for postsecondary success in college,
careers, and the community. Without this focus on transition plan completion, schools will struggle
to close the gap in labor force participation for individuals with disabilities, which is only about 38%,
compared to 77% for the general population. These outcome differences have tremendous effects on
individual earnings, as well as independence in society.2

Why it Works
STUDENTS SEEKING ALTERNATIVE PATHWAYS NEED CONTINUED SUPPORT.
Continuing targeted transition planning and programming support beyond graduation — particularly
for students with disabilities who are Black, Latinx, and experiencing poverty — ensures that students
who are not choosing 4-year college paths have the same support as their peers. Students choosing
an alternative non-college pathway are often left out of summer interventions that focus on ensuring
students have completed the necessary steps to start college. Students interested in advancing directly
into careers also need support in the summer to complete the steps necessary to start their post-high
school lives.
SIMILAR MODELS OF SUPPORT SHOW PROMISING RESULTS.
Research on postsecondary coaching programs that were location-specific to support students
attending 2- and 4-year colleges found that they have moderate positive effects on college enrollment
and persistence.3 Using this same strategy to support students on career, military, or community living
pathways could similarly help ensure that all students are successful after their high school experience.

Optional Step: “Summer Boot Camp”
During the 2020–21 school year, a time when students were transitioning between virtual and in-person learning,
Gratz launched this practice with a different set of steps. The original summer programming included a one-week
intensive “boot camp” followed by 1:1 coaching sessions. In this model, Mastery’s team frontloaded one week of
in-person days for students to complete orientation to the program, receive an overview of the transition pathway
options available, complete interest surveys or transition planning assessment tools if needed, and confirm goals
and priorities for their summer transition coaching within their existing College and Career Coaches (C3) model.
This approach allowed the team to form or strengthen relationships with students who they may not have worked
with closely due to the interruptions from COVID, and to establish expectations between students and their
coaches for goals, priorities, and routines to successfully complete the program.
The Gratz team has integrated much of the initial orientation and intensive-week content into activities with
students in the spring before they graduate from high school. By establishing strong data routines to identify
students in need of the C3 program — particularly those not on track to enroll in and begin their non-college
pathway — the Gratz team is able to complete training and assessment activities with students while they are still
enrolled in their high school classes. This early identification and engagement has resulted in the discontinuation
of the boot camp intensive-week model at the start of summer.
For schools that do not have the opportunity to complete activities with students in advance of the summer
program, an intensive week of summer transition programming may be a useful addition to this practice.

Bringing this Practice to Life
ENSURING ALL STUDENTS START SUCCESSFUL LIVES
AFTER HIGH SCHOOL
The COVID-19 pandemic threw schools across the country into chaos, and many of their students were
cast adrift. The postsecondary team at Mastery Schools’ Simon Gratz High School could not sit by
when, in the spring of 2021, they realized that few of their students were ready for life after high school.
Their quick planning that first year to create a summer boot camp for their seniors eventually spread
into a network-wide summer program to ensure that all students were ready to start the pathway of their
choice when they left high school.

Getting Students Back on Track in a Hurry
In March of 2021, students at Mastery Schools returned to in-person learning for the first time
since March of 2020 due to the COVID-19 pandemic. The postsecondary team at Gratz realized
immediately that their seniors were off track in completing postsecondary activities, such as
financial aid, college applications, resumes, job applications, and cover letters. By May, the Gratz
postsecondary team identified the need to implement more intensive interventions to ensure their
senior class — particularly the students with disabilities — reached their postsecondary goals.
The Gratz postsecondary team launched a three-day “boot camp” to provide an intensive learning
opportunity for students. All seniors who were off track with postsecondary tasks, including
students with disabilities, attended the boot camp on campus to try to quickly complete as many
postsecondary tasks as possible. With the help of three of the postsecondary team members,
students worked on resumes, searched for jobs, completed enrollment documents for colleges,
completed job applications, and practiced for interviews.

Creating Summer Structures for the Success of Students with Disabilities
The boot camp worked well for some, but not all, students. For the students who needed
additional support, the Gratz postsecondary and special education teams tried an intervention of
individualized coaching. The model for this intervention came from Mastery’s College and Career
Coaches (C3) program — an existing summer intervention program providing individualized
coaching, which was previously available only for Mastery students planning to enroll in 2- or 4-year
colleges. C3 attempts to increase matriculation to these postsecondary pathways and to reduce
“summer melt.”
The Gratz teams adapted the C3 program for the students with disabilities who were pursuing
non-college pathways. Over the course of nearly five weeks of summer, after the boot camp,
three Gratz postsecondary team members worked as coaches to support about 60 students with
disabilities in-person and remotely by phone, text, and email. Individualized coaching included
breaking down steps to complete transition goals, setting up regular deadlines with students to
meet transition checkpoints, and following up with students to ensure they met those deadlines.
The effort was a great success. By the end of summer 2021, 87% of Gratz students had
engaged in summer touchpoints with their coach, and 80% of students had enrolled in their
postsecondary pathway.

WHAT IS SUMMER MELT?
Summer melt is a term used to describe students who were accepted into college and intended to enroll
but did not complete the steps necessary to actually attend college in the fall. A host of interventions
are used to mitigate this issue and support students through the summer to complete the necessary steps for
enrollment. Groups working to reduce summer melt, such as nonprofit organizations, colleges, and high schools,
use tools like text messaging, coaching, mentoring, and summer bridge programs to make sure students enroll in
college each fall.

Expanding to Support All Students
Using the learnings and success of the first summer, the Gratz team enacted and formalized two
structures:
1. They strategically built the content from the boot camp into the existing 12th grade seminar to
ensure learning and progress monitoring were in place throughout the school year.
2. The C3 program adaptations were formalized and spread to other schools in the Mastery
network. By summer 2022, all Mastery students who were interested in non-college pathways —
regardless of whether they were in special education or not — could engage in C3 coaching.
During the 2022–23 school year, Gratz increased their team to include eight coaches — including
a social worker to support students and families with needs such as food and housing insecurity —
who worked with 184 students. The coaches spent about three days per week doing direct outreach

Lessons Learned for the Future
Within Mastery’s expanded coaching staff, not everyone had the original three coaches’ depth
of knowledge about the different pathways, requirements, and paperwork necessary to support
students easily. The addition of coaches taught the postsecondary team that they needed to
increase coaches’ training in the summer of 2023, leading them to provide additional training for
coaches prior to the summer of 2024.
The success of Gratz’s C3 program for students in non-college pathways is a result of their
adaptation of an existing model and the creation of an inclusive program that focused on helping
all students achieve the goals they set for themselves. In addition, Mastery’s willingness to use
extended school year funding and other financial resources for this program demonstrates the
network’s commitment to ensuring postsecondary success for every student regardless of pathway.

How to Implement this Practice
This section provides step-by-step guidance for implementation of summer transition supports specific
to a school and community context, along with accompanying resources to get started.

Before the End of the School Year
Step 1: Identify Students to Participate in the Program
Determine which students need or will likely need additional summer transition support to complete
their transition goals.
Potential factors could include:
• students with disabilities who are not in a self-contained program (as such programs often provide
their own transition support),
• students with a non-college postsecondary goal,
• students interested in transitioning into the workforce who are not currently working or who have
unstable employment, or
• students with low attendance.
The use of a postsecondary data tracking system during the school year can help inform which
students will be a good fit for summer transition support.

Step 2: Create Caseloads for Coaches
Build coaches’ caseloads by grouping students by pathway (e.g., workforce development, employment,
community living, or military) and then, if possible, by existing relationships between students and
coaches. Ideally, each coach can support 10–25 students on their caseload, with the exact number
depending on the number of milestones their students still have to complete, the level of support the
students need to be successful, and the type of pathway. For example, a workforce pathway can be
particularly time-consuming because the coach has to work with a student to find open positions, to
complete a number of applications, and to prepare for multiple interviews before the student is hired for
a position. Other pathways, such as the military, have a defined process that requires one application,
screening, and interview before the student is accepted. All of these factors should be considered when
creating a caseload for a coach.
It is especially helpful to both coach and student when the coach already knows the details of the
pathways assigned to them, such as the required paperwork, the primary points of contact at related
organizations or institutions, and the steps necessary for the student to enroll successfully. If possible,
coaches should have the opportunity to specialize in specific pathways and to become experts in
what students need to do to be successful when enrolling in those pathways. There can be nuance

for coaches to become experts in a particular pathway, schools improve the effectiveness of their
coaches, who can better help students to meet their goals during the summer.

activities throughout the summer.

Execute the Program
Step 7: Review Summer Plans with Students
At the first meeting of the summer, coaches review the final, individualized summer plans with the
students on their caseload. The plans should include:
• dates for when milestones should be completed,
• a specific schedule of 1:1 check-ins,
• dates and details of planned exposure experiences, and
• the schedule of small group meetings with other students working on the same pathway.
The review includes discussion of remaining questions, confirmation that students have the necessary
materials for the plan, and problem-solving for adjustments that may need to be made to the plan in order
to get started. With their plans in place, students can begin working on the steps indicated in their plans.

Step 8: Follow Up with Students Weekly
Using a meeting agenda, coaches conduct 1:1 check-ins with students on their caseload at least
weekly. Structured meetings support students’ progress towards specific milestones and completion of
key activities related to those milestones. These meetings are a regular opportunity to hold students
accountable for tasks they said they would do over the past week, reflect on the progress they have
made on their plan, adapt the plan if necessary, and set new tasks and deadlines.
Students who execute their plan independently may only need quick check-ins via text, while students
who struggle to complete the steps of their plan will likely need regular in-person support. In every
instance, it is important that coaches document successes, setbacks, and actions taken with students
so that the coach can keep the rest of the team informed.

As students complete milestones, coaches track their progress in a data tool. If the students are
off track according to the planned dates, coaches can determine whether students need additional
support, such as more time for specific milestones or more frequent check-ins, to achieve the targets.
Though sometimes the team will be able to predict before summer where a student will flourish and
succeed independently based on past performance or interactions, it may not always be possible to
anticipate which of the student’s tasks will be easier or harder for them to complete. For this reason,
coaching schedules should have flexibility so that the plan and schedules can be adapted at various
points throughout the summer in response to each student’s progress.
If a coach cannot get in touch with a student weekly, they can try to leverage other staff members who
have existing relationships with the student to help facilitate contact. It is best to try other methods
for contacting the student before reaching out to the family using the contact information provided at
orientation.

Step 9: Meet as a Team to Review Data and Troubleshoot
All coaches and members of the postsecondary and special education teams should meet regularly
throughout the summer to review data on student progress along pathways. An optimal cadence is
weekly or biweekly so that the team can help coaches problem-solve issues that are occurring for
individuals or groups of students in a timely manner (see Building School-Based Transition Teams
to Accelerate Student Progress Toward Postsecondary Goals). These meetings are also an
opportunity to adapt student plans or determine whether other staff members can be helpful points of
contact for students, families, or outside agencies.
The team can consider posing the following questions for discussion:
• What common barriers are students experiencing?
• What types of support seem to be most beneficial to students (e.g., text check-ins, small group
meetings, field trips)?
• How can the team meet the needs of students who are experiencing the greatest challenges to
make progress?

End of the Summer
Step 10: Debrief Summer with Team and Plan for Next
School Year
Set aside time at the end of summer to discuss the program’s implementation and effectiveness.
See How to Measure in the Appendix for different ways to measure program implementation and
effectiveness. It is important to use the documentation and learnings from coaches, students,
and regular summer team meetings to make needed changes for the coming school year and the
following summer.

Summary
Individuals with disabilities are half as likely to participate in the workforce as individuals
without disabilities. They need additional support to ensure that they have the skills and
resources needed to be successful once they leave high school. Schools can and should
provide support to all students the summer after graduation to ensure students achieve their
postsecondary goals. This support can come in many forms, such as 1:1 coaching, text
message reminders, or field trips. Students with disabilities may need more targeted support
from 1:1 coaching in order to complete the steps necessary to start their lives successfully
after high school. Gratz used their C3 program to ensure that all of their students with
disabilities were enrolled in a postsecondary program, in the military, or working. Like Gratz,
schools can increase the likelihood that students will participate in the workforce regardless
of their disability status by creating similar programs to support the critical transition to life
after high school.
