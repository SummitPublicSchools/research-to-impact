Snapshot
WHAT IS THE PRACTICE?
An interdisciplinary, cross-functional team that meets regularly with a clear purpose,
defined membership, and established routine to review data in order to identify strengths
and needs and problem-solve to take timely action to intervene for all students.

WHY IS IT IMPORTANT?
• School systems and teams are often designed to operate in silos, which limits effective
collaboration and ultimately hinders positive impacts on students. This is true at the
school level and also at the network level for many school systems across the country.
• Intentional structures are needed to break down silos and create opportunities to
analyze data across teams for the purpose of allocating resources and support where
needed.
• Network teams require a clear purpose, process, and next steps that align with existing
ways of working and organizational goals.
• This type of collaboration can reinforce a sense of shared responsibility for all students.
Students can be more fully supported in general education classrooms and achieve
positive outcomes when teams bring together diverse knowledge, expertise, and
creativity.

The Challenge
How can network teams use data to respond
effectively to school needs?
At Green Dot Public Schools, the Ed Management team has, for many years, existed as a group
working to improve network schools. Led by Green Dot’s chief programs officer, Leilani Abulon, and chief
academic officer, Ed Management brings together the area superintendents who manage and support
principals, along with all the program directors, who work under Leilani and lead specific network-level
departments such as math and science or special education. The departments and area superintendents
have long collaborated on an as-needed basis, but previously worked primarily within their own teams.
Through their participation in the Networked Improvement Community (NIC), Green Dot’s network
leaders saw how multi-tiered systems of support (MTSS) could be applied much more broadly than to
just classroom instruction. In particular, Leilani realized that MTSS could also be used as a framework for
network and school-level support, school leader support, and teacher support across the network. The
Ed Management team needed to adapt its team structures to make this possible.

The Practice
The Ed Management team
reviews network-level data to
take action for schools.
In collaboration with SWIFT Education Center and the
NIC’s MTSS professional learning community, Leilani
created an initial structured protocol (approximately
one hour long) so that network-level leaders on the Ed
Management team could have a routine:
• to analyze school-level data,
• to maintain a bird’s-eye view of the network to
understand high-level data trends, and
• to coordinate support for schools and the network.

Importantly, after use of the protocol during the 2022–23 school year, the team opted for a more
streamlined and shorter version of their data-for-equity protocol to analyze data. This type of protocol
requires participants to look specifically at gaps between subgroups and consider the larger context in
which data is collected. Team members are pushed to look at systemic inequities that may exist in their
network or schools and to devise concrete actions that can be taken to fix them.

How Green Dot Implemented the Practice
Over time, the Green Dot team developed a process to implement the data-to-action protocol, which they
iterate upon each year as new members join the team, priorities evolve, and schedules change. Green
Dot’s Ed Management team follows clear steps to ensure they are addressing needs across the network:

Step 1: Define the team’s purpose.
Step 2: Determine the team members.
• Make sure team members understand the purpose of the meeting.
• Define roles and responsibilities for team members.
Step 3: Create a meeting schedule.
• Reduce all possible conflicts.
• Reinforce the importance of the meeting by protecting the time on calendars.
Step 4: Create the protocol.
• Draft a team data protocol aligned to the purpose and vision of the teaming structure and routine.
– The protocol should allow for data analysis, identifying trends, setting a goal, creating an action plan,
and gaining consensus from the team.
– The protocol should align all team members on the goal of each meeting so that if teams are broken up
to focus on different data, topics, or even students, each group’s action plan meets the common goal.
• Gain consensus on the data protocol and questions used in the protocol.
Step 5: Identify the data to analyze.
• Determine what data is most applicable to the team’s purpose and goals.
• Data must be aligned with school and network priorities in order for it to be as useful as possible.
• Misaligned data will, over time, erode buy-in of team members.
Step 6: Train team members on data analysis tools and methods.
• Provide a specific step-by-step process and training on data tools that will be used by the team.
• Give examples of how to interpret the types of data that the team will look at regularly.
• Ensure understanding of how to analyze disaggregated data by subgroups based on the purpose and
goals of the team.
Step 7: Create a bank of potential action steps that can be taken.
• This can be built by the team over time and/or prepopulated with interventions.
• Action steps should be feasible, within the team’s locus of control, and timely.
Step 8: Hold data meetings.

Green Dot’s Ed Management team found success in meeting regularly and learning how to use the
protocol they had devised. Over time, the team learned important lessons about how to make these
meetings more impactful:
• They needed action plans that they could drive to solve problems for schools.
• Their solutions should not create more work for their school teams.
• They had to be very targeted in the data they looked at and keep it consistent over time. This led to
the eventual reduction in the amount of data analyzed.
• The data they looked at had to be similar to the data that individual schools were focused on
improving.
• They needed time twice a year to step back and reflect on how effectively they were working together.

The Impact
Use of the network-level data-to-action protocol
has built skills and capacity.
After about a year and a half of implementing and iterating upon their meetings, one of the biggest
improvements that the Ed Management team made was including data questions and a common data
routine during every meeting. Even if the team’s focus shifted to different data, their data analysis
skills and routines were still relevant. The team leaders started using the data-for-equity protocol in
department-level meetings to help explore more department-specific equity gaps in the data.
The team continues to meet once a month and now focuses on staff capacity data and how it relates
to professional development. The team is doing a book study and analyzing professional development
exit slip data to answer specific data questions. Because the Ed Management team is responsible
for much of the network-wide professional development, the team no longer needs to worry about
who is responsible for implementing interventions and can respond quickly to data. Green Dot plans
to triangulate student outcomes data and observational data in order to drive the focus of future
professional development.

Green Dot Public Schools California serves approximately 10,000 students in grades 6–12 across Los Angeles. Focal
schools for the practice included Ánimo Watts College Preparatory Academy and Ánimo Mae Jemison Charter Middle School
in Los Angeles, CA.
Green Dot Public Schools is a part of the Marshall Street Networked Improvement Community, a three-year intentional
community of 10 public charter school networks nationwide aiming to make dramatic gains for Black and Latinx students with
disabilities experiencing poverty. Collectively, the 10 networks identified over 20 best practices that improve learning outcomes
and school culture for students positioned furthest from opportunity. Learn more here.
