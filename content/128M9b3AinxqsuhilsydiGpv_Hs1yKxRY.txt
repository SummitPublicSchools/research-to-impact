Preface
In Igniting Postsecondary Aspirations: Proven school-based practices to empower students to transition
into meaningful college, career, and community postsecondary pathways, we share a set of best
practices for improving learning and postsecondary outcomes in secondary students — particularly Black
and Latinx students with disabilities experiencing poverty — using transition programming that empowers
students to seek individualized postsecondary pathways. The Networked Improvement Community for
Students with Disabilities (NIC), a group of 10 public charter school networks serving more than 75,000
students nationwide, identified the best practices through their work between 2019 and 2023.
Led by Marshall Street at Summit Public Schools, in partnership with technical assistance providers
NIRN, RTI International, SRI International, and SWIFT Education Center, the purpose of the three-year
NIC was to systematically improve the way our education system serves Black and Latinx students with
disabilities experiencing poverty and share these solutions with school systems everywhere. This report is
funded by the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation. The findings and conclusions contained within are those
of the authors and do not necessarily reflect positions or policies of the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation.
The NIC used continuous improvement to translate existing research for improving learning and
postsecondary outcomes into best practices and to iterate the practices to ensure they worked in a variety
of school contexts.
In Igniting Postsecondary Aspirations: Proven school-based practices
to empower students to transition into meaningful college, career, and
community postsecondary pathways, we share the results of this vital work:
A literature review, summarizing the key research related to providing transition
planning and services
Three best practices that schools used while in the NIC as part of their transition
planning and services to successfully improve postsecondary outcomes for students
with disabilities. Each best practice includes:

Case studies highlighting how schools in the NIC implemented the practices
Step-by-step implementation guidance
Key prerequisites and accelerants for successful implementation in any context

Two case studies that showcase how schools supported students and families in
connecting with internal alumni success coaches and external organizations to ensure
students received resources after high school

We share these best practices so that any school in the country can leverage the learning from the NIC
to accelerate dramatic gains in learning and postsecondary outcomes for their students with disabilities.
We hope that administrators and educators across the country can use the stories within this collection
to build their school cultures of continuous improvement as well.

About Marshall Street
Marshall Street is a division of Summit Public Schools based in Redwood City, California. Marshall
draws from more than 15 years of institutional knowledge and extensive experience in school
improvement. Through our programs, we increase the capacity of our school partners to carry their
work forward in the ways most meaningful to their communities.
Continuous Improvement is a set of principles and practices to help educators “get better at getting
better.” Marshall’s Continuous Improvement team uses these tools to tackle intricate, systems-level
problems in K–12 education. From 2019 to 2023, Marshall led a multi-year Networked Improvement
Community (NIC) to make dramatic gains in the learning experiences, environments, and outcomes for
Black and Latinx students with disabilities experiencing poverty.

Introduction
The Challenge
Postsecondary outcomes for students with disabilities lag behind those of
their peers.
Public schools across America struggle to meet the diverse needs of students, particularly those
furthest from opportunity. When marginalization intersects with disability, students often face low
expectations, are systematically segregated, are denied access to college and career curricula, and
do not receive sufficient support to obtain a high school diploma and pursue their postsecondary
goals.1 Ensuring that students have the necessary skills to successfully transition into adulthood after
high school — whether to 2- or 4-year college, workforce development, employment, the military, or
community living — is an essential goal for schools. Unfortunately, people with disabilities between the
ages of 14 and 64 are considerably less likely than people without disabilities to be employed, to both
enroll in and complete postsecondary education programs, and to live independently.2 In 2023, only
37.1% of people with disabilities were employed; the same year, of people without disabilities,
75.0% were employed.3 Despite requirements in the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA)
to center Individualized Education Programs (IEPs) on postsecondary education, employment, and
community living goals for students 16 years and older,4 schools continue to struggle to make sure
students are achieving those goals.

Competing priorities reduce opportunities for students with disabilities to
learn necessary skills.
While college enrollment after graduation continues to be an important focus for high schools, the
explicit teaching of postsecondary skills is also necessary for ensuring students with disabilities
experience success in college, a career, the military, or in their community once they leave high school.
The focus of many high schools on college enrollment after graduation for all students has made it

difficult for special education programs to teach postsecondary skills. As a result, there is limited time
during the school day for special educators to teach postsecondary employment and community living
skills when schools focus solely on college preparation.5 Students receive college readiness activities
through college preparation programs (e.g., seminars, advisory, or college counseling), but additional
time is necessary to support students with the development of employment and community living skills.6
When school goals don’t align with the goals of students and families, it is difficult to provide students
with disabilities opportunities to gain the skills necessary for successful postsecondary transitions.

Schools do not invest in the staffing necessary to create diverse
postsecondary opportunities for students.
Even when schools are interested in providing a diverse range of opportunities for students to reach
their postsecondary goals, they often lack the necessary infrastructure and relationships.7 Schools
may want to make more opportunities available for students with disabilities to learn postsecondary
skills, particularly through work-based learning opportunities, but they do not invest in staff who can
develop relationships with external organizations, businesses, and programs. There must be staff with
dedicated time for building connections with programs8 in a variety of industries so that students can
build skills regardless of the type of goal they have. In addition, schools need to ensure that students
with moderate to severe disabilities are sufficiently supported when engaging in activities outside the
school building in the same way they would be supported in school.9 Currently, not enough schools are
investing in their goal to provide students with diverse postsecondary transition learning opportunities
so that students with disabilities can experience the same successful outcomes as their peers.10

Our Approach
Experiential and work-based learning opportunities are necessary for students with disabilities to learn
the skills required for successful postsecondary transitions. Existing research demonstrates that
when students with disabilities engage in career and technical education programs, they are
more likely to graduate from high school, earn an industry certification,11 and be employed12
after graduation. By creating postsecondary pathways aligned with student goals and giving students
real-world opportunities in those pathways, students can be better prepared for life after high school.
The implementation of effective postsecondary programming and support is essential to the NIC’s
aim of systematically improving the way our education system serves Black and Latinx students
with disabilities experiencing poverty. To close the research-to-practice gap, the NIC explored and
developed a set of accessible and effective interventions to accelerate choice-filled postsecondary
pathways for students. At the foundation of these interventions are the following guiding principles.

Working with external organizations ensures students can pursue the
pathway of their choice.
Postsecondary transition programming needs to be designed so that, regardless of disability, students
can choose their postsecondary goals and have a variety of ways to work toward those goals. Every
student should be able to pursue their pathway of interest. This is particularly important for students
with moderate to severe disabilities, who have long been denied self-determination of their future.13

Partnering with community-based organizations is a necessity for providing students with a variety
of postsecondary readiness opportunities. Schools have to invest in hiring staff who can maintain
relationships between the school and community-based organizations; conduct ongoing research into
programs and potential partners, including making contact, acquiring information, and visiting locations;
develop and maintain a collaborative process for the program; and coordinate information-sharing
across various stakeholder groups (e.g., students, families, postsecondary teams, and IEP teams).
Engaging in inter-agency collaboration (e.g., collaboration between schools and the state department of
rehabilitation), as Uncommon Schools did while in the NIC, has proven to result in improved outcomes
for students with disabilities, including increases in both postsecondary employment and enrollment in
postsecondary education programs.14 Services — such as pre-employment training, job placements,
and workplace accommodations — are often essential for students with moderate to severe disabilities
once they complete high school and lose services they have relied on for years.
Mastery Schools, while engaged in the NIC, was able to develop new partnerships with external
organizations and thus increase the number of pathways available to their students with and without
disabilities. For schools to provide a range of opportunities and support in all students’ chosen
pathways — as Mastery and Uncommon did — they need to be dedicated to developing relationships
with external partners and organizations.

Engaging key stakeholders ensures students have a team supporting
their transition.
Engaging key stakeholders, including those who can continue to support students after graduation,
is central to ensuring postsecondary long-term success for students with disabilities. Families are
particularly important partners because students often need parents’ or guardians’ information to qualify
for programs. Educators can intentionally serve as liaisons and coordinators to help families who qualify
for services to:
• understand program eligibility criteria (e.g., income verification, documentation of disability, or
service needs),
• complete initial applications,
• submit required documentation,
• participate in intake interviews, and
• attend meetings to develop service plans.
While in the NIC, Uncommon solidified a process for engaging families throughout the school year to
support student enrollment in postsecondary services at community-based organizations. Connecting
families with the organizations was important for the students in Uncommon’s self-contained program
because they and their families often depend on higher-level and longer-term services more than
students who are fully included in the general education setting.
Collaboration among school-based stakeholders, including special education case managers, transition
coordinators, and college counselors, is also critical for supporting students in choosing and pursuing
the right postsecondary pathway. When postsecondary transition teams are composed of a diverse set
of staff members with various knowledge and viewpoints, team members can each give input, focus on
students’ interests, integrate family preferences, and mitigate potential biases that can unintentionally

direct our priority population to less lucrative or lower-status occupations. Uncommon built structures
for regular collaboration between their career and college success coaches, alumni success coaches,
and special education team members to ensure the engagement of students with disabilities in
postsecondary planning throughout high school and after graduation.

Using data regularly enables teams to assess and monitor students’
transition progress.
Regularly reviewing data on student progress toward postsecondary goals and providing interventions
when needed increases the number of students who successfully complete their postsecondary
pathways. Team members at NIC schools updated data as they interacted with students so that their
teams understood students’ current performance. Data on student attendance, grades, behavior,
and home life, considered alongside data on progress toward postsecondary goals, helped teams to
support students more efficiently. Mastery Schools accomplished this by refining meetings between
the postsecondary team, special education case managers, and social workers to regularly collect
and monitor data on students’ progress toward their transition goals. By reviewing this data at least
biweekly, Mastery’s team could respond promptly to student needs rather than waiting for students
to fall behind. Schools also used this data to determine whether all students with disabilities were
accessing transition opportunities, not just students with mild disabilities or those pursuing more
traditional pathways.

Enabling Conditions
Each of the practices in this collection strengthens postsecondary transition planning and services
to improve outcomes for Black and Latinx students with disabilities experiencing poverty. The
effective implementation of these practices was made possible because of three core enabling
conditions that were in place in NIC members’ schools: strong transition plans, collaboration
time with stakeholders, and dedicated staffing. Without these three conditions, the practices’ impact
is dramatically reduced.

Clear, Measurable, and Attainable Transition Plans
All students with disabilities ages 16 or older must have transition plans with a goal in each of the
following: postsecondary education, employment, and independent living. Some states and localities
require transition plans as part of the IEP when students are 14 years old. Clear transition plans that
are developed with the students’ and families’ input are essential for schools to create and implement
effective transition programming. Student interviews, career interest surveys, transcript audits, and
parent interviews are examples of ways that educators can collect data to help determine what
students’ IEP transition goals will be.
Like academic goals, transition goals need to be measurable and attainable for students. It is especially
important for transition goals and related activities to be aligned with the language used by the school
so that all educators and administrators can clearly understand how students’ goals fit with existing
structures. Nevertheless, students’ goals should be based on what is best for each student, not on
what is best for the school. In some cases, students’ IEP goals will require schools to develop new

Dedicated Time for Stakeholder Collaboration
For successful implementation of the transition best practices described in this collection, educators
leading the transition practices need dedicated time to collaborate with fellow educators, students, and
community-based organizations. Both before those meetings and after, educators need time to work
directly with students on their transition activities, assess and document progress toward transition
requirements, visit students at work-based learning sites, and enact additional support for students who
are off track toward their transition goals. Finally, at least one staff member needs dedicated time to
communicate and interact with community-based organizations to build and maintain relationships so
that students have access to important resources and programming. Mastery was able to create these
structures with biweekly team meetings that were led by a dedicated transition coordinator who kept the
team accountable.

Dedicated Staffing
Transition programs are strengthened when staffing allows a dedicated educator to monitor, develop,
and support transition services for students with disabilities. Having a staff member focused on
ensuring that every student successfully transitions to the postsecondary programming of their choice
is key to improving outcomes for students with disabilities. Case managers can write transition plans for
students’ IEPs, and special education teachers can teach
skills or collaborate with advisors or seminar teachers
to ensure students learn skills in school. However, a
strong transition program requires solid relationships with
community-based organizations, which takes significant
time and effort that needs to be dedicated to those tasks.
Both Mastery and Uncommon devoted resources so that
individuals at the school and network levels could support
the further development of external partnerships and
ensure staff collaboration between general and special
education within their schools and networks. These staff
members were integral to the implementation of their
transition programs.

The Collection
This collection includes three best practices that support the education of students with disabilities on
behalf of improving their postsecondary outcomes, and two case studies on additional ways to ensure
that students and families are able to access postsecondary resources.
Each of the practices detailed in this book is an independently strong and valuable practice. However,
each practice is most effective when built on a programmatic foundation that provides opportunities for
academic learning, disability-related skill development, and postsecondary transition experiences.

The audience for this collection is educators, schools, and districts who believe that all students are
capable of achieving the postsecondary goals of their choosing, who fundamentally believe that it is
the system’s responsibility to ensure this is a reality for all students it serves, and who are looking for
ideas on how to initiate or refine their approach to this work. While a great many research papers,
reports, and technical assistance centers provide guidance on postsecondary transition, this collection
is unique:
• It ties together the multiple elements critical to addressing the postsecondary transition needs of all
students and specifically those of Black and Latinx students with disabilities experiencing poverty.
• It emphasizes the importance of using data at all levels of the school system and of engaging
individuals from all levels of the school system.
• Each chapter is grounded in both the foundational and current research on the practice but
emphasizes (1) detailed, real-world examples of the practice as implemented in secondary schools
and classrooms within the NIC and (2) a step-by-step guide to the implementation of the practice.

Chapter 1: Building School-Based Transition Teams to Accelerate
Student Progress Toward Postsecondary Goals
Schools should monitor transition progress in the same way they monitor other student outcomes
so that teams can intervene and adapt support for students when data indicates it is necessary.
Postsecondary and transition teams need to regularly review data and make decisions on how best to
support students throughout the year in order to ensure all students achieve their postsecondary goals.
This chapter describes how schools can create regular meeting structures so that school staff who work
with students on their postsecondary goals can collaborate and monitor student progress.

Chapter 2: Establishing External Partnerships to Strengthen Work-Based
Learning Opportunities
Typical dual enrollment programs allow students to receive high school credit while taking college
courses. States like Pennsylvania allow students to receive high school credit while also meeting
requirements for college, certification, licensing, or other pathways students may choose to enroll
in after high school. To be successful, dual enrollment requires strong partnerships with external
organizations or institutions, both public and private, to provide students a variety of choices for
pursuing their postsecondary pathways. This chapter provides a guide to creating a dual enrollment
program, describes the staff and additional resources necessary for its successful implementation, and
outlines steps to create and maintain new external partnerships with community-based organizations.

Chapter 3: Providing Summer Transition Programming for
Postsecondary Success
When students are unable to secure a placement in a postsecondary institution (e.g., college,
workplace, workforce training center, community living, or the military) before graduation, intensive
support during the summer after graduation can help those students meet their postsecondary goals.
Counselors, working with students 1:1 and in small groups, can ensure that students are completing
the steps necessary to secure a postsecondary placement by the end of summer. This chapter outlines
how to develop such a summer program, including sample weekly schedules for counselors and an
explanation of how to develop a summer transition plan for students.

Case Study: Partnering with Families to Expand Enrollment in
Community-Based Programs
To ensure students’ successful transition out of high school, schools and educators often need to
engage directly with families in the steps necessary to obtain the continuing support and services
students need. Many families require explicit directions and detailed information about opportunities
for their children, as well as regular follow-up to ensure they are meeting application deadlines and
are able to get all the necessary documentation. This case study explains the steps that schools or
educators can use to help families enroll in community-based programs, particularly those provided by
the Division of Vocational and Rehabilitation Services. These steps can apply to any external program
and can help schools build stronger relationships with families.

Case Study: Developing Relationships Between Students and Alumni
Success Coaches
Counselors who support students after graduation need to understand the unique needs of students
with disabilities in order to properly support this population. In addition, students in separate classroom
settings need opportunities to build deep, trusting relationships with these counselors in order for
students to accept and use the support offered. This case study describes how one network met both
needs by creating structures for collaboration between counselors and special educators, which led
to students with disabilities better understanding the different staff members helping them reach their
postsecondary goals. Though not all schools have the alumni success coaches described in the case
study, the lessons offered in this chapter can be applied widely to collaborative activities between
special educators and college or school counselors.

How to Approach this Collection
The pieces in this collection can be read in any order, as one is not necessarily
dependent on another. Chapters are written to help the reader replicate the practices in
their own context. Case studies can be read to inspire ideas but will not necessarily give
the depth needed to implement the practice.

Literature Review
ABSTRACT
Studies examining postsecondary outcomes for students with disabilities report that
this population consistently experiences poorer outcomes than the general student
population across multiple indicators, including participation in postsecondary education
and employment, independent living, and job benefits and earnings (Newman et
al., Secondary School Programs, 2011). However, these results are not a foregone
conclusion; research also supports the finding that effectively implemented transition
planning and programming in middle and high school settings is positively correlated with
improved postsecondary outcomes (Cobb et al. 2013; Cobb and Alwell 2009; Test et al.
2009). This literature review synthesizes the existing research on practical approaches to
transition planning and programming. We define transition planning and programming for
students with disabilities, explain why it is important, and summarize what is known about
transition planning practices that are associated with positive postsecondary outcomes.
Woven throughout are the findings specific to Black and Latinx students with disabilities
experiencing poverty.

Introduction
A primary mandate of public schools is to prepare students for postsecondary life as engaged citizens
by equipping them with the necessary skills and knowledge to thrive in education, employment, and
beyond. This school-to-adulthood transition can be particularly challenging for students with disabilities,
and postsecondary outcomes for this population consistently lag behind those of their peers without
disabilities. Across several decades, extensive research has been conducted to understand the
challenges and best practices associated with postsecondary outcomes for students with disabilities.
This literature review was written for an audience of educators, schools, and districts seeking to
influence postsecondary outcomes for students with a focus on Black and Latinx students with
disabilities experiencing poverty. Our goal is to provide a comprehensive yet concise summary of recent
research, literature reviews, and meta-analyses on this topic to aid our audience in understanding (1)

What is Transition Planning and
Programming for Students with
Disabilities?
Transition services for students with disabilities are a federal mandate outlined in the Individuals with
Disabilities Education Act (IDEA). Beginning in 1990, IDEA mandated that schools provide transition
goals and services to students with disabilities aged 16 and older and required consideration of
student interests, preferences, and needs (Kohler and Field 2003; Martin, Boon, and Love 2007).
Presently, IDEA requires that Individualized Education Plans (IEPs) for students aged 14 and older
include appropriate, measurable postsecondary goals that are based on age-appropriate transition
assessments and transition services (including a course of study) needed to support the student with a
disability to achieve those goals (Individuals with Disability Education Act 2004).
IDEA defines transition services as a coordinated set of activities for a child with a disability that is:
• results-oriented,
• focused on improving the academic and functional achievement of the child with a disability,
• based on the individual child’s needs, and
• inclusive of instruction, related services, community experiences, the development of employment
and other post-school adult living objectives, and if appropriate, acquisition of daily living skills and
provision of a functional vocational evaluation (Individuals with Disability Education Act 2004).
School districts are further required to invite the student to participate in any IEP meeting related to
transition planning and programming and to consider the student’s strengths and preferences when
developing the individual transition plan (US DOE 2017).
The transition planning process consists of the following steps (National Technical Assistance Center
on Transition, "Predictors of Post School Success," n.d.):
1. Collecting information on student strengths, needs, interests, and preferences in the areas of
academics, career interests, self-determination skills, and independent living skills
2. Establishing post-secondary goals in the areas of training and/or education, employment, and
independent living
3. Identifying services and activities (including a course of study) that will assist the student in
achieving the established goals
4. Specifying annual IEP goals in alignment with the course of study
5. Engaging the student, family, and community organizations to facilitate the individual components
of the transition plan

Why is Transition Planning and Programming for
Students with Disabilities Important?
The National Longitudinal Transition Study-2 (NLTS2) followed a nationally representative sample
of students aged 13 to 16 years at the start of the study in 2000 until 2010 in an effort to understand
their educational, vocational, social, and personal experiences as they transitioned from adolescence
to adulthood (Newman et al., Secondary School Programs, 2011). The NLTS2 found that students
with disabilities consistently performed more poorly than the general student population on a range
of indicators: they were less likely to both enroll in and complete postsecondary education programs,
were less likely to live independently, and experienced poorer employment outcomes (fewer hours per
week, lower salaries, and reduced benefits). According to the US Department of Commerce (Newman
et al., Post-High School Outcomes, 2011), an individual with a high school diploma who has not been
identified as having a disability will earn, on average, one million dollars more over a lifetime than a
similarly educated person with a disability.
Black and Latinx students with disabilities experiencing poverty are at risk for greater postsecondary
employment difficulties (Dutta et al. 2019). The NLTS2 found that Black students with disabilities
enrolled in and completed postsecondary training and education programs at significantly lower rates
than white students with disabilities. Students with disabilities from households earning less than
$25,000/year are significantly less likely to be engaged in postsecondary employment than students
with disabilities from households earning more than $50,000/year (Newman et al., Secondary School
Programs, 2011).
While US schools have a long way to go to ensure students with disabilities achieve postsecondary
success commensurate with their peers without disabilities, it is encouraging that school-based
transition services have resulted in improved student outcomes. Since transition planning for students
with disabilities was mandated in 1990, fewer students with disabilities have dropped out of school,
and more have enrolled in postsecondary education. Students with disabilities are employed at higher
rates now than they were prior to 1990 (Trainor, Morningstar, and Murray 2016), and transition planning
during high school is associated with increased access to disability support in postsecondary education
programs (Newman, Madaus, and Javitz 2016). This suggests that transition programs grounded in
evidence-based practices have the potential to positively impact postsecondary outcomes for students
with disabilities.
Further, a body of research examining the predictors of postsecondary success for students with
disabilities provides the field with a strong research base of specific interventions and evidence-based
practices for transition planning and programming.

Work-Based Learning Experiences
Work experience refers to any activity that places students in authentic workplace environments. It can
include work sampling, job shadowing, internships, and paid employment. Work-related experiences
during school are consistently correlated with higher rates of employment and engagement in
postsecondary education programs for students with disabilities (Test et al. 2009; Mazotti et al. 2021).
These opportunities contribute to developing technical, vocational, and employment skills and assist
students in making informed decisions about long-term career interests (Cease-Cook et al. 2015).
Work experiences are most effective when they include the following (Kohler and Field 2003; National
Secondary Transition Technical Assistance Center, n.d.):

Self-Determination and Self-Advocacy Skills
Self-determination refers to the combination of skills, knowledge, and beliefs that enable individuals
to engage in goal-directed, self-regulated, autonomous behavior. Self-advocacy is a subskill within
self-determination, and refers to an individual’s ability to “effectively communicate, convey, negotiate
or assert his/her own interests and/or desires” (National Technical Assistance Center on Transition,
“Instruction in Self-Advocacy,” n.d.). Instruction in self-determination results in increased use of these
skills and increased participation in IEP meetings (Algozzine et al. 2001). Self-determination skills are
also associated with improved outcomes in postsecondary education programs (Test et al. 2009) and
increased rates of independent living (Mazotti 2021).
Self-determination and self-advocacy skills can also increase the likelihood that students with
disabilities have access to accommodations in postsecondary educational programs (Davis and
Garfield 2021). Students with disabilities are more likely to successfully complete 2-year and 4-year
college programs if they receive supports (those available to all students and disability-specific
supports). Yet, only 24% of students with disabilities receive accommodations in postsecondary
education. This is often because students do not disclose their disability status to their institutions
(Newman, Madaus, and Javitz 2016). Active participation in transition planning meetings can serve
to build student awareness of their disability and necessary accommodations and set up students for
successfully advocating for services after high school (Morgan et al. 2017).
One evidence-based approach to building student self-advocacy skills is the Self-Directed IEP, in which
students with disabilities learn to actively participate in and lead their own IEPs (Martin et al. 2006).
The Self-Directed IEP uses video modeling, student assignments, and role-playing to teach skills like
asking for feedback, stating goals, asking questions, and dealing with differences in opinion. Studies
have found that participating in Self-Directed IEP meetings results in greater student leadership and
participation in IEP meetings, more positive student and adult perceptions of the IEP process, and
increased levels of self-determination (Martin et al. 2006; Askvig et al. 2020).

Interagency Collaboration
Interagency collaboration describes the exchange of information and sharing of resources across
multiple stakeholders from schools, service agencies, community organizations, families, and students
(Transition Coalition, n.d.) Schools should consider interagency collaboration with organizations that
provide health and mental health services, vocational services, continuing education opportunities
and supports, and services tailored to individual student circumstances (National Collaborative on
Workforce & Disability for Youth 2018).
When executed effectively, interagency collaboration is associated with improved enrollment in
postsecondary education programs and increased rates of postsecondary employment (Test et al.
2009). There are benefits to the participating agencies as well, including access to additional resources
to support transition services, cost savings, and elimination of redundant services (Martin, Boon, and
Love 2007).
Factors associated with successful interagency collaboration include (Kohler and Field 2003; Poirier et
al. 2022):
• formal interagency agreements that outline roles, responsibilities, communication, and the like;
• a dedicated staff member (e.g., a “transition coordinator”) responsible for transition planning and
programming tasks, including establishing and overseeing interagency collaboration; and
• interagency participation in shared training opportunities.
Citing the lack of specific training for working with culturally and linguistically diverse (CLD) students
and families in the transition planning process, Brown Ruiz and Scott (2021) present a framework for
implementing culturally responsive practices during collaborative transition planning. The steps of the
culturally responsive pre-employment transition services framework are as follows:
1. Service providers from the school and community agencies and organizations build professional
relationships by establishing mutual understanding of roles and strengths, providing feedback, and
holding each other accountable for understanding biases.
2. Service providers invest in a long-term process and work to understand the historical and
institutional barriers faced by students and families.
3. Service providers engage in critical reflection to acknowledge systems of oppression and build
social capital within the community.
4. Service providers incorporate culturally responsiveness and critical action in the transition planning
process, including building student and family self-advocacy skills.

with improved attendance, academic outcomes, and student self-esteem and reduced dropout rates
(Kohler and Field 2003). Factors associated with increased family engagement in transition planning

Culturally and linguistically diverse (CLD) families and families experiencing poverty have lower rates
of participation in school-based transition planning activities than white, English-speaking families
(Hirano et al. 2018; Ju, Landmark, and Zhang 2018). Factors contributing to these lower participation
rates can be categorized into three groups:
• School barriers: lack of awareness of cultural values and issues on the part of school staff, lack of
availability of interpreters, and untimely communication about transition planning activities
• Adult barriers: undervaluing of the family’s role in student-centered planning, low expectations for
family involvement, and the level of bureaucracy encountered by families and students
• Family barriers: low self-efficacy, stress, lack of resources, and lack of cultural capital
Strategies to increase the meaningful participation of all families in the transition planning process
include the following:
• Provide training and workshops to families to increase their knowledge about the transition
planning process.
• Create networking opportunities for families to build relationships with other families in the school
community.
• Provide professional development and coaching to build school staff knowledge and skills related
to the engagement of CLD families in the transition planning process.
• Improve school-family communication by using multiple means of presenting information,
avoiding jargon in communication with families, and enhancing translation and interpretation
services.

Inclusion in General Education Classrooms
Students with disabilities who are primarily educated in the general education environment consistently

special education settings. They are more likely to participate in postsecondary education, be engaged
in postsecondary employment, and live independently (Baer et al. 2003; Test et al. 2009; Mazotti et al.
2021).

What are the Barriers to the Implementation of SchoolBased Transition Planning and Programming Practices?
Despite the evidence that well-designed transition planning and programming structures are associated
with improved postsecondary outcomes for students with disabilities, many US schools do not
consistently implement evidence-based practices for transition planning (Martin, Boon, and Love 2007;
Scheef and McKnight-Lizotte 2022).
First, many students simply fail to participate in high school transition planning activities (Trainor,
Morningstar, and Murray 2016). There are a number of factors influencing participation in transition
planning, including a lack of opportunity to practice the self-determination skills required to engage
in transition tasks (such as leading IEP meetings) and a mismatch between the normalization and
independence required for self-determination and familial and cultural values of particular students
and their families (Griffin 2011). Research has also documented dissatisfaction from students with
disabilities and their families who have not felt adequately valued or included in the transition planning
process (Trainor, Morningstar, and Murray 2016).
While interagency collaboration has been established as a best practice for transition planning, it is an
underutilized practice, with less than 25% of transition planning meetings including a representative
from a community agency or organization (Trainor, Morningstar, and Murray 2016). Barriers contributing
to low levels of interagency collaboration include a lack of training on how to establish interagency
relationships and how to refer students for services and a lack of awareness from community
organizations and agencies on the role they should play in transition planning (Scheef and McKnightLizotte 2022).

What Evidence-Based Frameworks Can Schools Use to
Design Transition Planning and Programming?
Kohler’s Taxonomy for Transition Programming
Kohler’s Taxonomy for Transition Programming 2.0 (Kohler et al. 2016) summarizes the literature
regarding predictors of postsecondary outcomes and strategies to foster successful transition for
students with disabilities into college and careers. It is the predominant framework for transition
planning and programming referenced across the literature on transition. The taxonomy provides five
primary practice categories:
• Student-focused planning: Student-focused planning refers to the activities involved in designing
and implementing individual student transition plans. It includes IEP development, planning
strategies, and student participation.
• Student development: Student development refers to the functional and academic skills that
support a positive and successful transition into post-secondary activities. It includes instruction in
academic, social-emotional, and employment skills, as well as assessment, student support, and
instructional context.

• Interagency collaboration: Interagency collaboration refers to coordinating the efforts of schools
and community agencies, such as college offices of disability services, vocational centers, and
adult service providers, to most effectively implement transition planning and programming
services for students with disabilities. It includes a collaborative framework and collaborative
service delivery.
• Family engagement: Family engagement refers to the ways in which schools actively support
family involvement in the transition planning and programming process. It also refers to schools'
specific actions to build family capacity to implement transition plans and includes family
involvement, family empowerment, and family preparation.
• Program structures: Program structures refer to the elements of transition programs that
support postsecondary outcomes for students with disabilities and the processes that schools
use to evaluate and continually improve transition planning and programming for them. Program
structures include program characteristics, program evaluation, strategic planning, policies and
procedures, resource development and allocation, and school climate.

Guideposts for Success
The National Collaborative on Workforce and Disability for Youth established the Guideposts for
Success in 2005 as a national framework for transition planning for students with disabilities (2018).
The guideposts were revised in 2018, and they currently outline five domains essential to effective
transition planning:
1. Career preparation and work-based learning experiences
2. Preparatory experiences
3. Youth development and leadership
4. Connecting activities
5. Family involvement and supports
The Guideposts for Success are meant to identify both what students with disabilities need to be
successful in postsecondary education and employment as well as what is needed to ensure they
achieve independence in adulthood.

Conclusion
In conclusion, formal postsecondary transition planning and programming for students with disabilities
is not only a federal mandate (Individuals with Disabilities Education Act 2004) but, when conducted in
alignment with practices supported in the literature, it is a critical contributor to positive postsecondary
outcomes (Cobb et al. 2013; Cobb and Alwell 2009; Test et al. 2009). Factors associated with improved
outcomes for students with disabilities include work-based learning experiences, self-determination and
self-advocacy skills, interagency collaboration, inclusion in general education, and family involvement.
Kohler’s Taxonomy for Transition Programming 2.0 (Kohler et al. 2016) and the National Collaborative
on Workforce and Disability for Youth’s Guideposts for Success summarize for schools and districts
the factors and transition practices most closely associated with improved outcomes for students
with disabilities. District staff looking to improve transition planning and programming for students
with disabilities are advised to implement outcomes-oriented transition programs that reflect high
expectations for students with disabilities, use data to continually evaluate and improve their transition
planning and programming practices, and engage multiple stakeholders in the design, implementation,
and evaluation of transition programs for students with disabilities.

Why is a School-Based Transition Team Important?
After high school, students with disabilities are less likely to live independently or to enroll in
postsecondary education, and they experience poorer employment outcomes than their peers without
disabilities.1 Transition planning combined with rapid support in implementing transition plans can improve
postsecondary outcomes for students with disabilities, no matter which postsecondary path they pursue.

Why it Works
TRANSITION PLANNING INCREASES THE LIKELIHOOD THAT STUDENTS WITH
DISABILITIES RECEIVE SERVICES IN POSTSECONDARY SETTINGS.
Transition planning, like any other type of academic counseling, can have an outsized impact on a
student’s ability to meet their long-term goals. Students with disabilities who engaged in postsecondary
transition-planning education, including education about what services they need after high school,
were highly correlated with students actually receiving postsecondary services in academic and nonacademic settings.2 Despite this, only about 20% of students with disabilities are enrolled in a high
school program that provides them with the opportunities and services to meet their transition goals.3
This low enrollment indicates that, though transition planning and programs can have positive outcomes
for students, a significant percentage of students with disabilities are not receiving the transition
services that would best support long-term success.
TEAMS THAT USE DATA TO SUPPORT TRANSITIONS HELP STUDENTS MAKE
PROGRESS TOWARD GOALS.
After establishing transition goals and plans, students with disabilities also need support in navigating
enrollment in programs or other steps required to progress on their transition plans. School teams that
provide support in implementation — including tracking student progress towards transition goals and
intervening when students are falling off track — increase the number of students who successfully
access their postsecondary pathways.4
COLLABORATIVE TEAMS BRING TOGETHER DIVERSE PERSPECTIVES.
Notably, systems for tracking student progress will be most successful when there is collaboration
between the core stakeholders, including special education case managers, transition coordinators,
college counselors, and families. Engaging in inter-agency collaboration (e.g., collaboration between
schools and the state department of rehabilitation) has proven to result in improved outcomes for
students with disabilities, including increases in both postsecondary employment and enrollment in
postsecondary education programs.5 When teams that support students’ postsecondary transition
progress are composed of a diverse set of staff members with various knowledge and viewpoints,
team members can each give input, keep the team focused on students’ interests, and include family
preferences, as well as help mitigate potential biases that can unintentionally direct our priority
population to less lucrative or lower-status occupations.
TRANSITION PLANNING IS THE LAW
The Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA) mandates transition planning activities for students
with disabilities starting at age 16, though many states require it to begin earlier. The act intends for these
transition planning services to be a collaborative endeavor that includes all individuals and agencies involved
in an individual student’s postsecondary plan.

Bringing this Practice to Life
USING DATA REGULARLY TO ENSURE SUCCESSFUL
POSTSECONDARY TRANSITIONS AT MASTERY SCHOOLS
Mastery Schools’ Simon Gratz High School, a college preparatory public charter school in Philadelphia, PA,
wanted to do a better job at keeping track of student progress on transition activities. They saw tracking this
data as essential for improving postsecondary outcomes for students with disabilities at their school. An
interdisciplinary team focused on postsecondary transitions began meeting every two weeks to review student
progress using a data tracker to help guide student-focused conversations. The case study below describes
Gratz’s process and the encouraging results of the practice.

Mastery Gratz’s Biweekly Transition Milestone Meetings
Every two weeks, the conference room table on the second floor of Simon Gratz High School
is surrounded by a team of the school’s veteran staff. College counselors, social workers, the
transition coordinator, and special education case managers all sit with their laptops open in sight
of a large TV screen. The screen displays a spreadsheet with data on every senior with an IEP on
a non-college pathway (e.g., workforce development, career, or military). Within the spreadsheet,
red, yellow, and green highlighting clearly shows overall student progress.

Keeping Track of Every Student
These transition meetings and their follow-up actions attempt to ensure that students complete the
necessary steps — “milestones” is the term Gratz uses — along the pathway to each student’s
postsecondary goal. The ultimate objective is that every student is engaged in a postsecondary

pathway when they graduate. The tools and protocols used during transition meetings all support
these purposes. The team keeps track of every student with an IEP, taking action to support those
furthest off track and regularly highlighting students’ success in dual enrollment programs.

Sharing Knowledge about Students
The color-coding in the spreadsheet makes it easy for the team to zoom in and focus on individual
students. The transition team focuses first on students who are furthest off track, those coded in
red. Team members with deeper knowledge about a given student let the team know any pertinent
information about barriers that impact the student’s ability to stay on track with their postsecondary
milestones. Barriers can include challenges at home, attendance issues, or that the student no
longer enjoys or is not meeting the expectations of their dual enrollment program. Each type of
barrier requires the team to consider the best way to support the student, whether with a phone call
home, a tour of a program accepting applications, or some other action to address the obstacle.
The conversation for each student is relatively short and ends when the team agrees upon one
or more action items and the team member responsible for executing the action items. The team
decides the best member to address the student’s needs by considering the type of relationship
each member has with the student, as well as their knowledge of the selected action step.
Following the meeting, case managers meet with each student who is off track to discuss the
proposed action plan. Case managers contact the student’s family with the student present to
ensure they are aware of the barrier(s) and can support the action plan.

Gains in Student Postsecondary Success
By using this practice over the last three years, Gratz has increased the number of students with
a clearly defined postsecondary goal and ensured that students are making progress toward the
goal during senior year. They also found that students remain engaged in the postsecondary
pathway after graduation. Gratz’s transition program efforts have increased, from 53% to 77%, the
percentage of students enrolled and engaged in postsecondary pathways in education, workforce,
military, or job training six months after graduation. One year after graduation, 90% of these
recent graduates with disabilities were engaged in their postsecondary pathway.

USING SUMMER TO ENSURE SUCCESS
Gratz has a summer transition support program for students who graduate without completing their
postsecondary milestones. The program, known as C3, also leverages rapid-response team actions
based on milestone data. Offering this summer transition support program would not be possible without
accurate milestone tracking throughout the school year. See Providing Summer Transition Programming for
Postsecondary Success for details.

Step 2: Input Initial Student Data
Create a system so that prior-year case managers, advisors, or seminar teachers can give the new
transition team information about students’ postsecondary goals to populate the tracker. During the
summer, transition teams can use this information in the tracker to map out milestones for the coming year.
Mapping milestones is considerably easier if the school has a clearly developed set of postsecondary
pathways outlining the necessary steps that lead to successful engagement after graduation. (See
Establishing External Partnerships to Strengthen Work-Based Learning Opportunities.)

The First Meeting of the School Year
At Gratz, all students learn about the school’s available pathways in their 11th grade seminar classes
and identify which pathway they are most interested in by the end of the year. Thus, transition
coordinators have the information they need to start tracking seniors’ milestones before school begins
in the fall. If a school does not have a system like this in place, the transition teams can meet in the
summer or early fall to share information about IEP transition plans, discuss milestones, and request
information from college counselors about students’ postsecondary plans. Teams can also use this
opportunity to clarify individualized expectations for seniors with IEPs, confirm necessary modifications
for specific students, and identify events or activities that students with disabilities need to attend.

Step 3: Form and Launch the Transition Team
Including a variety of general and special education staff will improve school transition teams’
effectiveness. Each team member will have unique information about students, the various pathways
they may choose, and the necessary resources to support them as they work toward their goals. It is
helpful for the transition team to have a leader, such as the transition coordinator, the school leader, or
a postsecondary advisor.
Consider including the following individuals in transition teams:
TRANSITION COORDINATOR
The transition coordinator is the special education team’s postsecondary expert and will often possess
more information about alternatives to 4-year college. They also often have relationships with external
organizations or agencies that can support students during and after their transition out of high school.

SPECIAL EDUCATION CASE MANAGERS
Special education case managers are responsible for ensuring transition plans are updated and easily
accessible for the transition team. In addition, case managers are often the primary contact for families.

COLLEGE COUNSELORS
The college counselors — who may also work with general education students in advisory or seminar
classes — hold the majority of information around college application deadlines and requirements, and
they can communicate that information to the transition team for students with goals to attend college.
College counselors may also be responsible for follow-up with graduates to ensure they receive any
additional support for their disability after graduation. While not all students will attend college, college
counselors have their own contacts, networks, and resources that can also apply to non-college
postsecondary education. Having college counselors present at transition team meetings is an easy way
to ensure that they are informed about the needs of students with disabilities and can support them in
reaching their goals with the same success as their peers.

SOCIAL WORKERS
Social workers are essential to help address student needs outside of academics or employment. At
times, social workers may be the only appropriate staff to take a deeper level of support or action steps
for students or families. They can add valuable input regarding services, agencies, or other resources
students may need in order to reach their postsecondary goals. Social workers can help the transition
team develop a plan to address housing and food insecurity, along with mental health and other social
services that a student may require to be successful.

SCHOOL LEADER (OPTIONAL)
Including a school leader as part of the transition team can help ensure that the leader is knowledgeable
about different types of postsecondary goals and the steps students need to take to reach those goals.
School leaders may also be helpful in communicating with families, ensuring that students can leave
school if necessary for transition activities, removing any barriers to implementing plans, and considering
how the larger school program can support students with goals apart from a 4-year college. However,
when transition team members mentioned above have been with the school for many years, they may
hold enough capital and sway within the school to make the addition of the school leader less essential to
the transition team.

Step 7: Plan Action Steps
For students who are not on track to meet their postsecondary milestones, the transition team
collaboratively designs action steps to address student needs.
Action steps could include the following:
• The student works with a case manager to complete a resume.
• The student meets 1:1 with the college counselor to complete the FAFSA.
• An educator emails follow-up reminders for students.
• An educator holds meetings with outside agencies.
• A team member contacts the family to discuss progress and problem solve barriers.
• The student changes their dual enrollment program.
• The student changes their pathway.
• The IEP team convenes to discuss the transition plan.
• A team member supports with situation-specific interventions (e.g., community resources,
advocates, legal support for families).

Step 8: Assign Next Steps
Finally, the transition team determines which adults will support students with each action step. Action
steps are reviewed by the team and documented in the tracker. The transition team lead may send
a summary of the meeting discussion and action items to the team following the team meeting to
communicate plans to absent team members and supporting leadership. This summary can also serve
as a reminder of action items, as well as confirm dates for completion and convey collaboration needs
from those not in attendance.

After the Meeting
Step 9: Include Families in Action Steps
After the meeting, case managers follow up with students and families on current data, including
attendance, grades, discipline, and transition activities that need to be completed. The case
manager also collaborates with families on action steps the transition team recommended so that
families can support current action plans.
Whether a student is on track or off track, keeping families informed about student progress toward
transition goals is an important part of helping students stay motivated and focused on their longterm goals.

Summary
Ensuring that students with disabilities have a smooth transition into their life after high
school is the ultimate goal for most special education programs. To ensure that students
with disabilities engage in meaningful activities that help them meet their individualized
postsecondary goals, schools need to convene interdisciplinary teams of special and general
educators to review data on student transition progress toward transition goals and intervene
when students are not on track to meet their goals. These interdisciplinary transition teams are
most successful when they meet at a regular cadence and include a diverse set of educators
who support students with postsecondary transitions. Students also benefit tremendously
from having clear pathways with specific milestones that lead to their identified postsecondary
goals. Mastery Gratz has been able to help students successfully graduate and enroll in
postsecondary placements by using biweekly transition team meetings to keep students on
track along their pathways and intervene during the school year and into the summer after
graduation. This data-centered meeting structure is something that any transition team can put
into place to help improve postsecondary outcomes for students with disabilities.

Why are Dual Enrollment Opportunities Important?
Employment is an important component of adult life because it allows for independence, learning, and
socializing. Unfortunately, people with disabilities between the ages of 14 and 64 are considerably less
likely to be employed than people without disabilities. In 2022, only 34.8% of the population of people
with disabilities were employed; the same year, of people without disabilities, 74.4% were employed.1
Schools can play an important role in increasing the percentage of people with disabilities who are
employed by helping students engage in work-based learning opportunities before they graduate from
high school. Work-based learning gives students meaningful experiences, awareness of opportunities,
and information about what they may or may not want to do in the future.
Dual enrollment programs offered at most high schools let students earn college credit when enrolled
in college-level courses at accredited institutions. Many schools even allow students to have a shorter
high school schedule to make time for these college courses. These college-focused programs let
students experience college prior to graduating from high school and enrolling full-time, as well as get
a head start on college requirements. While some schools in the United States offer credit-bearing
vocational programming for students, many others do not provide students opportunities to experience
the workforce or to get workforce training prior to graduating from high school — especially not during
the school day or for credit of any type. Without these opportunities, students are not exposed to
supports and learning experiences that build their skills and knowledge to successfully transition into a
non-college environment. Through dual enrollment structures that include opportunities for students on
alternative postsecondary pathways, students are able to access support, training, and experience in
meaningful and relevant postsecondary areas of interest.

Why it Works
For students with disabilities, dual enrollment programs can be particularly useful for the
following reasons:
School staff can ensure that programs are a good fit for students and that program staff
understand student needs.
Students have the opportunity to transition into their postsecondary pathway with the support of
their current teacher and school staff.
Students can earn credit toward graduation.
Classes and services at school can help reinforce skills or lessons needed in programs.
Peers attending the same program can support one another.
Students can leave high school with work experience, new skills, and a potential future career.

Bringing this Practice to Life
GROWING A DUAL ENROLLMENT PROGRAM AT
MASTERY GRATZ HIGH SCHOOL
Mastery Schools’ Simon Gratz High School in Philadelphia, PA, needed to find opportunities for their students
with disabilities to engage in employment training to meet their postsecondary goals. Using a model commonly
employed for students planning to attend 2- or 4-year colleges, the Gratz transition team developed a way
for students to get high school credit for participating in work-based learning opportunities during the school
day. Within three years, the team grew the number of spots available to students pursuing postsecondary
employment from seven to ninety-one.
The following case study demonstrates how Gratz built a dual enrollment program that gives students with
disabilities opportunities to gain meaningful work experience and skill development before they graduate
from high school.

Mastery’s History of Dual Enrollment
For almost a decade, Mastery Public Schools has offered dual enrollment opportunities for collegebound students. These programs allow students to take college courses while in high school and
to earn both high school and college credit. At Gratz, postsecondary advisors and the school’s
transition coordinator recognized that some students wanted to gain experience in the trades
rather than enroll in college. So, they partnered with Orleans Technical College (a program of

Creating a Long-Term Vision of Dual Enrollment
For typical college-going dual enrollment programs, identifying partners is generally straightforward
because local community colleges already have partnership programs in place. Identifying partners
is far more challenging when trying to develop opportunities for students in a variety of different
postsecondary pathways and with diverse interests for work-based learning.
Initially, Gratz identified students who were at risk of dropping out of high school and had an
interest in going into the workforce. To support these students, Gratz partnered with Hopeworks,
a nonprofit organization that trains students, provides them with internships, and then helps them
find a job placement in high-demand, high-wage industries. The program was initially designed for
young adults who had no more than a high school diploma, but Hopeworks was open to developing
a partnership with Mastery to begin training students who were still attending high school. In the
2020–21 school year, seven students at Gratz were able to participate in dual enrollment with
Hopeworks as part of their high school schedule.
In 2021–22, when students returned to school in person after COVID-19 school closures, Gratz
started building from the collaboration with Hopeworks to fully develop their multiple pathways
model. Gratz’s transition coordinator, Carrie Stoltzfus, was able to meet in person with Hopeworks
to further cement the relationship and create structures and timelines to allow students to enter the
program earlier in the school year. Gratz’s ultimate goal, however, went beyond the partnership
with Hopeworks. They sought to have a more inclusive dual enrollment structure so that all 12th
grade students who were not 2- or 4-year-college bound could be enrolled in an outside program of
choice by the second semester of their senior year.
The Gratz team used career interests identified by students during transition interviews and in
junior seminar coursework to determine additional types of programs to pursue. The postsecondary
advisors and transition coordinators began inquiring with more outside training programs about
developing partnerships to offer more pathways to students. This required several levels of
outreach:
• Researching local businesses and organizations
• Calling them to ask questions and gain more information about what they did
• Visiting the program sites as needed and knocking on doors to get the right person to engage
in discussions about program services and potential partnerships

Results
The Gratz team’s commitment to building their dual enrollment program allowed them to engage
more students each year in non-college pathways. The seven seats at Hopeworks that first
year helped Gratz improve from having only 53% of students with disabilities engaged in their
postsecondary pathway after graduation to having 62%. In the next year, Gratz was able to add

By the end of the 2022–23 school year, Gratz was able to open up a total of 91 spots for students

How to Implement this Practice
This section provides step-by-step guidance for the development of external partnerships and the
implementation of a dual enrollment program.

Forming Partnerships with External Partners
To create an effective and sustainable dual enrollment program, time must be dedicated to the
ongoing development and maintenance of external partnerships.

Step 1: Understand Student Career Interests
An important first step in developing external partnerships for a dual enrollment program is
understanding the types of careers students are seeking after high school and the skills and
experiences they will need to attain jobs in those fields. Special education case managers, advisors,
counselors, or transition coordinators can use career interest surveys or student interviews to learn
more about the types of opportunities students want and need. These information-gathering activities
are part of the IEP and transition planning process for students with disabilities, but they can also be
embedded in the content of advisory programs, senior seminars, homerooms, or other opportunities
in which students learn about their postsecondary options.

Step 2: Research New Organizations
Next, school staff can research organizations and external partners that match students’ expressed
interests and are available for students to enroll in while attending their existing high school program.
Gratz discovered new potential partnerships by reaching out to programs through which their
alumni found jobs after high school. By using existing relationships with programs, social service
organizations, and other schools, potential partnerships can more easily be developed.
Where existing connections or relationships do not exist, exploring an organization’s website can be
a useful first step in researching whether a partnership will be possible. When making calls to speak
directly with an employee at the organization, prepare key questions and information to share about
the purpose and intent of the potential partnership. For some organizations, knocking on doors or
visiting the office in person may be the only way to make early introductions and gather information.

Regardless of the method used to contact organizations, consider the key pieces of information that
you need to know in order to determine whether to pursue a partnership. Some types of information
can include:
• What ages do they work with?
• Do they offer training to current high school students, or would they be interested in expanding
their services to high school students?
• Do they support work-based learning experiences?
• Where are they located, and is transportation easy to coordinate?
• What is the cost to the school for student participation?
• How many hours do students need to attend the program?
• What types of coordination are required between the school and the program?
• Does the program support students after graduation?

Step 3: Develop Strong Partnerships
In order to glean all the necessary information to determine if a new partnership is possible, it may be
necessary to have several conversations, in-person meetings, and visits to the program. A primary
contact person will likely become clear during this process, and it will be helpful to develop a rapport
with that person to ease future communication.
Part of developing a partnership is negotiating the logistics for formalizing the relationship. Some of
these logistics might include:
• funding or other resources (e.g., bus passes, lunches, or technology) the school will need to
provide,
• the number of open seats the program is willing to designate for the school’s students,
• the application process and/or prerequisites students need to complete to be considered for the
program, and
• arrival and departure times and required frequency of student attendance.
Additionally, it is important to clarify the expectations of students, including:
• attendance policies (including notification of lateness and absences),
• course requirements,
• reasonable accommodations or modifications,
• behavior and safety, and
• dress code.
By going through the process of solidifying the details of the partnership, a strong relationship can
be established between the school and the organization that will make future communication that
much smoother.

Step 4: Create a Communication Schedule
It will be important to establish a system and schedule for ongoing communication and coordination
between external organizations and the school, students, and families. School staff managing the
dual enrollment program will need to develop a schedule for the different types of communication
each organization requires. The frequency and type of communication will likely depend on factors
such as the number of students enrolled in the program, students’ needs, program needs, and
school capacity. While some programs may receive weekly in-person visits from school staff, others
will only require weekly email updates. At Gratz, the staff generally have the most interaction with
programs that enroll the greatest number of students.
Maintaining some type of regular communication with programs will ensure that students receive
what they need, program administrators feel supported by the school, and the partnership is
productively maintained.

Step 5: Develop a Process for Students to Apply to Programs
Before enrolling students into dual enrollment programs, it is important to create a clear and transparent
process for how students apply and get accepted to a dual enrollment program of interest. When
devising steps for the process, consider any prerequisite skills students may need for the program,
as well as how to determine whether students are committed to the program and are able to meet the
program’s expectations.
Not only did Mastery Gratz create their own student application for dual enrollment consideration, but
they also required students to attend information sessions and/or visits to the program itself before
completing their application. These requirements help make sure that students are fully informed about
the program, clearly understand expectations, and are committed to seeing through their engagement
with the program.
Gratz also created their own application for each program so that they could determine — before
students applied to the program itself — whether students would be a good fit for the program and
whether they had met the program’s prerequisites. Including this preliminary step can help reduce the
burden put on programs to review unqualified applications and applications for students who may not
be set up for success in the program.
Gratz’s full process is comprised of the following four steps:
• Students attend an information session and/or a visit to the program.
• Students complete Gratz’s application for the specific program.
• Gratz’s postsecondary team narrows down the pool of students who applied to the program,
basing their decisions on which students are best fit for the program, the program’s expectations,
and the open number of seats in it. Students also provide input in decision-making.
• Selected students complete the program’s official application.

Implementing Dual Enrollment
Step 6: Enroll Students in Dual Enrollment Programs
COUNSEL STUDENTS IN IDENTIFYING INTERESTS AND PATHWAYS.
Staff and students can use transition interviews, career interest surveys, and other student interests
and hobbies to identify possible dual enrollment programs that would fit each student well. The
postsecondary team can counsel students who are not well-suited for a particular program to consider
applying to another program. Consider having the team member with the best relationship with the
student have that conversation with them, as it may be difficult for some students to process.
GROUP STUDENTS IN SIMILAR PATHWAYS.
Group students, when possible, based on the pathway or program they are interested in so that
information sharing can be coordinated, timely, and relevant and so that students can support each
other in the process. The students from each interest group who are accepted and eventually enrolled
in a program will likely have a considerable amount of interaction together. They can start to form
working relationships with one another through the application process.
Creating pathway-aligned groups also allows staff members who may have deeper knowledge or
relationships with specific program partners to provide students with specialized support through the
application process. Prioritizing staff focus on a subset of partnerships and programs also ensures that
no one staff member is responsible for supporting all students or for knowing every program deeply.
ESTABLISH A TIMELINE TO SUPPORT THE COMPLETION OF APPLICATIONS.
Create a calendar that is backward-planned from application deadlines for each program. Due dates for
each step of the application process can take into account students who need additional time as well
as those who may be absent when information sessions or tours are held. To implement the application
and enrollment process, set up specific times for students to apply and enroll in a program. These
scheduled sessions help students meet deadlines and make sure they can successfully navigate the
enrollment process.

Monitoring student progress through application, enrollment, attendance, and, finally, postsecondary

Practice Overview
What is the Practice?
A summer program that provides students with targeted, 1:1 transition coaching to support them in
completing the final steps needed to meet their transition goals when they have not met them at the
time of graduation.

Why is Summer Transition Support Important?
Students with disabilities do not just need to graduate from high school. They also need to successfully
complete their transition plans so that they can follow their chosen path after graduation. Recent
government statistics showed that within the first eight years after graduation from high school, people
with disabilities made a dollar less per hour ($10.40 vs. $11.40), were less likely to both enroll in (60%
vs. 67%) and complete (41% vs. 52%) postsecondary education programs, and were less likely to live
independently (45% vs. 59%) than people without disabilities.1 This practice addresses the need to
support students, even after graduation, to finish essential steps for postsecondary success in college,
careers, and the community. Without this focus on transition plan completion, schools will struggle
to close the gap in labor force participation for individuals with disabilities, which is only about 38%,
compared to 77% for the general population. These outcome differences have tremendous effects on
individual earnings, as well as independence in society.2

Why it Works
STUDENTS SEEKING ALTERNATIVE PATHWAYS NEED CONTINUED SUPPORT.
Continuing targeted transition planning and programming support beyond graduation — particularly
for students with disabilities who are Black, Latinx, and experiencing poverty — ensures that students
who are not choosing 4-year college paths have the same support as their peers. Students choosing
an alternative non-college pathway are often left out of summer interventions that focus on ensuring
students have completed the necessary steps to start college. Students interested in advancing directly
into careers also need support in the summer to complete the steps necessary to start their post-high
school lives.
SIMILAR MODELS OF SUPPORT SHOW PROMISING RESULTS.
Research on postsecondary coaching programs that were location-specific to support students
attending 2- and 4-year colleges found that they have moderate positive effects on college enrollment
and persistence.3 Using this same strategy to support students on career, military, or community living
pathways could similarly help ensure that all students are successful after their high school experience.

Optional Step: “Summer Boot Camp”
During the 2020–21 school year, a time when students were transitioning between virtual and in-person learning,
Gratz launched this practice with a different set of steps. The original summer programming included a one-week
intensive “boot camp” followed by 1:1 coaching sessions. In this model, Mastery’s team frontloaded one week of
in-person days for students to complete orientation to the program, receive an overview of the transition pathway
options available, complete interest surveys or transition planning assessment tools if needed, and confirm goals
and priorities for their summer transition coaching within their existing College and Career Coaches (C3) model.
This approach allowed the team to form or strengthen relationships with students who they may not have worked
with closely due to the interruptions from COVID, and to establish expectations between students and their
coaches for goals, priorities, and routines to successfully complete the program.
The Gratz team has integrated much of the initial orientation and intensive-week content into activities with
students in the spring before they graduate from high school. By establishing strong data routines to identify
students in need of the C3 program — particularly those not on track to enroll in and begin their non-college
pathway — the Gratz team is able to complete training and assessment activities with students while they are still
enrolled in their high school classes. This early identification and engagement has resulted in the discontinuation
of the boot camp intensive-week model at the start of summer.
For schools that do not have the opportunity to complete activities with students in advance of the summer
program, an intensive week of summer transition programming may be a useful addition to this practice.

Bringing this Practice to Life
ENSURING ALL STUDENTS START SUCCESSFUL LIVES
AFTER HIGH SCHOOL
The COVID-19 pandemic threw schools across the country into chaos, and many of their students were
cast adrift. The postsecondary team at Mastery Schools’ Simon Gratz High School could not sit by
when, in the spring of 2021, they realized that few of their students were ready for life after high school.
Their quick planning that first year to create a summer boot camp for their seniors eventually spread
into a network-wide summer program to ensure that all students were ready to start the pathway of their
choice when they left high school.

Getting Students Back on Track in a Hurry
In March of 2021, students at Mastery Schools returned to in-person learning for the first time
since March of 2020 due to the COVID-19 pandemic. The postsecondary team at Gratz realized
immediately that their seniors were off track in completing postsecondary activities, such as
financial aid, college applications, resumes, job applications, and cover letters. By May, the Gratz
postsecondary team identified the need to implement more intensive interventions to ensure their
senior class — particularly the students with disabilities — reached their postsecondary goals.
The Gratz postsecondary team launched a three-day “boot camp” to provide an intensive learning
opportunity for students. All seniors who were off track with postsecondary tasks, including
students with disabilities, attended the boot camp on campus to try to quickly complete as many
postsecondary tasks as possible. With the help of three of the postsecondary team members,
students worked on resumes, searched for jobs, completed enrollment documents for colleges,
completed job applications, and practiced for interviews.

Creating Summer Structures for the Success of Students with Disabilities
The boot camp worked well for some, but not all, students. For the students who needed
additional support, the Gratz postsecondary and special education teams tried an intervention of
individualized coaching. The model for this intervention came from Mastery’s College and Career
Coaches (C3) program — an existing summer intervention program providing individualized
coaching, which was previously available only for Mastery students planning to enroll in 2- or 4-year
colleges. C3 attempts to increase matriculation to these postsecondary pathways and to reduce
“summer melt.”
The Gratz teams adapted the C3 program for the students with disabilities who were pursuing
non-college pathways. Over the course of nearly five weeks of summer, after the boot camp,
three Gratz postsecondary team members worked as coaches to support about 60 students with
disabilities in-person and remotely by phone, text, and email. Individualized coaching included
breaking down steps to complete transition goals, setting up regular deadlines with students to
meet transition checkpoints, and following up with students to ensure they met those deadlines.
The effort was a great success. By the end of summer 2021, 87% of Gratz students had
engaged in summer touchpoints with their coach, and 80% of students had enrolled in their
postsecondary pathway.

WHAT IS SUMMER MELT?
Summer melt is a term used to describe students who were accepted into college and intended to enroll
but did not complete the steps necessary to actually attend college in the fall. A host of interventions
are used to mitigate this issue and support students through the summer to complete the necessary steps for
enrollment. Groups working to reduce summer melt, such as nonprofit organizations, colleges, and high schools,
use tools like text messaging, coaching, mentoring, and summer bridge programs to make sure students enroll in
college each fall.

Expanding to Support All Students
Using the learnings and success of the first summer, the Gratz team enacted and formalized two
structures:
1. They strategically built the content from the boot camp into the existing 12th grade seminar to
ensure learning and progress monitoring were in place throughout the school year.
2. The C3 program adaptations were formalized and spread to other schools in the Mastery
network. By summer 2022, all Mastery students who were interested in non-college pathways —
regardless of whether they were in special education or not — could engage in C3 coaching.
During the 2022–23 school year, Gratz increased their team to include eight coaches — including
a social worker to support students and families with needs such as food and housing insecurity —
who worked with 184 students. The coaches spent about three days per week doing direct outreach

Lessons Learned for the Future
Within Mastery’s expanded coaching staff, not everyone had the original three coaches’ depth
of knowledge about the different pathways, requirements, and paperwork necessary to support
students easily. The addition of coaches taught the postsecondary team that they needed to
increase coaches’ training in the summer of 2023, leading them to provide additional training for
coaches prior to the summer of 2024.
The success of Gratz’s C3 program for students in non-college pathways is a result of their
adaptation of an existing model and the creation of an inclusive program that focused on helping
all students achieve the goals they set for themselves. In addition, Mastery’s willingness to use
extended school year funding and other financial resources for this program demonstrates the
network’s commitment to ensuring postsecondary success for every student regardless of pathway.

How to Implement this Practice
This section provides step-by-step guidance for implementation of summer transition supports specific
to a school and community context, along with accompanying resources to get started.

Before the End of the School Year
Step 1: Identify Students to Participate in the Program
Determine which students need or will likely need additional summer transition support to complete
their transition goals.
Potential factors could include:
• students with disabilities who are not in a self-contained program (as such programs often provide
their own transition support),
• students with a non-college postsecondary goal,
• students interested in transitioning into the workforce who are not currently working or who have
unstable employment, or
• students with low attendance.
The use of a postsecondary data tracking system during the school year can help inform which
students will be a good fit for summer transition support.

Step 2: Create Caseloads for Coaches
Build coaches’ caseloads by grouping students by pathway (e.g., workforce development, employment,
community living, or military) and then, if possible, by existing relationships between students and
coaches. Ideally, each coach can support 10–25 students on their caseload, with the exact number
depending on the number of milestones their students still have to complete, the level of support the
students need to be successful, and the type of pathway. For example, a workforce pathway can be
particularly time-consuming because the coach has to work with a student to find open positions, to
complete a number of applications, and to prepare for multiple interviews before the student is hired for
a position. Other pathways, such as the military, have a defined process that requires one application,
screening, and interview before the student is accepted. All of these factors should be considered when
creating a caseload for a coach.
It is especially helpful to both coach and student when the coach already knows the details of the
pathways assigned to them, such as the required paperwork, the primary points of contact at related
organizations or institutions, and the steps necessary for the student to enroll successfully. If possible,
coaches should have the opportunity to specialize in specific pathways and to become experts in
what students need to do to be successful when enrolling in those pathways. There can be nuance

Choosing the Right Coach
for Each Student
Often, Gratz case managers work as
summer coaches for the C3 program
because of their long-standing
relationships with many students and
families. Case managers typically are
not as well versed in transition activities
as transition staff or college and career
advisors. Gratz also has seminar teachers
who serve as coaches. Seminar teachers
may not know all students as well as case
managers, but they are typically very
familiar with student pathways. Gratz uses
each coach’s strengths and weaknesses
to determine which students should be on
the coach’s caseload. In other words, the
pairing of a particular student and coach
depends on whether the student would
benefit from having a coach they have an
existing relationship with or the student
would be better off with a coach who
knows the ins and outs of their pathway.

Training prior to the summer should include opportunities for coaches to learn deeply about the
pathways in which they will be supporting students, helping them to develop the specialization
mentioned above. Such opportunities may include talking to postsecondary team members who already
work with students on a pathway, collecting documents and resources, and visiting program locations to
develop relationships with the individuals who will work with students. By providing time and resources
for coaches to become experts in a particular pathway, schools improve the effectiveness of their
coaches, who can better help students to meet their goals during the summer.

Execute the Program
Step 7: Review Summer Plans with Students
At the first meeting of the summer, coaches review the final, individualized summer plans with the
students on their caseload. The plans should include:
• dates for when milestones should be completed,
• a specific schedule of 1:1 check-ins,
• dates and details of planned exposure experiences, and
• the schedule of small group meetings with other students working on the same pathway.
The review includes discussion of remaining questions, confirmation that students have the necessary
materials for the plan, and problem-solving for adjustments that may need to be made to the plan in order
to get started. With their plans in place, students can begin working on the steps indicated in their plans.

Step 8: Follow Up with Students Weekly
Using a meeting agenda, coaches conduct 1:1 check-ins with students on their caseload at least
weekly. Structured meetings support students’ progress towards specific milestones and completion of
key activities related to those milestones. These meetings are a regular opportunity to hold students
accountable for tasks they said they would do over the past week, reflect on the progress they have
made on their plan, adapt the plan if necessary, and set new tasks and deadlines.
Students who execute their plan independently may only need quick check-ins via text, while students
who struggle to complete the steps of their plan will likely need regular in-person support. In every
instance, it is important that coaches document successes, setbacks, and actions taken with students
so that the coach can keep the rest of the team informed.

As students complete milestones, coaches track their progress in a data tool. If the students are
off track according to the planned dates, coaches can determine whether students need additional
support, such as more time for specific milestones or more frequent check-ins, to achieve the targets.
Though sometimes the team will be able to predict before summer where a student will flourish and
succeed independently based on past performance or interactions, it may not always be possible to
anticipate which of the student’s tasks will be easier or harder for them to complete. For this reason,
coaching schedules should have flexibility so that the plan and schedules can be adapted at various
points throughout the summer in response to each student’s progress.
If a coach cannot get in touch with a student weekly, they can try to leverage other staff members who
have existing relationships with the student to help facilitate contact. It is best to try other methods
for contacting the student before reaching out to the family using the contact information provided at
orientation.

Step 9: Meet as a Team to Review Data and Troubleshoot
All coaches and members of the postsecondary and special education teams should meet regularly
throughout the summer to review data on student progress along pathways. An optimal cadence is
weekly or biweekly so that the team can help coaches problem-solve issues that are occurring for
individuals or groups of students in a timely manner (see Building School-Based Transition Teams
to Accelerate Student Progress Toward Postsecondary Goals). These meetings are also an
opportunity to adapt student plans or determine whether other staff members can be helpful points of
contact for students, families, or outside agencies.
The team can consider posing the following questions for discussion:
• What common barriers are students experiencing?
• What types of support seem to be most beneficial to students (e.g., text check-ins, small group
meetings, field trips)?
• How can the team meet the needs of students who are experiencing the greatest challenges to
make progress?

End of the Summer
Step 10: Debrief Summer with Team and Plan for Next
School Year
Set aside time at the end of summer to discuss the program’s implementation and effectiveness.
See How to Measure in the Appendix for different ways to measure program implementation and
effectiveness. It is important to use the documentation and learnings from coaches, students,
and regular summer team meetings to make needed changes for the coming school year and the
following summer.

Summary
Individuals with disabilities are half as likely to participate in the workforce as individuals
without disabilities. They need additional support to ensure that they have the skills and
resources needed to be successful once they leave high school. Schools can and should
provide support to all students the summer after graduation to ensure students achieve their
postsecondary goals. This support can come in many forms, such as 1:1 coaching, text
message reminders, or field trips. Students with disabilities may need more targeted support
from 1:1 coaching in order to complete the steps necessary to start their lives successfully
after high school. Gratz used their C3 program to ensure that all of their students with
disabilities were enrolled in a postsecondary program, in the military, or working. Like Gratz,
schools can increase the likelihood that students will participate in the workforce regardless
of their disability status by creating similar programs to support the critical transition to life
after high school.

WHAT IS THE PRACTICE?
Educators develop strategic relationships with students, families, and local organizations
that provide postsecondary resources to students. Educators can then support and
progress-monitor families’ submission and completion of applications to the organizations.

WHY IS IT IMPORTANT?
• Families need opportunities to learn more about the different supports and resources
available for their students after high school.
• Nationally, only about 14% of IEP meetings for students with disabilities who are 14
years and older include a vocational rehabilitation counselor. Another 17% of IEP
meetings include someone from a different outside organization.1
• Only eight percent of individuals with disabilities in the US who were 16–24 years
old applied for vocational rehabilitation services, even though an average of 56% of
applicants received services.2
• Many families experience significant barriers when applying for and accessing services.
By acting as intermediaries, educators can better ensure that students who need and
qualify for services receive those services.

The Challenge
Families need guidance to navigate student enrollment
in community-based organizations.
Many students with disabilities need transition services to help them reach their goals after high school.
Community-based organizations offer students with disabilities services, including educational day
programs, job training programs, and support for instructional accommodations for college students.
For many students, these services are essential for their successful transition out of high school.
Families do not always have the time to get all the information they need about community-based
organizations and make the connections themselves. Unfortunately, these organizations can also be
mired in bureaucracy and difficult to navigate. Families can struggle with different steps required for
enrollment in a program:
• Understanding program eligibility criteria (e.g., income level, disability type, or service needs)
• Completing initial applications
• Submitting required documentation (e.g., income verification, documentation of disability, or IEP)
• Participating in intake interviews
• Attending meetings to develop a service plan for the individual student
For North Star Academy Lincoln Park High School (LPHS) in Newark, NJ, connecting students
with disabilities and their families with these organizations is key to helping students reach their
postsecondary goals. Uncommon Schools has always worked to connect students and families with
community-based organizations that provide services to students after high school, but they did
not have a clear process and set of resources to support case managers and college and career
counselors in making these connections.

readiness teacher, worked with a diverse set of stakeholders from the network: Joe Frick, Uncommon’s

Snapshot
WHAT IS THE PRACTICE?
To effectively support students with disabilities during their high school-to-postsecondary
transition, special education teams partner with postsecondary teams to facilitate
relationships between alumni support staff and students. This proactive effort is
concentrated in students’ senior year, ensuring both students and alumni support
staff understand each other’s roles, needs, and ways of working together to achieve
individualized postsecondary goals. Special education case managers guide the process,
facilitating the establishment of effective relationships and collaboration methods to
achieve students’ individualized postsecondary goals upon graduation.

WHY IS IT IMPORTANT?
• Research suggests that when students with disabilities feel a sense of connection and
support from school staff, they are more likely to actively engage in educational services
and interventions.1
• Establishing these connections between students and alumni counselors before
graduation ensures students leverage the team’s resources and guidance for
postgraduation programs and services.
• Many charter school networks have transition-focused programs and staff, yet they
traditionally emphasize college enrollment and persistence. Awareness among students
with disabilities and their families about these available programs and their broader
postsecondary benefits is often lacking.
• Engaging with students over their years in high school helps staff members understand
the varied learning profiles and postsecondary transition goals of students with disabilities.
This common understanding allows staff to productively partner with students, and
families as appropriate, so students enroll in and persist with one or more college, career,
or community programs aligned to their individualized transition plans and goals.

The Challenge
Traditionally, postsecondary support teams do not
collaborate and coordinate with special educators to
address the individualized transition needs of students
with disabilities.
Remote learning during the COVID-19 pandemic made it challenging for school staff across the United
States to regularly interact with students. This made forming relationships with students difficult,
particularly for new teachers and staff. For graduating seniors, these difficulties sometimes meant that
they did not have strong connections with school staff, to whom they would normally turn for support
with the transition out of high school. This absence was felt even more acutely by students with
disabilities, for whom transition support is essential to connect with necessary services or to pursue
less traditional postsecondary goals, such as those in workforce development or community living.
At Uncommon Schools, the challenges of remote learning were particularly difficult for the Office
of College Access and Success (OCAS) team as they endeavored to facilitate relationship-building
between students and their alumni success coaches. These coaches support students after graduation
to ensure they achieve their individual postsecondary goals. Tonya Ballard, a special education teacher
and the college and career readiness coach for North Star Academy Lincoln Park High School’s
self-contained class — the Impact Program — realized that only about 60% of her students were
responding to their alumni success coach, Roy Pellew. The OCAS team recognized that they needed
to develop solutions so that Uncommon staff like Roy and Tonya could collaborate to increase students’
engagement in working toward their postsecondary goals.
Before working with alumni success coaches like Roy, Uncommon students attend seminar classes
from freshman to junior year to explore postsecondary opportunities and to prepare for the transition out
of high school. In their senior year, students learn more about the support that alumni success coaches
can provide once students leave high school. During the pandemic, students’ initial interactions with
their alumni success coaches had to

reason to pick up the phone or answer

The Practice
Proactively Introducing Postsecondary Transition
Support Systems
To create a game plan for how to better engage students in the Impact Program with their alumni
success coach, Tonya and Roy knew they needed to work together more than ever before. With the
support of their special education and OCAS teams, Tonya and Roy devised a plan to foster working
relationships between Tonya’s students and Roy throughout the school year. In doing so, they believed
students would be more open to Roy’s outreach attempts and Roy could learn more about students’
postsecondary goals long before their graduation.
In September, to start off the school year, Roy and Tonya delivered to all students in the Impact
Program a presentation about Roy and his role. Roy explained that he would be meeting with students
throughout the year and working with them on their postsecondary plans. Tonya then held an evening
workshop specifically for the Impact Program families at which she introduced Roy and the Uncommon
alumni services that he could provide. The workshop was an important opportunity to emphasize that,
though the overwhelming message is often “college, college, college,” Roy would support students in
achieving their goals regardless of the pathway they chose.
After the first workshop, Roy and Tonya scheduled specific times for Roy to visit Tonya’s classroom. The
visit would enhance students’ awareness of Roy’s role on their postsecondary team and his availability
as a resource. At the visit, Roy actively participated in a JobCorps workshop with Tonya’s students to
gain insights into their career and community interests beyond college. Furthermore, Roy collaborated
with Tonya throughout the school year to deepen his understanding of the transition process and the
special education support available to students.
In the first stage of their collaboration efforts, Tonya and Roy experienced a number of barriers that
required them to adapt their approach. For instance, after the initial meeting with Roy at the start of the
school year, a sample of students was surveyed on their understanding of Roy’s role and their plans to
work with him. Five out of six students reported a clear understanding of the alumni success coach’s
role, and two-thirds already planned to work with their coach after graduation. The remaining students
were uncertain about future collaboration with him. Because they had started early in the school year,
Roy and Tonya were able to work together to alleviate some of this uncertainty among Tonya’s students.
Tonya and Roy created opportunities to clarify to both students and families how Roy could support
students before graduation, as well as how the alumni success coach role continues to work with
students after high school. Roy taught lessons specifically for students in the Impact Program to learn
about the different options they have available to them after high school. Roy and Tonya met to create
materials that could help students and families understand what students’ accommodations might
look like in postsecondary life and how to use their accommodations in college. Just as he would have
done before the pandemic, Roy met with each student individually to make sure they had a plan in
place and knew the resources available to help them reach their postsecondary goals. These personal
interactions helped Roy understand that many students needed help staying motivated as they finished
high school and feeling like they had agency over their future goals.

By starting at the beginning of the school year, Roy was able to build students’ knowledge slowly and
in small bites throughout the year. As students learned what they needed to do in their final year of
high school and how Roy could support them, they were able to build the agency they needed to be

reinforced Roy’s role with students and families when they reached out to her for issues related to
postsecondary planning by encouraging them to contact Roy instead. Tonya and Roy’s continued
collaboration also helped them both stay aligned and able to communicate students’ needs as they arose.

How Uncommon Implemented
the Practice
The process Uncommon’s team designed includes the following steps:

Step 1: Hold the First Collaboration Meeting Before School Starts
Special education teachers, case managers, or transition coordinators need to meet with alumni success
coaches before school starts or near the start of the school year (i.e., within the first quarter). This meeting is
a chance to align on students’ transition goals, resources available, and anticipated student needs. Special
education coaches and/or OCAS leaders may join where needed to support the team in their planning and
remove barriers to its implementation.
Step 2: Schedule Dates for Collaborative Activities During the School Year
Alumni success coaches and special education teachers create a calendar of joint activities that happen at
least quarterly. Some of these can be informal, like meet and greets, which help students and families build
trust and get comfortable with their alumni success coach. Other activities can be more formal opportunities
for alumni success coaches to teach lessons about certain postsecondary topics (e.g., navigating the FAFSA,
writing personal statements, or interviewing).
Step 3: Hold the First Meet and Greet Activity (1st Quarter)
This first meet and greet can be with students or students and their families. The activity has several
purposes:
• Learn who the alumni success coach is
• Understand what the alumni success coach does
• Provide students an opportunity to ask questions and share about themselves
• Begin building relationships
If this activity does not include families, another family engagement event should be planned early in the
year so families also know their alumni success coach and the coach’s role in helping students meet their
postsecondary goals. Use the time with families to collect additional contact information in case students
change phone numbers, emails, or home addresses. At least one of these should stay constant.

Step 4: Alumni Success Coach Attends Other Transition-Related Events
Throughout the school year, alumni success coaches should periodically engage with students and staff
for major transition-related events, such as college and career fairs or special education specific transition
events. As the coaches learn about other organizations and disability specific topics, they can assist students
and staff in making connections with community and college resources and provide information to guide
postsecondary transition efforts.
Step 5: Winter and Spring Meetings with Students
The team establishes meetings during the school day for students to meet with their alumni success coach in
both winter and spring. At these meetings, the coach shares resources developed by the OCAS team with
students and families to provide proactive support for working on postsecondary pathways. Meetings and
content should align with students’ chosen postsecondary goals and pathways. When appropriate, meetings
may also include other support staff who are working with the student on their plan for life after graduation.
Step 6: Alumni Success Coach Attends Students’ Exit IEPs (End of Year)
As the end of the school year approaches, case managers and alumni success coaches meet to discuss each
senior student, their goals, and their needs for coaching support upon graduation. They also set up times for
alumni success coaches to hold an exit meetings or to attend students’ exit IEPs to discuss a plan for support
directly with students. Attending these exit meetings allows alumni success coaches to align with students on
the postsecondary support they will receive based on their goals and priorities postgraduation. It also allows
coaches to have a better understanding of students’ disability-specific needs (e.g., accommodations, services,
or social-emotional needs). Coaches utilize the information gathered from this exit meeting to guide their
future interactions with students.
Step 7: Establish Follow-Up Communication Channels
Alumni success coaches confirm or revise the best communication methods for connecting with students and
families, as well as with school staff to monitor postsecondary transition progress. Alumni success coaches
may need to coordinate with their OCAS team and other site or network leadership to leverage shared
resources or address unique needs for student transition progress.

IGNITING POSTSECONDARY ASPIRATIONS

Conclusion

Preparing students with the necessary skills to transition successfully into adulthood after high school
— whether they’re entering 2- or 4-year colleges, workforce development programs, employment,
the military, or community living — is a critical objective for schools. Unfortunately, to date, individuals
with disabilities aged 14 to 64 are significantly less likely than those without disabilities to secure
employment, enroll in and complete postsecondary education, and live independently.1 While high
schools traditionally emphasize college enrollment post-graduation, it is equally vital for schools
to explicitly teach postsecondary skills to ensure that students with disabilities succeed no matter
their preferred postsecondary pathway. Research indicates that participation in career and technical
education programs increases the likelihood of high school graduation, attainment of industry
certifications, and post-graduation employment for students with disabilities.2 Aligning postsecondary
pathways with student goals and providing real-world opportunities within these pathways enhances
students’ readiness for life after high school.3
Over the course of three years, the Marshall Street Networked Improvement Community for Students
with Disabilities (NIC) saw teachers and leaders from 10 participating public charter networks
implement, study, and adjust various best practices for improving outcomes for Black and Latinx
students with disabilities who are experiencing poverty. This collection — itself an outcome of the work
these schools have done — describes some of the strongest of these practices, focusing specifically on
how participating schools improved choice-filled transition planning and programming. It highlights three
best practices: school-based transition teams, external partnerships for work-based learning,
and summer transition programming. There
are also two case studies, one focusing on
how educators at Uncommon Schools use
strategic relationships with families to expand
enrollment in community-based programs
and another on how Uncommon Schools
focused on building relationships between
special education staff and college and
career readiness teams to improve alumni
success coaches’ understanding of students’
individual postsecondary needs, allowing them
to support students after graduation more
effectively.

The practices that the participating schools utilized over the lifespan of the NIC significantly improved
postsecondary outcomes for the population of students they were targeting. For example, Mastery
School’s Simon Gratz High School developed and expanded dual enrollment programming to allow
students to gain work experience and learn life skills with local organizations while also earning credit.
Mastery Gratz also created school-based transition teams to monitor and support students as they met
milestones toward postsecondary pathway enrollment. This combination of practices increased the
percentage of students with disabilities who were engaged in their chosen postsecondary pathway after
graduation between 2019 and 2022 from 53% to 77% — a number that increased to 95% one year
after graduation in 2023. Two years earlier, the summer after returning to in-person learning following
the pandemic, the Gratz team implemented structured summer programming to support transitions out
of high school, including a three-day boot camp and individualized student coaching. These actions led
to 80% of students enrolling in their postsecondary pathway in what otherwise would have been a very
challenging year.

Key Takeaways
THERE ARE MANY WAYS TO PURSUE A CHOICE-FILLED PATHWAY AFTER HIGH
SCHOOL.
While college has traditionally been seen as the ideal postsecondary pathway, this assumption
has evolved over the last decade.4 More and more young people are electing to pursue diverse
postsecondary next steps across college, career, and community pathways. High schools need to
prepare students to achieve their secondary goals by implementing outcomes-oriented transition
programs that reflect high expectations for students with disabilities regardless of the pathway students
may choose. To shift mindsets about successful postsecondary transition, schools must embrace
inclusivity, recognizing that success extends beyond traditional college routes. This includes developing
tools, resources, and cross-functional teams to proactively support transition planning for all students,
particularly those with disabilities.
RUNNING A SUCCESSFUL POSTSECONDARY TRANSITION PROGRAM REQUIRES
DEDICATED STAFF TO LEAD AND OVERSEE THIS WORK.
Schools with the most successful transition programs in the NIC recognize that effective support
requires a comprehensive approach that plans for the needs of all students, including those with
disabilities. These schools made a significant financial investment in staffing to ensure that someone
could devote most or all of their time to focusing on students’ success after high school. This
investment allowed dedicated staff to develop meaningful relationships with students, understand their
goals and unique needs, manage program logistics, and forge external connections with community
organizations, resulting in successful postsecondary outcomes for students. To streamline the process,
both Mastery and Uncommon utilized existing structures and expanded them to accommodate students
with disabilities. This integration ensured that transition support for students with disabilities became
part of the school’s overall framework.

CHOICE-FILLED TRANSITION PATHWAYS REQUIRE PARTNERSHIPS ACROSS
STAKEHOLDERS.
For students to access the postsecondary pathway of their choosing, stakeholders must collaborate
to fill in gaps in the system and to educate students and families about the options available to them.
Students and their families are often navigating these transitions for the first time and need schools to
serve as a bridge between them and external partners (e.g., 2- and 4-year colleges, apprenticeship
programs, or community agencies) to learn about, apply to, and complete enrollment in these pathway
programs. Partnerships among students and their families, school and district staff and teams, and
relevant community organizations facilitate information sharing, the navigation of bureaucracies,
and essential progress monitoring to enhance student postsecondary prospects. Both Mastery and
Uncommon had cross-departmental transition teams that included counselors, transition coordinators,
and other key school-based staff, as well as thoughtful communication strategies to engage regularly
with students, families, and outside organization.
THE FREQUENT AND CONSISTENT USE OF DATA IS ESSENTIAL FOR SUCCESSFUL
TRANSITION PROGRAMMING.
Regularly reviewing student progress data towards postsecondary goals, along with timely
interventions, significantly increases the likelihood of students successfully completing their chosen
postsecondary pathways. A proactive data-driven approach allows educators to track student progress,
identify challenges early on, and provide targeted support, ultimately maximizing students’ chances of
achieving their postsecondary aspirations.

All students, regardless of race, socioeconomic status, or
ability, deserve to choose the life they want to lead.
Schools are responsible for doing everything in their power to give their students that
choice, especially their students positioned furthest from opportunity, at the intersection
of race, class, and ability. Simply creating an IEP that includes a transition goal is not
enough. If it were, we would not see the gaps in college and career attainment that
currently exist in the United States. The practices laid out in this collection highlight
some of the ways that schools in the NIC changed the landscape of possibilities for their
students with disabilities. Schools must ensure they are not creating two realities: one for
white, non-disabled students and one for marginalized students. Establishing a strong
system of support for postsecondary transitions is one way to do so.
