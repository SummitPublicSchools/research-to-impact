Overview
What is the
Practice?
The regular analysis of
student data to proactively
design classroom-based
interventions, typically
using a customized data
dashboard.

What do we mean by student
data for interventions?
For the purposes of this practice, “student data” includes any
quantitative or qualitative data that is:
1. Aligned: directly connected to the CMO or school’s mission
or goals for student learning, giving attention to the needs
of priority subgroups;
2. Regularly collected: routinely collected at regular
and predictable intervals, often on a weekly or biweekly basis;
3. Actionable: within a teacher’s locus of control so they can
take steps to improve student performance on the variable
measures; and
4. Student-level: related to student outcomes, whether that
be academic, social/emotional, behavioral, or for college,
community, or career readiness.
Student data may include individual assignments, test grades,
and mastery of standards.

Why is the Use of
Data Important?
Data-driven instruction is foundational to
effectively implementing multi-tiered systems of
supports (MTSS). Research shows that a lack
of knowledge and training around data-driven
problem solving for teachers, administrators,
and district staff is a key barrier to implementing
MTSS with fidelity.1 Schools can remove this
key barrier by thoughtfully designing systems for
teachers to regularly and frequently use data to
inform tiered supports. Disaggregating student
data by priority populations, such as students
with disabilities, helps educators think about the
instructional decisions they are making in the
classroom, as well as interventions that best
serve these students.

School Spotlight
Collegiate Academies created a data
dashboard to track student mastery of
Louisiana’s prioritized standards for
students with disabilities. This dashboard
helps teachers at Collegiate customize
instruction by allowing them to identify
specific high priority standards where
students are struggling and to adapt
future instruction for those students. The
dashboard provides teachers with the data
necessary to better individualize instruction
for each student, ensuring that all students
meet each of the standards and master
content necessary for graduation.

Why it Works
DISAGGREGATED DATA ALLOWS FOCUS ON PRIORITY POPULATION.
Whole-class, or aggregate, data masks differences that may exist between groups or individual
students. Therefore, it is important that data dashboards are able to disaggregate by group —
particularly for the different subgroups within our priority population, students with disabilities who are
Black and/or Latinx, and living in poverty — allowing teachers to unpack what is happening in their
classroom across subgroups. With this detailed data, teachers can ensure they are responding to the
students who most need additional support.
DATA CAN IDENTIFY AND MITIGATE BIAS IN THE SYSTEM.
Reviewing and comparing data across student subgroups can help teachers reflect on how
instruction was planned, lessons were delivered, and assignments were graded. When educators have
access to disaggregated data, they can identify trends and patterns of student performance on a variety
of mastery assessments, such as standardized assessments or teacher-made assignments, to see
discrepancies and reflect on their own systems or practices that may contribute to them.2
CUSTOMIZED DATA VISUALIZATION ENHANCES EXISTING SYSTEMS.
When schools have an existing student information systems (SIS), they are able to provide
efficient analysis and monitoring that helps teachers analyze student data in order to respond to
individual needs. Access to these systems, and other available data products, make it possible for
schools to maintain up-to-date student grades, test scores, disciplinary records, and progress toward
goals all in one place. It is especially critical for this priority population that data can be viewed in real
time, before students fall behind their peers. The end of a grading period is too late for a student to
make up or revise work, demonstrate their knowledge, or earn a passing grade. Timely intervention
within the school day can prevent very real repercussions for students, like failing to graduate high
school on time.

The Data Analysis Cycle
The data analysis cycle — that is, the process of teachers analyzing data, designing interventions
based on that data, and delivering those interventions — is essential to ensure that students who are
struggling to master content receive necessary support from teachers to be successful.

Bringing this Practice to Life
IMPLEMENTING ROUTINE DATA CYCLES TO RAPIDLY
ADDRESS STUDENT NEEDS AT STEM PREP
STEM Prep, a college preparatory public charter school network in Los Angeles, CA, was seeking to
empower teachers with the data tools teachers needed to regularly support students with personalized
interventions. After introducing a gradebook distribution tool and corresponding professional development,
math teachers at STEM Prep’s Crown Preparatory Academy (CPA) doubled the percentage of students with
disabilities with As and Bs in their classes. Their journey is described below.

STEM Prep’s Gradebook Distribution Tool
The principal and staff at Crown Prep Academy,
STEM Prep’s 5–8 school in Los Angeles,
noticed a challenge: the end-of-semester rush
to support students in completing classwork and
reassessments was primarily focused on raising
grades, rather than ensuring mastery of content.
The principal and staff decided to create a
system of progress monitoring using grades
from classes. They hypothesized that a custom
data dashboard would help teachers analyze
student grades throughout the grading period so that they could regularly respond to individual
student needs, and ensure early, targeted intervention to improve mastery of important skills and
content. Early intervention, they thought, would lead to greater mastery and higher student grades,
especially for students with disabilities.

Continuous Improvement Cycles
To start, STEM Prep leaders developed a
gradebook tool for adoption across the
teaching staff. The tool allowed teachers to
frequently monitor student progress toward
learning goals throughout the semester and
provide personalized interventions to support
students in the moment, rather than all at once,
at the end of a term.
To pull data into the gradebook tool, teachers
download a report from PowerSchool, STEM
Prep’s student information system, containing student letter grades on classwork. The report feeds
into a spreadsheet that groups students by grades, learning goals, grade reporting periods, and
subgroup, such as students with disabilities or multilingual learners. A dashboard overview for
teachers highlights how grades are distributed overall and how students are performing on average
against specific learning targets. The overview color-codes students with As, Bs, Cs, and nonpassing grades.
Using the gradebook tool, teachers can easily identify the students who are at risk of not passing
classes, quickly create short-term SMART goals focused on mastery of content, and immediately
develop an action plan based on each student’s needs. For example, teachers can use this
information to determine small-group workshops or invite students to join after-school tutoring.
The magic of the process is in how often teachers use the tool. When teachers use the gradebook
tool at scheduled intervals, they can see if previously identified students have met their SMART
goals, or whether different
action plans are necessary. This
approach allows teachers to
see and celebrate progress with
students on rapid cycles: students
do not have to wait months to see
whether their hard work is paying
off, and teachers can replicate
the instructional practices and
interventions that yielded the
greatest gains for students.
In 2021–22, Crown Prep saw
substantial growth, especially
for students with disabilities,
when teachers reviewed data at
least monthly.

A Culture of Learning
When first implemented, the dashboard struggled
to gain traction. The gradebook tool was presented
as a system teachers would use, yet teachers
didn’t fully understand why it was needed. Teachers
said that the tool was difficult to understand and
navigate. Subsequently, adoption was poor, and
the leaders needed to rethink the rollout of the
gradebook tool.
Approaching with a culture of continuous
improvement, the Crown Prep leadership team adapted their approach rather than scrapping
it. School leadership used feedback from teachers to improve the tool and created professional
development to build staff buy-in. To explain the purpose of the tool, Crown Prep used a thermometer
and thermostat mental model: PowerSchool, they explained, is the thermometer that shows the
current temperature of student performance. The tool is the thermostat, enabling teachers to change
that temperature through focused action.
The mental model landed well with teachers, who were able to connect the effort to Crown Prep’s
goals for student performance. According to Kate, an assistant principal at Crown Prep, “Being
transparent and explicit about our school-wide goals helped teachers to see the bigger picture and
empowered teachers to create more targeted and clear goals for their subgroups.”
With teacher buy-in on track, Kate reviewed the initial action plans. She found that they were
primarily student-centered (i.e., “the student needs to ...”) rather than teacher-centered (i.e., “I will
... for the student.”). Again, the leadership team adjusted based on what they had learned. School
leaders created a tool to develop action plans with each data pull. This tool provided guiding
questions to focus the teacher’s attention on who is struggling, what they’re struggling with, and what
actions the teacher would take to support the student.
To further support the use of data-driven instruction, scheduled data pulls and discussions were
built into the monthly professional development calendar, which proved essential to the success of
the effort. Teachers shared their goals for students with their coaches and administrators to inform
professional development and coaching sessions. In return, teachers got feedback on their approach
and progress. Coaches could also use the data to ask guiding questions to reinforce the protocol
such as, “How can I help you action-plan?” Repetition made the practice routine.
Having coaches support the development of interventions for students was a key way for Crown
Prep to build teacher capacity. Coaches suggested new interventions when prior interventions were
not successful. Coaches also guided teachers to reflect on why their initial attempts at instruction
were unsuccessful for particular students. This capacity building particularly benefited students with
disabilities who regularly need differentiated instruction and accommodations to meet their IEP goals.
Further success came when teachers began downloading data on their own, between monthly
professional development sessions, and independently sought opportunities to plan and take action
using their data.

Making the Grade
As the tool was adopted, Crown Prep began seeing a gradual increase in the percentage of
students with disabilities achieving As and Bs in their classes, and a similar decrease in the
percentage of students with disabilities not passing their classes. Among the 30 students with
disabilities served, the percentage of A and B grades increased from 25%, when continuous
improvement efforts began, to 81% in the Fall of 2022.
By focusing on students placed furthest from opportunity, Crown Prep also saw improvement in the
experience of all students. Among students at Crown Prep who do not have disabilities, A and B
grades improved from 49% to 77%.

Carrying it Forward
After implementing the tool at Crown
Prep, STEM Prep decided to begin using
it at their high school, Math & Science
College Prep (MSCP). MSCP conducted
professional development with their staff in
September and ran a follow-up professional
development in October, to ensure teachers
knew how to use the tool to analyze data
and make corresponding intervention plans.
By the end of the first semester, MSCP
teachers reflected that they struggled
to download their data to input into the
tool outside of monthly meetings, and
thus were more often going back to their
student information system instead of the
dashboard. As a result, MSCP’s leadership
decided to integrate more explicit time for
teachers to download data during weekly
professional development in an effort
to make the use of the dashboard more
regular. MSCP continues to iterate on
their process and find strategies to ensure
that routine data cycles can occur more
frequently and consistently for teachers.

Creating an Effective
Data Tool
For STEM Prep, the gradebook

successful because it ensured student

• aligned to their organizational
priority to improve student

• regularly collected, reviewed in
monthly meetings, and embedded

How to Implement this Practice
This section provides step-by-step guidance for implementing routine data cycles to guide student
supports, along with accompanying resources to get started. The steps are organized into four phases
for implementation: Prepare Needed Structures, Train Teams, Implement Routine Data Cycles, and
Provide Ongoing Support.

Prepare Needed Structures
Step 1: Determine School-Level Goals for Student Success
Data-driven instruction systems can fail to be adopted by teams when the systems are not deeply
aligned to a school’s mission and current student needs. Base goals on existing data and past trends
so that the goals are realistic and, more importantly, based on specific needs for priority subgroups. Use
historical data to create a baseline for the goals prior to beginning interventions in order to measure the
effectiveness of the practice. Goals need to be measured regularly and with fidelity over time.
For example, a school might explore their baseline data and learn that only 13% of students with
disabilities are on grade level in reading based on NWEA MAP. They could set a goal of improving to
25% by the end of the year, with benchmarks of growth at three percentage points per quarter. Then,
they set up the data tool to collect measures that can show growth on a regular basis throughout the
year so all educators know if they are on track to meet the goal.

Step 2: Set Up Data Dashboard
If an existing student information system does not provide educators with easy access to specific,
disaggregated data aligned to a school’s goals, school leadership will need to develop a data
dashboard for teacher use. (See Navigating Data for Multi-Tiered Systems of Support Overview for
more information on creating data dashboards.) Using school-wide goals, determine which classroomlevel data points best measure the goals to be used in the dashboard. These measures need to be
relatively easy for teachers to collect with fidelity and consistency. Examples of data include:
• engagement measures: attendance, assignment completion;
• formative assessments: exit tickets, checkpoints, independent practice; or
• summative assessments: project grades, course grades, unit tests.
Once the dashboard is established, test it for usability by teachers. Will teachers be able to easily
disaggregate data at many different population levels, including class periods and subgroups? Can they
easily refresh data or upload new data? These factors will help set the dashboard up for successful use.

Step 3: Design Protocols
A protocol provides directions for each step in a process, as well as the order in which steps should be
completed, so that different users of the protocol are all doing the same thing to complete the activity.
When using data for instructional support, it is important to use a protocol to help guide educators
through the data review, data analysis, and instructional planning process in a systematic way that is
standardized across teams.
Design the protocol to encourage teachers not only to look at their overall student trends, but also
to focus on individual students or small groups of students (e.g., students with disabilities), paying
particular attention to students who have not met learning expectations across several periods of time.

Step 4: Design Routines and Norms for Using Data
DATA ENTRY AND USE OF THE TOOL
Effective use of a data tool to design instructional supports for students relies on the timely — often
real-time — entry of data into the defined data systems. In addition, it's important to systematize data
collection and calibrate the quality of data collected in order to establish expectations and create
accountability for aligned, ongoing use of data for this purpose.
STEM Prep, for example, requires teachers at their high school, Math and Science College Prep, to have
all assignment scores updated every Wednesday. This norm allows teachers to pull and input data into
their dashboards for analysis during weekly professional development time. Without this clear expectation
for regular data entry from school leadership, teachers might have incomplete data during collaboration
sessions and may not be able to use the dashboard effectively for planning with colleagues.
Data dashboards that draw from an external database, such as a student information system, may
require manual downloads to populate the custom dashboard with up-to-date data. Some schools ask
teachers to do this themselves, and others do it within their central office. Consider the technical abilities
and capacity of educators when making this choice.
Special consideration should also be given to how data is updated in student information systems. For
example, if teachers are expected to input grades by 5:00 p.m. on Fridays, the data system should
refresh grade data on Friday nights. These decisions should be made based on teacher input. If these
systems are not created in a manner that works for teachers, long-term buy-in for the systems may suffer.

every two weeks, if not weekly. Each iteration of the cycle consists of three key steps: (1) analyze data,
(2) design interventions, and (3) implement interventions.

data and develop action plans based on that analysis. A fillable version of the data protocol template is
available in the Appendix.

Step 6: Analyze Data to
Identify Student Needs
The purpose of analysis is to identify groups of students with
common needs, or individual students with specific needs, that can
be addressed through interventions in future classes. In this step,
teachers look for commonalities among students, assignments,
and event dates to better understand why some students
mastered content and others did not.
It is often helpful to start by looking at the class as a whole and
noticing any data points that stand out. Use these outliers in the
data to guide further analysis. Consider which students may have had
procedural challenges versus conceptual challenges to help with the next step of planning
interventions. Sometimes it will be necessary to analyze students’ work in order to better understand
what they struggle with on an assignment (see A Collaborative Approach to Student Work Analysis
to learn more).
For example, imagine a class that averaged 80–90% on exit tickets in the last two weeks. Suddenly,
over the course of a few days, the average drops closer to 60%. This decrease is a sign that the
teacher should look carefully at those days to understand what happened. Similarly, they might look at
data for subgroups — students with disabilities, multilingual learners, students living in poverty, etc. —
to see whether these subgroups mastered the content at the same level as other students in class or
whether some assignments arise as concerns. Those outliers in the data tell the teacher to investigate
which students struggled the most with the content and to reflect on their own instructional practices on
those days.

Step 7: Plan and Design Interventions
Once teachers have students grouped by needs based on their
analysis of the data, they plan interventions aligned to the different
focus areas. Planning by focus area ensures that students learn
missing skills, correct misconceptions from prior lessons, or master
content to help them better access the next lesson. Interventions
can take many forms, but in every case should be designed based
on what was learned through the data analysis.
For example, teachers might determine from the data that
they should:
• design a small group session during the Do-Now,
• spiral in prior concepts for the whole class to practice,
• provide 1:1 support to help build essential foundational concepts, or
• coordinate with a special educator to plan a lesson.
School-level teams should develop a bank of interventions that can serve as a living reference for
all teachers and teams as they plan interventions that best meet student and classroom needs.

How the NIC Used Data to Make Instructional Decisions
Schools in the NIC have used data tracking and analysis in different ways. At Collegiate Academies,
teachers use Collegiate’s custom-made April Dunn Tracker to understand which standards students
have mastered at different levels over time. The tracker makes it easy for co-teachers to determine
which students in their class need re-teaching of content or skills related to a standard, to design
lessons so re-teaching can occur, and to reassess student mastery of the standard.
At Green Dot Public Schools, teachers have used data from work analyses to guide interventions.
For example, a co-teaching pair, after introducing a new concept in class and analyzing students’ exit
tickets, realized that their students had a wide range of needs. Subsequently, they designed mixedability groupings to allow students to learn from one another more easily and to help their peers starting
with varying amounts of prior knowledge. Using mixed-ability groupings allowed the co-teachers to
work more closely with individual students or groups that needed additional guidance.

Step 8: Implement Interventions
The most important part of the routine data cycle is implementation
of the planned interventions for students. Structures like
collaborative planning can help teachers design and implement
interventions with teaching teams (see Establishing Effective Data
Routines in School-Based Teams). Teachers can also work with
coaches to find new resources and improve intervention planning
and implementation across data cycles.
The following actions and considerations may be helpful for the
successful implementation of instructional interventions:
• Teachers develop classroom norms and routines to make
implementing interventions possible.
• Teachers plan a new lesson format or activity to re-teach content to reach a group of students.
• Coaches conduct observations to provide feedback to teachers to support their growth and
development implementing data-driven interventions.
• Teachers complete peer observations to access models and examples of interventions in practice.
• Coaches work with teachers to determine areas of growth in implementing interventions.
• Coaches schedule short periods of time with teachers when interventions will happen so that
coaches can maximize how many teachers they observe in a day.
Once the interventions are implemented, a data cycle is complete. Teachers will begin the next iteration
of the cycle by analyzing data to determine the effectiveness of their prior data-based decisions and
subsequent implementation of interventions.

Practice Snapshot
COLLEGIATE ACADEMIES’ APRIL DUNN STANDARDS TRACKER
In collaboration with the Networked Improvement Community, Collegiate Academies in New Orleans
developed a data system tailored to track progress toward graduation requirements of diverse learners.
Initially prototyped and tested in collaboration with classroom and school leaders, the data tracker tool and
the systems designed for its use have been scaled across all campuses.

In 2014, Louisiana’s Legislature passed Act 833, giving students with disabilities an alternative
method of achieving high school graduation requirements. Specifically, the change meant that
students with disabilities were no longer required to pass a Louisiana statewide assessment,
known as the LEAP. Instead, new “April Dunn Act” requirements offered another way for students
to demonstrate mastery of standards. For some eligible students, IEP teams are able to specify
that students will graduate through the mastery of state standards using alternative means other
than passing end-of-course LEAP assessments. The IEP teams are required to document the
achievement of the standards and fulfillment of requirements for high school graduation.
Collegiate Academies, like all schools in Louisiana, had to adopt and adapt to April Dunn
requirements for a subset of their students with disabilities. A key practice Collegiate developed
to meet the needs around April Dunn requirements was data-driven decision-making. Collegiate
created a standards tracker that allowed teachers to document and see which April Dunn statewide
standards each student met and how. Collegiate used their existing tracker for general education
statewide standards as a model for their April Dunn tracker. Originally separate, the two trackers
were eventually combined into one tool for all teachers to access.
The April Dunn tracker was particularly important to Collegiate’s goals for co-teaching. All students
with April Dunn standards included in their IEP had a plan for graduation, including taking at
least math and English in co-taught classes. Using a tracker allowed Collegiate’s co-teachers to
effectively support these students to meet their individual IEP goals and master the standards to
graduate.
At the beginning of the 2022–23 school year, after Collegiate had completed prototyping and testing
of the tracker tool with a subset of teachers, they began training all teachers on the use of the
tool. Using a fake data set, Collegiate leaders taught educators how to download data and use the
tracker. They found that it was easier to set expectations for the use of the tracker generally, and
for its use in co-planning, when launched at the beginning of the school year rather than mid-year.
Additionally, through the beginning-of-the-year roll-out, Collegiate’s leaders were able to train more
school leaders to independently edit and create functionalities in the tracker.
By training both teachers and school leaders, Collegiate created a long-term, sustainable system
for tracking, reviewing, and making instructional decisions for all learners, especially those in our
priority population.

IMPLEMENTING ROUTINE DATA CYCLES
TO RAPIDLY ADDRESS STUDENT NEEDS

Summary
Systematically reviewing, analyzing, and planning instruction based on specific, disaggregated student
data is essential in responsive instruction, particularly for addressing the unique needs of priority
populations such as students with disabilities. The success of this practice relies not only on developing
high-quality materials for data analysis and disaggregation but also on integrating them effectively
into the school’s culture, staff training, and teaching practices. Thoughtful design of systems that
enable frequent data use, particularly when disaggregated by priority populations, ensures informed
instructional decisions, targeted interventions, and the identification and mitigation of biases in the
educational system.
School leaders can ensure educators focus on the needs of students with disabilities by allocating
regular time and defining clear protocols to identify student performance trends and to review
disaggregated group and individual student data in light of those trends. Further, associated
professional development, particularly in the summer, sets educators up to successfully use data to
design interventions during lesson planning.
