Preface
In Turning the Page to Secondary Literacy: Tested practices for schools to identify gaps and implement
interventions for secondary readers, we share a set of best practices for improving learning outcomes
in secondary students — particularly Black and Latinx students with disabilities experiencing poverty
— using evidence-based practices for reading intervention in the secondary setting. The Networked
Improvement Community for Students with Disabilities (NIC), a group of 10 public charter school
networks serving more than 75,000 students nationwide, identified the best practices through their work
between 2019 and 2023.
Led by Marshall Street at Summit Public Schools, in partnership with technical assistance providers
NIRN, RTI International, SRI International, and SWIFT Education Center, the purpose of the three-year
NIC was to systematically improve the way our education system serves Black and Latinx students with
disabilities experiencing poverty and share these solutions with school systems everywhere. This report is
funded by the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation. The findings and conclusions contained within are those
of the authors and do not necessarily reflect positions or policies of the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation.
The NIC used continuous improvement to translate existing research for improving learning outcomes
into best practices and to iterate the practices to ensure they worked in a variety of school contexts.

Collection Introduction
The Challenge
Reading proficiency correlates strongly with educational attainment and
broader life outcomes.
Proficient reading and writing skills contribute to a wide range of life outcomes, affecting an individual’s
academic achievement, career opportunities, earning potential, and physical and social-emotional
well-being. While adequate literacy skills have far-reaching positive effects on individuals and society,
low literacy rates have equally impactful consequences. Individuals with poor reading skills are more
likely to drop out of school1 and to be incarcerated.2 They are less likely to obtain employment,3 to earn
an above-average salary, and to receive promotions at work.4 They also have higher reported rates of
depression and anxiety.5

Secondary students with disabilities are not developing the reading skills
necessary to support college and career readiness.
On the 2022 National Assessment of Educational Progress assessment:

Because low reading skills are a barrier to accessing grade-level content and standards, this reality
results in inequitable access to the rigorous academic content students need to engage with to be
prepared for postsecondary success.

The diverse needs of nonproficient secondary readers pose unique
challenges for schools.
Schools and districts seeking to improve reading outcomes of nonproficient secondary readers face
several unique challenges:
SECONDARY READERS ARE A PARTICULARLY HETEROGENEOUS GROUP.
They can differ greatly, not only in the degree of their reading difficulties but also in the nature of those
difficulties. They also tend to have difficulty with more than one aspect of reading.
THIS RANGE OF UNDERLYING CAUSES OF READING DIFFICULTIES NECESSITATES
AN EQUALLY BROAD RANGE OF INTERVENTIONS.
However, reading intervention in middle and high school settings has often represented a “one-size-fitsall” approach, with one intervention available to all students reading below grade level, regardless of
their individual reading profiles.
OUR TRADITIONAL ASSESSMENT SYSTEMS IN SECONDARY SCHOOLS AREN’T
DESIGNED TO ILLUMINATE THESE INDIVIDUAL READING PROFILES.
Commonly administered assessments at the secondary level often tell us which students are failing to
meet grade-level reading benchmarks and standards. However, without a tiered approach that includes
diagnostic assessment, we have little insight into why those students aren’t reading proficiently.
SECONDARY STUDENTS WHO DON’T READ WELL HAVE HAD MANY YEARS
TO DEVELOP SELF-CONCEPTS AS POOR READERS.
Reading is often effortful and laborious for them, and as a result, they read a significantly smaller
volume of text than their peers who are reading proficiently. These factors contribute to an overall low
motivation to read.

Why “Nonproficient Readers”?
Students with reading skills that fall below age- or grade-level expectations are often referred to as
“struggling readers.” This phrase represents deficit language that has the potential to perpetuate low
expectations and reinforce biases. The authors of this collection have intentionally chosen to avoid this
terminology and instead refer to students who are not yet reading at age- or grade-level expectations
as “nonproficient readers.” We hope this language will adequately describe the subgroup of students
that is the focus of this collection while promoting respectful and inclusive dialogue.

A research-to-practice gap means that secondary educators are not
equipped with the skills and knowledge necessary to improve outcomes for
nonproficient secondary readers.
The problem is not a paucity of research findings on reading intervention for older students. In fact,
leading reading researchers stated in a 2006 report that “...enough is already known about adolescent
reading — both the nature of the problems of struggling readers and the types of interventions and
approaches needed to address these needs — in order to act immediately on a broad scale.”6
The reality is that secondary teachers simply haven’t been equipped with the skills and knowledge
necessary to implement evidence-based instruction for nonproficient readers. They consistently report
that both teacher preparation programs and job-embedded professional development opportunities fail
to equip them with the knowledge and skills necessary to address reading deficits for this population.7

“It is both fascinating and frustrating that, despite what can only be described as
incredible advances in the broad and interdisciplinary field of reading and literacy
research, coupled with progress in both education policy and practice...Black and Brown
children have yet to benefit in sustainable, replicable, and transformative ways. Despite
its promise, the science of reading is not benefiting Black and Brown children at scale.”
DR. NICOLE PATTON TERRY, PROFESSOR AND DIRECTOR,
FLORIDA CENTER FOR READING RESEARCH8

Our Approach
Given the importance of reading proficiency to individuals’ long-term outcomes, the reality of the
current state of reading proficiency in our schools, and the complexity of teaching nonproficient
secondary readers, the implementation of effective reading interventions is essential to the NIC’s
aim of systematically improving the way our education system serves Black and Latinx students
with disabilities experiencing poverty. To close the research-to-practice gap, the NIC explored and
developed a set of accessible and effective interventions for secondary readers. At the foundation of
these interventions are the following guiding principles.

Effective secondary reading intervention is driven by data-informed
decision-making.
Schools within the NIC understood that to design and implement effective reading interventions, they
first needed to understand the needs of the nonproficient readers within their systems. Thus, our
approach started with universal screening of reading proficiency. That is, NIC schools first administered
assessments to all students to identify those who were not yet reading proficiently.

1.4

OVERVIEW

Literacy

While universal screening of reading proficiency is a relatively common practice in secondary schools,
it is often the only systematic assessment practice implemented in these settings. Because of the
heterogeneity of nonproficient secondary readers, simply identifying students reading below gradelevel expectations is insufficient for developing interventions that are likely to result in gap-closing
growth. As such, our approach also included diagnostic assessment, or a process of administering
additional, targeted reading measures to uncover the specific skill deficits contributing to low reading
proficiency for students identified via the universal screening process. By identifying the specific needs
of nonproficient readers, schools within the NIC could develop menus of reading interventions that
targeted students’ specific reading needs.
Exemplifying this commitment to data-informed decision-making, Green Dot Public Schools leveraged
a computer-based, adaptive measure of reading proficiency to identify students reading below an
established cut point. They then administered curriculum-based measurements of reading fluency to
this group of students. Analyzing the data from this fluency assessment allowed them to subdivide the
nonproficient students into three subgroups:
• Students who had not yet developed proficient word recognition skills
• Students who could recognize words but lacked the reading fluency necessary for reading
comprehension
• Students with adequate reading fluency but low language comprehension skills
Equipped with this comprehensive picture of reader profiles, Green Dot was able to tailor interventions
to individual student needs.

Effective secondary reading intervention is aligned with
evidence-based practices.
While many secondary schools implement some level of reading intervention for nonproficient readers,
these approaches often fall short because (1) they represent a “one-size-fits-all” approach in which all
students receive the same intervention regardless of their individual reading profiles, (2) they tend to
focus narrowly on procedural reading skills without consideration of the conceptual and knowledgebased skills necessary for comprehension of grade-level secondary texts, and (3) they are driven by the
availability of commercial intervention programs rather than the evidence-based practices that the field
of reading science has established with respect to secondary reading intervention.
The NIC understood that accelerating reading proficiency for Black and Latinx students with
disabilities experiencing poverty would require the implementation of practices that align with what
works and what doesn’t for our target population. The specific intervention practices implemented by
schools within the NIC aligned with evidence-based practices that researchers consistently cite as
critical to understanding and supporting nonproficient secondary readers. These practices include
multisyllabic word reading, reading fluency, and motivation and engagement. In response to the
recommendation that reading interventions at the secondary level are most effective when they target
multiple subcomponents of reading, schools within the NIC sought to provide interventions that would
address a range of reading needs rather than narrowly focusing on interventions designed to address
isolated skills.

At Uncommon Charter High School, educators redesigned an existing reading intervention structure
targeting 9th grade students to more comprehensively integrate evidence-based practices for
nonproficient secondary readers. Diagnostic data indicated that students participating in the reading
intervention course had specific skill deficits in multisyllabic word reading and reading fluency. Using
this data, the Uncommon team developed a daily instructional protocol that leveraged grade-level
texts to provide specific word study instruction, opportunities for repeated reading practice to build oral
reading fluency, and explicit vocabulary instruction in high-leverage, academically relevant words. They
paired this instructional protocol with strategies to build motivation and engagement, including student
conferencing and collaborative goal setting.

Effective secondary reading intervention enables meaningful participation
in rigorous, grade-level academics.
At many secondary schools, reading intervention is isolated from the core academic program,
creating a structure that lessens its effectiveness. For example, students may participate in a reading
intervention “class” that leverages a commercially available reading intervention program unrelated to
core course content and texts. This lack of alignment contributes to low levels of skill generalization
across contexts and creates barriers to providing inclusive services within general education
classrooms. It can also diminish motivation as students struggle to see the applicability of the reading
practice completed in reading intervention outside of that particular context.
Throughout the work of the NIC, it became clear that effective, developmentally appropriate, and
culturally responsive reading intervention needed to keep participation in rigorous grade-level content
as the north star. With this goal in mind, schools endeavored to align reading intervention efforts
with the core academic program. For example, Noble Schools designed an oral reading fluency
intervention that was facilitated by a special education and general education co-teaching pair within
the context of a general education World History class. This approach resulted in several benefits
for students, including reduced stigma associated with pull-out special education services, increased
access to reading intervention for students who did not receive special education services, and
improved reading comprehension of relevant academic texts.

Effective secondary reading intervention requires consistent, quality
professional learning opportunities for educators.
Research shows that effective professional development is both intensive and sustained — teachers
will learn a new skill well enough to positively impact student learning after approximately 50 hours
of professional development. However, professional development delivered in US schools does not
consistently align with these recommendations. A survey of US teachers designed to assess the current
status of professional development revealed that most professional development fails to explore
topics deeply and that nearly half of teachers are dissatisfied with their professional development
opportunities.9
Secondary teachers report that, even when they are provided with opportunities to learn strategies to
teach nonproficient readers, they rarely receive ongoing support. It comes as no surprise then, that they
also report feeling woefully underprepared to teach reading to this population of students.10

To build the capacity of educators to implement secondary reading interventions, the NIC established
a responsive, ongoing professional learning structure that included regular content-driven learning
opportunities and individualized coaching. The approach was designed to align with the elements of
effective professional development:11
• It’s content-focused: Professional learning for schools within the NIC working to implement
secondary reading intervention focused on discrete content related to evidence-based practices
(e.g., supporting motivation and engagement in secondary readers, word study interventions, and
vocabulary development).
• It incorporates active learning: Every professional learning opportunity provided to educators
within the NIC included structures to promote reflection, meaning-making, and application to
practice.
• It supports collaboration: Professional learning was structured via a professional learning
community (PLC), which facilitated collaboration and connection across schools within the NIC.
• It uses models of effective practice: Participants in the NIC professional learning community
had regular opportunities to observe instruction that aligned with evidence-based practices.
• It is of sustained duration: The PLC met monthly over the course of a full school year, with
individual coaching sessions provided between PLC meetings.

“Explicitly teaching and leveraging the science allows us to overcome our blind spots,
assumptions, and biases which impact every aspect of instruction. In other words,
the quest to fortify our society and its institutions begins with preparing our teachers
to apply the science of reading and reach all students. That’s the way to save us from
ourselves and avoid a permanent underclass filling our correctional institutions as
prisoners of the ‘reading wars.’”
KAREEM WEAVER, EDUCATION COMMITTEE, NAACP

Enabling Conditions
Each of the aforementioned principles contributed to improved experiences and reading outcomes
for Black and Latinx students with disabilities experiencing poverty within the NIC. The effective
implementation of these practices was made possible because of three core enabling conditions that
were in place in NIC members’ schools: assessment systems and practices, adaptive and flexible
service schedules, and responsive, strategic development opportunities for educators. Without these
three conditions, the practices’ impact is dramatically reduced.

Assessment Systems and Practices
Effective secondary reading intervention is implemented within the context of a Multi-Tiered System
of Support (MTSS), which includes universal screening practices to identify students in need of
intervention support, diagnostic assessment to pinpoint specific reading skill deficits, and ongoing
monitoring of the reading progress of students participating in interventions. In addition to the
assessment systems themselves, effective secondary reading intervention can only be achieved
when schools implement collaborative structures in which educators regularly review data collected
through screening, diagnostic, and progress-monitoring practices and leverage it to inform intervention
decisions for students.
Schools in the NIC implemented a range of screening measures to identify subsets of their student
populations who would require reading intervention to meaningfully participate in grade-level instruction
and curriculum. They conducted diagnostic assessment to further pinpoint specific skill deficits for
individual students and designed decision-making criteria to guide teams. With the belief that students
should only participate in interventions as long as they need them, schools monitored student progress
so that they could ensure students exited from interventions as skill deficits were remediated, or that
they were referred for increasingly intensive levels of support if needed.

Adaptive and Flexible Service Schedules
Scheduling is often cited as a primary challenge in the implementation of Response to Intervention
(RtI) models in secondary settings.12 Limiting reading intervention to a specific course offered within the
context of students’ schedules makes it difficult to return students to lower levels of support as skills
are remediated. It also limits students’ ability to engage in credit-bearing coursework, and it prevents
educators from flexibly adjusting intervention dosage based on student need.
To address these challenges, schools within the NIC were flexible and adaptive in the design of
reading intervention service schedules. For example, Green Dot Public Schools embedded an oral
reading fluency intervention within an existing comprehensive reading course, while Noble Schools
implemented a similar intervention within a co-taught general education World History course.

Responsive Development for Educators
Teacher knowledge of language and literacy constructs is associated with improved reading outcomes
for students. Effective teachers of reading know the basics of reading development, understand
language structure for both word recognition and language comprehension, apply evidence-based
practices in their instruction, and use valid and reliable assessments to inform their teaching.13 The
majority of professional development experiences offered to educators fail to adequately develop
this knowledge and these competencies, resulting in many secondary educators reporting feeling
unprepared to address their students’ needs as readers.
Eager to build their knowledge and skills related to language and literacy constructs and instruction,
educators within the NIC participated in ongoing collaborative and responsive professional learning
activities, including a regular professional learning community and individual coaching opportunities.
These structures provided targeted, relevant content with immediate opportunities to apply learning,
receive expert feedback, and revise approaches accordingly.

The audience for this collection is educators, schools, and networks who believe that all students are
capable of reaching reading proficiency levels that support meaningful participation in rigorous gradelevel instruction, who fundamentally believe that it is the system’s responsibility to ensure this is a
reality for all students it serves, and who are looking for a roadmap to initiate or refine their approach
to this work. While a great many research papers, reports, and technical assistance centers provide
guidance in each of the areas described above, this collection is unique:
• It ties together the multiple elements critical to accelerating reading outcomes specifically for Black
and Latinx students with disabilities experiencing poverty.
• Each chapter is grounded in both foundational and current research on the practice but
emphasizes (1) detailed, real-world examples of the practice as implemented in secondary
schools and classrooms within the NIC and (2) a step-by-step guide to implementation of the

Chapter 1: Guiding Literacy Interventions Through Tiered Assessment
Tiered assessment to guide literacy intervention planning refers to the practice of identifying secondary
students who are at risk of not meeting grade-level ELA benchmarks or standards, pinpointing specific
reading skill gaps for these students, and planning aligned evidence-based interventions to address the
identified skill gaps. This chapter outlines the individual steps involved in this process — (1) universal
screening of reading, (2) diagnostic assessment of reading, and (3) alignment of evidence-based
interventions to student needs — and provides guidance on the selection of assessments, development
of decision-making criteria, and protocols for collaborative data-driven decision-making.

Chapter 2: Repeated Reading: Oral Reading Fluency Intervention
for Secondary Readers
Oral reading fluency is highly correlated with reading comprehension, and schools that use a tiered
assessment approach to guide literacy interventions will likely encounter a subgroup of students who
lack the reading fluency necessary for reading comprehension. This chapter describes the
implementation of repeated reading, an evidence-based practice for building oral reading fluency,
across several settings within the NIC.

Chapter 3: Developing Multisyllabic Word Reading Skills for
Secondary Readers
Weak word-analysis skills and inaccurate decoding of multisyllabic words are often key contributors to
low reading fluency rates for nonproficient secondary readers. This chapter describes the implementation
of a word study intervention designed to improve fluency and accuracy of multisyllabic word reading.

Chapter 4: Strengthening Timely Interventions through Goal Setting
and Progress Monitoring
Effective implementation of secondary reading intervention requires establishing clear student goals,
implementing valid and reliable assessments to monitor student progress toward those goals, and
engaging in regular analysis of progress-monitoring data to guide instruction and intervention planning
decisions. This chapter provides guidance on the selection of progress-monitoring tools and outlines
a process for collecting data, conferencing with students, and analyzing data to inform intervention
decisions.

Case Study: Enhancing Educator Capacity to Implement Evidence-Based
Reading Interventions
Teacher knowledge of language and literacy constructs, implementation of evidence-based practices,
and proficiency with assessments and data-based decision-making are all associated with improved
student outcomes. Building these competencies requires practitioner-focused professional development
and aligned coaching for educators. This case study highlights the approach taken by the Marshall
improvement team to build the capacity of educators across the NIC working to implement secondary
reading intervention through a professional learning community and individual coaching structures.

Guidance Document: How-To: Assessment of Adolescent Readers
This brief resource describes components of assessment and decision-making for adolescent readers
for school and district leader consideration within an MTSS framework.

How to Approach this Collection
Readers of the collection should begin with chapter 1, as this chapter provides the
framework for (1) identifying students within the system who will require intervention to
reach grade-level ELA benchmarks and standards and (2) pinpointing the specific reading
needs of those identified students for the ultimate purpose of aligning interventions to
student need.
After chapter 1, readers are encouraged to learn about the specific evidence-based
interventions aligned with student needs identified via the tiered assessment process. This
collection includes chapters that clearly outline the implementation of interventions aligned
with two specific components of proficient adolescent reading: oral reading fluency and
multisyllabic word reading. However, as highlighted above, secondary readers often also
present with needs in vocabulary and reading comprehension strategy use. While these
components are not explicitly addressed in this collection, readers are encouraged to
leverage the following resources as practical, research-aligned implementation guides:
Vocabulary: Bringing Words to Life
by Isabel L. Beck, Margaret G. McKeown, and Linda Kucan
Comprehension: The Reading Comprehension Blueprint by Nancy E. Hennessy, M.Ed.

In addition, the following resources provide comprehensive summaries of the research on
secondary reading interventions:
Effective Instruction for Adolescent Struggling Readers: A Practice Brief
by Center on Instruction
Improving Adolescent Literacy: Effective Classroom and Intervention Practices,
an IES Practice Guide
Interventions for Adolescent Struggling Readers: A Meta-Analysis with Implications
for Practice by Center on Instruction
Providing Reading Interventions for Students in Grades 4–9
by What Works Clearinghouse

After identifying students, pinpointing their specific skill gaps, and aligning interventions
accordingly, readers are encouraged to leverage chapter 4 to create a plan for engaging
students in progress monitoring and the case study to plan for building teacher capacity.

Literature Review
ABSTRACT
Proficient reading is a critical contributor to positive postsecondary outcomes, yet US
schools fail to consistently and equitably equip students with the reading skills necessary
to participate meaningfully in college and career opportunities. This literature review
provides a synthesis of the existing research base of reading intervention for students
in grades 4–12. We define secondary reading intervention, explain why it is important,
summarize what is known about reading development generally, and synthesize the
literature on evidence-based instructional practices for nonproficient readers. We
provide a summary of the limited research findings specific to Black and Latinx students
with disabilities experiencing poverty and highlight relevant findings related to teacher
professional development.

Introduction
In addition to the specific college and career-ready standards specified for each grade level, the authors
of the Common Core State Standards have articulated the characteristics of literate individuals,
including that they:
• demonstrate independence,
• build strong content knowledge,
• respond to varying text demands,
• comprehend as well as critique,
• value evidence,
• use technology capably, and
• come to understand other perspectives and cultures (National Governors Association Center for
Best Practices 2010).

Achieving these characteristics requires secondary readers to flexibly and strategically employ a range
of complex reading behaviors, including reading text fluently, deducing the meaning of unknown words,
using strategies to support comprehension actively and flexibly, bringing background knowledge on a
wide range of topics to the reading experience, reasoning and making inferences while reading, and
maintaining motivation to read and engagement in the reading process (Torgesen et al. 2007). Indeed,
the demands on secondary readers are significant, yet current and historical assessments of reading
proficiency suggest that many middle and high school students do not demonstrate proficient reading
skills (National Assessment of Educational Progress 2022).
This literature review was written for an audience of educators, schools, and districts seeking to change

This literature review is part of a larger collection of improvement tools for districts. The Networked

What is secondary reading intervention?
Secondary reading intervention refers most simply to interventions implemented by educators and
school systems to improve reading outcomes for nonproficient secondary readers.
Lupo et al. (2024) offer a conceptual framework for literacy intervention at the secondary level which
defines literacy intervention as “any instructional effort to support adolescents’ reading, including
foundational skills, cognitive processes, meaning-making, agency, and identity.” The framework
further differentiates within-classroom from supplemental supports and emphasizes the importance of
professional learning that addresses critical content, supports a collaborative approach, and is aligned
with broader school and district structures, vision, and goals.

Why is secondary reading intervention important?
Reading intervention in the secondary setting is important because reading proficiency is associated
with myriad school-based factors, including achievement in content area classes and graduation
rates (Kamil et al. 2008; Snow and Biancarosa 2003), as well as overall life outcomes such as
employment, earnings, and physical and psychological well-being (Mulcahy et al. 2016). For students
with disabilities, especially students with multiple disabilities, the ability to read and learn contributes to
increased levels of independence in life skills (Kauffman 1996 in Almalki 2016). Additionally, reading is
the primary vehicle for learning in the secondary grades (Vaughn and Fletcher 2012). However, based
on the 2022 National Assessment of Educational Progress assessment, just 29% of 8th grade students
attending public schools in the US are reading proficiently. When the results are disaggregated by the
subgroups that represent the Networked Improvement Community’s target population, the numbers are
even more concerning; just 16% of Black 8th grade students and 21% of Hispanic 8th grade students
are reading proficiently, compared to 38% of white students. Nine percent of 8th grade students with
disabilities are reading proficiently compared with 34% of students without disabilities.
While preventing reading difficulties in the primary grades has historically received more attention than
secondary reading intervention, this population has been increasingly studied in recent years. In fact,
“Enough is already known about adolescent reading—both the nature of the problems of struggling
readers and the types of interventions and approaches to address these needs—in order to act
immediately on a broad scale” (Biancarosa and Snow 2006, 10). The focus of reading instruction at the

Understanding Reading Development
To effectively design and implement interventions for nonproficient secondary readers, it is important
to first understand how reading develops. The phrase “Science of Reading ‘’ has become common
vernacular for educators interested in improving student reading outcomes. The Reading League, a
national nonprofit organization with a mission to advance the awareness, understanding, and use of
evidence-aligned reading instruction, defines the science of reading as follows:

“The science of reading is a vast, interdisciplinary body of scientificallybased research about reading and issues related to reading and writing.
This research has been conducted over the last five decades across the
world, and it is derived from thousands of studies conducted in multiple
languages. The science of reading has culminated in a preponderance
of evidence to inform how proficient reading and writing develop; why
some have difficulty; and how we can most effectively assess and teach
and, therefore, improve student outcomes through prevention of and
intervention for reading difficulties.”
THE READING LEAGUE, N.D., 6

For this literature review, three key frameworks proposed within the science of reading will lay the
foundation for understanding how reading develops: the Simple View of Reading, Scarborough’s
Reading Rope, and the National Reading Panel Report.
First, the Simple View of Reading (Gough and Tunmer 1986) offers a straightforward way of
conceptualizing the factors that contribute to a process that is anything but simple. The Simple View
of Reading emphasizes that reading comprehension, which is the ultimate goal of reading, is the
product of two factors: word recognition and language comprehension. Word recognition refers to the
understanding that graphemes (or letters and letter combinations) represent sounds (or phonemes),
learning the relationship between phonemes and graphemes, and using this knowledge to read
unknown words, while language comprehension refers to understanding the meaning of words,
phrases, sentences, and passages. The Simple View of Reading illustrates the importance of these two
factors as individual but equally influential elements of proficient reading.

Understanding Adolescent Reading
While these three frameworks are helpful for understanding the components of proficient reading, much
of the science of reading has focused on emerging and developing readers in the primary grades.
For example, while the NRP report highlighted important findings from the field and is still frequently
referenced today, it focused primarily on instruction and outcomes in the early elementary grades.
Since that time, researchers have endeavored to better understand and communicate the science of
teaching adolescent readers and, more specifically, adolescent readers who have not yet developed
reading proficiency.
In a review of the impact of reading interventions on adolescent readers, Edmonds and colleagues
(2009) note that proficient readers engage the following skills while reading: efficient decoding of
unknown words, fluent and accurate reading of text, knowledge of word meanings, connecting new
content to prior schema and knowledge, applying strategic reading comprehension strategies, and
monitoring their understanding while they read. These skills represent the myriad ways in which
the reading process can break down for nonproficient adolescent readers. In fact, the majority of
nonproficient secondary readers will have deficits in more than one subcomponent of reading (Roberts
et al. 2008; Clemens et al. 2017; Biancarosa and Snow 2006).
While research consistently suggests that early intervention is both more efficient and more effective
than intervening in the later grades, there is also evidence that targeted interventions can benefit older
nonproficient readers (Scammacca et al. 2007). The instructional targets for nonproficient secondary
readers, however, will differ slightly from the focus on preventing reading failure in the primary grades.

Instructional Targets for Nonproficient
Secondary Readers
Research on reading interventions for nonproficient secondary readers highlights instructional foci
that differ only slightly from the recommendations of the National Reading Panel. The recommended
instructional targets include multisyllabic word reading, reading fluency, reading comprehension
strategies, vocabulary, and motivation and engagement (Boardman et al. 2008; Roberts et al. 2008;
Vaughn et al. 2022). .

Multisyllabic Word Reading
Often, older students who are not yet reading grade-level text fluently and accurately can decode
single-syllable words but have difficulty decoding multisyllabic words, or words comprised of more than
one syllable (Archer et al. 2003; Vaughn et al. 2022). The majority of words that students will encounter
in the middle and high school classroom are words of two or more syllables (Nagy and Anderson
1984), and the slow, effortful reading of these words represents a serious impediment to overall text
comprehension.

Word study instruction, which focuses on teaching word analysis skills and building morphological
awareness, has been shown to benefit older nonproficient readers (Boardman et al 2008; Scammaca
et al. 2007). Fluency with multisyllabic word reading can improve overall reading fluency and
comprehension, build students’ confidence in reading grade-level texts, and improve motivation and
engagement in reading (Vaughn et al. 2022).
Word analysis instruction typically consists of teaching students syllabication strategies, or strategies
for breaking words into syllables (Honig et al. 2018; Vaughn et al. 2022), then reading the word by
syllables and blending the segments to form a word that is in the student’s oral working vocabulary
(Bhattacharya and Ehri 2004). It is important to ensure that students have adequate monosyllabic
word reading skills, such as mastery of phoneme-grapheme relationships and phonic decoding skills,
before focusing on multisyllabic word reading instruction (Vaughn et al. 2022).
Instruction designed to build students’ morphological awareness focuses on teaching the
pronunciation, orthography, and meaning of morphemes, or the smallest unit of words that hold
meanings, including prefixes, suffixes, and roots (Honig et al. 2018). Explicit instruction designed
to build morphological awareness has been shown to be especially helpful for multilingual learners
(Haager and Osipova 2017; Cárdenas-Hagan 2020) and secondary students with disabilities (Harris et
al. 2011; Roberts et al. 2008).
Word study instruction should teach students to be flexible decoders who are able to apply word
analysis and morphological awareness skills to reading of unknown words and who are also able to
recognize irregular words that do not follow predictable phonological and/or orthographic patterns
(Boardman et al. 2008). Embedding spelling instruction within word study and providing students
with many opportunities to practice word analysis skills during independent reading can increase the
impact of word study instruction (Vaughn et al. 2022).

Fluency
Reading fluency is defined as accurate reading of connected text, at a conversational rate and with
adequate prosody (Hudson et al. 2008) and fluent older readers typically read texts at rates ranging
from 120 to 170 correct words per minute (Hasbrouck and Tindal 2005). Fluent text reading is
strongly correlated with reading comprehension (Torgesen and Hudson 2006). This is in part because
fluent and automatic reading frees up cognitive resources, allowing the reader to devote attention
to meaning-making rather than word reading (Honig et al. 2018). This is especially important for
adolescent readers who are tasked with acquiring a significant amount of content area knowledge
through reading (Boardman et al. 2008).
Nonproficient adolescent readers, including students with reading disabilities, often struggle to read
grade-level text fluently and automatically (Boardman et al. 2008; Meyer and Felton 1999) and when
reading is slow and effortful, working memory is taxed and reading comprehension is compromised
(Perfetti and Lesgold 1977). There is some evidence that instruction targeting reading fluency can
increase oral reading fluency rates, but the degree to which these increases generalize to nonpracticed passages and overall comprehension for older nonproficient readers is mixed. Some
researchers suggest that reading fluency instruction is more effective when it is paired with targeted
word-learning instruction, such as the word study strategies described above (Roberts et al. 2008).

Instructional practices for building reading fluency that are supported by research included repeated
reading (Honig et al. 2018; Therrien 2004; Stevens et al. 2017), partner reading (Honig et al. 2018),
and goal-setting and progress-monitoring procedures that actively involve students (Boardman et
al. 2008), with repeated reading cited as the most promising practice for building fluency. Repeated
reading has been shown to increase both reading rate and reading accuracy for Black middle school
students with disabilities (Paige 2006). Strategies to increase the effectiveness of repeated reading
include establishing a clear purpose for the activity with the student, pre-teaching difficult words,
explicitly teaching students to read with prosody, and checking for understanding after reading (Vaughn
et al. 2022).

Comprehension Strategies
Reading comprehension is a complex, multidimensional endeavor, and proficient adolescent readers
strategically link new learning to existing knowledge, monitor their understanding as they read, and
deploy “fix-up” strategies when reading breaks down. Nonproficient readers, even those with adequate
word reading and fluency skills, often fail to use these strategies (Roberts et al. 2008). Explicit
instruction in reading comprehension strategies can improve students’ strategy use and overall reading
comprehension (Scammacca et al. 2007; Edmonds et al. 2009; Swanson et al. 2017; Sohn et al. 2023).
The following reading comprehension strategies are recommended for explicit instruction (Roberts et
al. 2008; Kamil et al. 2008; Vaughn et al. 2022):
• Activating prior knowledge: As described previously, nonproficient secondary readers develop
less background knowledge than their proficient peers and often fail to access the knowledge they
have while reading. Explicitly teaching students to activate prior knowledge supports meaningmaking by building connections between the known and the new. Educators can build and activate
prior knowledge by providing brief introductions to the topic(s) prior to reading, sharing multimedia
and asking students to notice specific elements of the information presented, and asking targeted
questions.
• Use of graphic organizers to categorize information: Nonproficient secondary readers often
fail to apply strategies to organize new information as they read. The use of graphic organizers
can support students in activating prior knowledge, making connections between key ideas, and
recording important information to support summarizing.
• Comprehension monitoring: Nonproficient secondary readers often fail to notice when
comprehension breaks down while reading, so they are unable to apply “fix-up” strategies.
Teaching students explicit techniques for monitoring comprehension, such as visualizing, asking
questions while reading, pausing periodically to summarize, and re-reading can support overall
comprehension.
• Summarizing: Nonproficient secondary readers often have difficulty synthesizing large amounts
of information into main ideas. Modeling the steps to organizing a summary, teaching students
to use text structures to support summarizing, using metacognitive “think alouds,” and analyzing
examples and non-examples of accurate summaries can support students in developing
independence with summarization.

Research has suggested that, while comprehension strategy instruction must be explicit, direct
instruction can be brief, and instructional routines can quickly shift to focus on both collaborative
and independent application (Vaughn et al. 2022). In fact, some researchers argue that the specific
strategies that are taught are less important than the active engagement of comprehension strategies
generally (Kamil et al. 2008). Further recommendations include embedding comprehension strategy
instruction within multiple content areas, direct and explicit teaching of strategies using a metacognitive
approach, and modeling of strategies accompanied by guided practice opportunities (Swanson et al.
2017; Boardman 2008; Scammacca et al. 2007).

Vocabulary
Vocabulary knowledge is strongly correlated with reading comprehension across grade levels and
reading abilities (Beck et al. 2013). This is particularly true for secondary readers who are tasked with
learning through content-area texts in which specialized vocabulary represents much of the text’s
meaning (Kamil et al. 2008). While students do learn word meanings through direct instruction, the
majority of new word learning in the middle and high school grades occurs through extensive reading
practice across a range of genres (Roberts et al. 2008). Nonproficient adolescent readers tend to
avoid reading and often lack the reading skills necessary for independent engagement with grade-level
texts. This results in fewer opportunities to develop the depth and breadth of vocabulary knowledge
necessary for reading comprehension (Hirsch 2003).
Recommended instructional practices for developing vocabulary included strategic selection of words
for direct instruction, providing student-friendly definitions of target words, multiple exposures to words
over time, providing opportunities for students to actively engage with word meanings, and explicitly
teaching word learning strategies (Boardman et al. 2008; Kamil et al. 2008; Swanson and Wexler
2017).
Beck and colleagues (2013) suggest that educators focus explicit word instruction on “Tier 2 words,”
or those words that students are likely to encounter frequently in text but are unlikely to implicitly learn
via conversation. These words are typically drawn from content-area texts and represent those that
students will encounter frequently in grade-level reading.
Researchers suggest that it can take up to 17 exposures for a student to learn a new word, and
educators should plan for multiple exposures to target words both within a single lesson and over time
(Kamil et al. 2008). Activities to encourage students to actively engage with word meanings include
asking students to distinguish between and identify examples and non-examples of words, to describe
how a target word is related to another known word, and to include target vocabulary words in their
writing (Beck et al. 2013).
Word learning strategies include using context clues, leveraging morphological awareness, and
accessing resources such as online dictionaries (Beck et al. 2013; Vaughn et al. 2013).
Similar to the findings related to fluency instruction, vocabulary instruction has mixed results in
improving overall reading comprehension for nonproficient secondary readers (Scammacca et al.
2007). The most promising avenue to improved vocabulary instruction for older readers is likely to be
“reading a lot, reading well, and reading widely” (Cunningham and Stanovich 1998 as cited in Roberts
et al. 2008).

Motivation and Engagement
Making meaning from complex texts is an active, effortful process that requires the conscious
application of strategies on the part of the reader. Motivated students “usually want to understand text
content fully and therefore, process information deeply. As they read frequently with these cognitive
purposes, motivated students gain in reading comprehension proficiency” (Guthrie et al. as cited in
Roberts 2008, 67). Nonproficient adolescent readers often lack motivation to read (Boardman et al.
2008; Roberts et al. 2008), resulting in a lower volume of reading and fewer opportunities to practice
strategy use and develop the vocabulary and background knowledge necessary to comprehend gradelevel texts.
Evidence-based practices for fostering reading motivation and engagement among nonproficient
adolescent readers include (Kamil et al. 2008; Boardman et al. 2008, Roberts et al. 2008; Biancarosa
and Snow 2006):
• Establishing concrete goals for reading and engaging students in monitoring progress
toward those goals: Studies show that, when educators establish performance goals, emphasize
the learning process, and provide verbal praise based on effort, students are more likely to
develop personal learning goals and are more likely to be motivated and engaged in reading.
• Supporting student autonomy through choice: Offering students choice in both reading
materials and reading activities is associated with greater ownership of learning and overall
engagement.
• Leveraging texts that are aligned with student interests: Students are more likely to persist
in reading texts about topics they are interested in, and they are also more likely to remember
information from these materials. In designing libraries of texts for independent reading, secondary
educators should consider cultivating texts on topics about which students have background
knowledge, texts that students perceive they will be successful at reading, and texts that are
relevant to students’ lived experiences.
• Increasing social interactions related

Cross-Cutting Features of Effective
Secondary Reading Interventions
In addition to a general consensus on the instructional priorities for secondary reading intervention,
research has identified several cross-cutting features of effective approaches to improving reading
outcomes for nonproficient secondary readers.
FIRST, NONPROFICIENT SECONDARY READERS OFTEN NEED SUPPORT IN
DEVELOPING MORE THAN ONE OF THE SUBCOMPONENT SKILLS RELATED TO
PROFICIENT READING.
It is not surprising, then, that interventions that target multiple components tend to be more effective
than those that target a single component of reading (Edmonds et al. 2009). It is particularly important
that educators focus secondary reading interventions on the ultimate goal of reading comprehension,
and word-level or fluency interventions should also integrate reading comprehension instruction
(Edmonds et al. 2009; Sohn et al. 2023).
SECOND, READING INTERVENTION AT THE SECONDARY LEVEL SHOULD BE
CONTENT-EMBEDDED.
This involves both direct teachers of reading (e.g., English Language Arts (ELA) teachers, reading
intervention teachers) providing content-area texts for instruction and independent practice and
content-area teachers reinforcing those reading skills that are most applicable to their subject areas
during instruction (Biancarosa and Snow 2006). Embedding reading instruction within content-area
texts supports generalization, increases student access to reading instruction across the day, and
provides students with more opportunities to practice applying reading skills in a variety of texts.
THIRD, SECONDARY READING INTERVENTIONS ARE MOST EFFECTIVELY
IMPLEMENTED WITH A MULTI-TIERED SYSTEM OF SUPPORT (MTSS) (VAUGHN AND
FLETCHER 2012).
Because secondary nonproficient readers have diverse and complex needs, it is important that
interventions are aligned with students’ identified areas of need (Scammacca et al. 2007; Kamil et al.
2008; Boardman et al. 2008). Categorizing students into subgroups by priority skills for development
is a process that can be achieved via universal screening and follow-up diagnostic assessment for
students identified as nonproficient readers. See the Multi-tiered Systems of Support Literature
Review for a comprehensive review of the literature on MTSS.
FINALLY, ONE CONTRIBUTOR TO POOR READING SKILLS FOR SECONDARY READERS
IS A LACK OF EXPOSURE.
Nonproficient readers tend to engage in a lower volume of reading than proficient readers, meaning

Experiencing Poverty
Black and Latinx students with disabilities experiencing poverty have significantly lower reading
proficiency rates than white students, students without disabilities, and students who are ineligible for
federal free and reduced lunch (Snow and Biancarosa 2003). Factors that explain the link between
socioeconomic status (SES) and reading achievement include both home environmental factors, such
as the home literacy environment, parental stress, and oral language exposure, and school-based
factors, such as the percentage of students reading below grade level, the concentration of students
from low SES backgrounds, and teacher perception, expectations, and knowledge of the language and
literacy skills that contribute to reading development (Aikens and Barbarin 2008; Romeo et al. 2022).
While SES is a strong predictor of reading achievement, it is not predictive of responsiveness
to evidence-based reading interventions, and studies have shown that students from low SES
backgrounds respond at least as favorably to these interventions as students from average to high SES
backgrounds (Morris et al. 2012).

and students who are eligible for remedial services can become competent

There has been long-standing concern in the field of special education that particular subgroups
of students, including Black students, students who are multilingual learners, and students from
households with a low socioeconomic status are disproportionately represented within special
education (Morgan 2020) and that they experience poorer outcomes as a result (Bal and Trainor 2016).
However, more recent analyses have found that Black and Hispanic children are significantly less likely
than otherwise similar white children to receive special education services. Farkas and Morgan (2018)
found that 74% of 4th grade white students with reading difficulties were receiving specialized reading
services through special education, whereas only 44% of Black and 43% of Hispanic students with
reading difficulties received such specialized services. Schools with majority white student populations
have, on average, 3.5 specialists who have been trained in specialized instruction for students with
reading disabilities, compared to schools with student populations that are less than 15% white who
employ just one such specialist on average (Carr 2022). White students are also more likely than Black
students to be diagnosed with dyslexia, resulting in Black students having less access to programs
designed to support their academic success (Farkas and Morgan 2018). Even when students who
are experiencing poverty and students living in households speaking languages other than English
do receive specialized reading services in schools, this instruction tends to rely on the availability of
commercial programs that focus on the procedural subskills that contribute to reading, such as word
reading and reading fluency, at the exclusion of conceptual and knowledge-based skills, such as
vocabulary knowledge and verbal reasoning skills (Lesaux 2012).
This literature review suggests that Black and Latinx students with disabilities experiencing poverty are
likely to experience reading difficulty due to myriad factors and that they are less likely than their white
and higher-income peers to receive quality reading intervention in school. Research suggests that
this population of students does respond favorably when provided with evidence-based reading
interventions and that it is not that the approach must be significantly different, but rather
that the practices that have been shown to be effective for nonproficient secondary readers
must be more consistently available to all students, regardless of socioeconomic status, racial
background, or disability status.

Building Educator Capacity
These recommendations require specialized knowledge and expertise on the part of secondary
educators. In a survey of professional development practices within US schools, Darling-Hammond
and colleagues (2009) found that while most teachers participate in multiple professional development
opportunities each year, nearly half are dissatisfied with these activities. It is not surprising, then,
that secondary educators report feeling underprepared to meet the needs of nonproficient readers
(Kamil et al. 2008; Boardman et al. 2008). Still, other secondary content-area teachers do not feel that
teaching reading is their responsibility (Kamil et al. 2008). Even when educators do receive training in
strategies to support nonproficient readers, they rarely receive ongoing support, resulting in low fidelity
of implementation (James-Burdumy et al. 2009).
Because professional development delivered in a conducive environment has been associated with
improved student outcomes (Wayne et al. 2008; Basma and Savage 2018), schools need to consider
the features of effective professional development. Structurally, professional development activities
should be:
• “intensive, connected to practice, and ongoing,
• directly connected to student learning goals and address the teaching of specific curriculum
content,
• aligned with school improvement goals,
• designed to build strong, professional relationships among teachers, and
• designed to include active learning for teachers” (Kosanovich et al. 2010, 86).
At the secondary level, professional development
should focus on building teacher understanding of
the process of reading comprehension, instructional
routines and approaches that support reading
development, and how students’ cultural and
linguistic backgrounds impact their interactions
with texts (Lupo et al. 2024). When applicable,
professional development should also center the
curricular resources teachers are using in their
classrooms (Hayes-Jacobs and Johnson 2009).
Finally, professional development that occurs
through varied avenues, including coaching,
analysis of student data to inform instruction, and
communities of practice contributes to increased
knowledge and implementation of evidence-based
reading instruction (Connor et al. 2014).

Conclusion
In conclusion, reading proficiency is an essential component of college and career readiness, and
learning demands in the secondary setting require a wide set of complex reading skills. While reading
proficiency rates for secondary students, and in particular Black and Latinx students with disabilities
experiencing poverty, are disappointing, educators and schools interested in improving these outcomes
can be encouraged by the finding that secondary students can benefit from appropriately designed and
implemented reading interventions.
As district officials make program decisions on their approaches to reading intervention, the following
key points from the research can serve as a north star:
• The Simple View of Reading and Scarborough’s Reading Rope provide useful models for
understanding how individuals learn to read. These models can be used to think strategically and
tactically about the literacy programs and interventions needed to support students in becoming
skilled readers.
• Recommended instructional targets for nonproficient secondary readers include multisyllabic word
reading, reading fluency, vocabulary, reading comprehension strategy use, and motivation and
engagement.
• Nonproficient secondary readers tend to have deficits in more than one subcomponent of reading,
and intervention for these students should target multiple components.
• Secondary reading intervention is most effective when implemented in the context of a multi-tiered
system of support and, more specifically, when diagnostic assessment is used to identify students’
specific areas of need and interventions are aligned accordingly.
• Secondary reading intervention is most impactful when it is embedded in content-area instruction
and texts.
• Students need extensive opportunities to practice
reading skills while independently reading connected
texts, and educators should be mindful of providing
both the time and the materials to support independent
reading across the day.
• Secondary teachers feel unprepared to teach
nonproficient secondary readers. Professional
development should serve to build an understanding
of the reading process, increase capacity to design
and deliver evidence-based reading instruction, and
develop an awareness of the ways in which students’
cultural and linguistic backgrounds impact their
interactions with text.

What is the Practice?
Guiding literacy interventions through tiered assessment refers to the practice of identifying secondary
students who are reading below grade-level expectations, pinpointing specific reading skill gaps for
these students, and planning aligned evidence-based interventions to address the identified skill gaps.
The ultimate goal of this tiered assessment process is the delivery of evidence-based reading
interventions aligned with student need to promote accelerated reading achievement for secondary
students who are not yet proficient readers.

Why is Tiered Assessment Important?
Many secondary students, particularly Black and Latinx students with disabilities experiencing poverty,
are not yet proficient readers of grade-level text.1 For students with disabilities, a lack of participation in
grade-level reading instruction contributes to the achievement gap between students with disabilities
and their typically developing peers.2
Secondary readers have diverse reading profiles and the degree and nature of support can vary greatly
among students; however, interventions at the secondary level have historically reflected a “one-sizefits-all approach” to limited success. Further, commonly administered assessments of reading at the
secondary level fail to pinpoint specific skill gaps for individual readers.
Using tiered assessments that include both universal screening and diagnostic assessments allows
educators to identify individual reading needs and deliver precisely planned reading interventions to
accelerate reading progress.

Bringing this Practice to Life
BUILDING A TIERED ASSESSMENT APPROACH AT GREEN DOT
CALIFORNIA PUBLIC SCHOOLS
Green Dot Public Schools has a long history of providing reading intervention to students who enter their
schools not yet reading at grade level. For many years, the network used one assessment, the Reading
Inventory, to identify students to be programmed into just one intervention, Read 180. While Read 180 met
the needs of many students, other students participated in Read 180 for multiple years and continued to
read below grade-level benchmarks.
Green Dot did not have a clear way of understanding why these students weren’t comprehending gradelevel text, even after multiple years of receiving interventions targeted at reading comprehension. Was it
because the students did not have the phonics skills to read the words on the page? Perhaps the students
could read the individual words, but reading was effortful and slow, leaving reduced cognitive capacity to
think about meaning? Or could it be that some students could read the words accurately and fluently, yet
had language gaps that were impeding their ability to make meaning from the text? Green Dot needed a
way to better understand these students’ reading needs so that they could refine their reading intervention
offerings with the goal of accelerating reading growth for all.

What is a Curriculum-Based Measurement for Reading?
An Oral Reading Fluency Curriculum-Based Measurement (ORF CBM) is a one-minute timed
assessment of the rate and accuracy of a student’s oral reading fluency. ORF CBMs are
administered in a 1:1 setting during which the student reads aloud for one minute from a leveled
passage while an adult listens and scores, noting errors and the total number of words the student
read in one minute. The administrator of the assessment calculates both accuracy (the percentage
of the total words read that were read correctly) and rate (the total number of words read
correctly, typically expressed as “correct words per minute,” or CWPM). ORF CBMs are one of the
strongest predictors of a student’s overall reading performance4 and are widely used as screening,
diagnostic, and progress monitoring tools. Common commercial publishers of ORF CBMs include
DIBELS, Acadience, AIMSweb, and FastBridge.

As soon as Green Dot designed decision-making criteria to guide the analysis of universal
screening data, they realized that it would be important to establish similar criteria to guide
the analysis of the diagnostic ORF CBM data. The criteria could then be used efficiently either
to organize students into specific interventions or, for the students with the most significant
literacy needs, to conduct additional diagnostic assessments to pinpoint specific needs. After
identifying reading needs in phonics and fluency via the diagnostic assessment, Green Dot added
interventions targeting these two areas.

Step 1: Vet and Select a Universal Screener
and Diagnostic Assessment Tools
The Center on Multi-Tiered System of Supports recommends a three-step process for designing a
screening assessment:
• Clarify the purpose of screening.
• Assess needs, priorities, and logistics.
• Select screening tools.5
See the Further Learning section of this chapter for a detailed guide with questions to direct
conversations at each step of the screening design process.
The usefulness of universal screening data for the purpose of instructional planning is highly dependent
on the validity and reliability of the assessment for the school population. When evaluating potential
screeners, schools should consider factors related both to the quality of the assessment and to the
school’s specific context.
THE FOLLOWING QUESTIONS CAN BE USED TO CONSIDER AN ASSESSMENT’S QUALITY:
• Classification accuracy: Do scores on this assessment accurately identify students reading
below grade level?
• Validity: Does this assessment measure what it is supposed to measure?
• Reliability: Are the scores on this assessment consistent with other measures of the same skills?
• Statistical bias: Is this assessment biased against any subgroups of students?
• Sample representativeness: Is the normative sample for this assessment representative of my
school’s target population?
See Further Learning for a resource from the National Center on Intensive Intervention that
provides additional guidance for determining validity and reliability of screening assessments.

Step 3: Plan Structures to Support Data-Based Decision-Making
BUILD THE TEAM.
Designate the team that will be involved in analyzing the screening and diagnostic data. The team should
be composed of individuals who are familiar with both the data yielded by the assessments and the
decision-making criteria (see Establish Decision-Making Criteria below), and who are responsible for
implementing the instructional next steps following the screener. This might include special education
leadership, school leaders, ELA instructional coaches, counselors, and/or reading intervention teachers.
Schedule team data meetings to occur immediately following each screener administration (1–3 times
yearly) and calendar the meetings on the school and/or district calendar.
TRAIN STAFF.
Provide training of staff members responsible for (1) administering the assessments and (2) analyzing
the results to support intervention planning and programming decisions. Consider how to onboard staff
members who miss the initial training. Many assessment platforms provide options for low-cost virtual
training in the administration of the assessment and interpretation of results. Particularly for diagnostic
assessments that are administered in a 1:1 format, providing opportunities for teachers to practice
administering and scoring the assessments during the training can support implementation fidelity.
ESTABLISH DECISION-MAKING CRITERIA.
Establish the cut points or decision-making criteria that will be used to classify students as reading within
grade-level expectations, slightly below grade-level expectations, and significantly below grade-level
expectations. Plan the response for students in each group. Decision-making criteria should include:
• A score above which students are likely reading within grade-level expectations and below which
students are likely reading below grade-level expectations. This score will represent the first “sort”
of students into within-grade-level and below-grade-level groups.
• The next step for students scoring below this cut point. This will likely involve a specific intervention
or further diagnostic assessment to pinpoint specific skill gaps.
• Criteria for sorting based on diagnostic results. See the case study for an example of decisionmaking criteria applied to ORF CBM diagnostic data and the Further Learning section of this
chapter for a detailed guide to establishing decision rules published by the North Carolina
Department of Public Instruction.

How do you analyze the diagnostic data
to determine specific reading needs?
At Uncommon Schools, teachers participating in Marshall Street’s Networked
Improvement Community (NIC) are working to improve the use of tiered assessment
data to plan effective interventions. In an early coaching conversation, Val, a high school
literacy innovation specialist at Uncommon Schools, worked with Genevieve Thomas, a
literacy content area expert, to collaboratively analyze the results of an oral reading fluency
curriculum-based measurement that Val had administered to the students in her reading
intervention class.
1. Are students reading fluently and accurately?
Val and Genevieve first looked to see if there were students who were reading both
fluently and accurately. Fluently meant reading an eighth-grade passage at a rate of at
least 120 CWPM. Accurately meant with at least 95% accuracy.
The data showed that no students met this criterion, yet some students were reading
accurately (95%+) but slowly (fewer than 100 CWPM). Additionally, some students
were reading both inaccurately and slowly.
2. What do word reading errors indicate?
Val and Genevieve then reviewed the individual assessments of the students who were
reading both slowly and inaccurately to determine what type of word reading errors the
students were presenting. Together, Val and Genevieve determined that most students
seemed to have basic word reading skills, but many struggled to read multisyllabic
words fluently and automatically.
Based on this conclusion, Val and Genevieve focused on building word study activities
for these students. (See Developing Multisyllabic Word Reading Skills in Adolescent
Readers.)

with Identified Skill Gaps
In phase III, school teams analyze screening and diagnostic data in order to align
evidence-based reading interventions with identified skill gaps for each student reading
below grade level.

In preparation for phase III, school leadership will want to plan and prepare for the team
review meetings at the start of the year or start of the process if beginning midyear.
Early preparation will allow the team collaboration during data review and intervention
planning to be timely and effective for team discussion and aligned action.
The following steps are not required when implementing phase II, yet they are considered highly beneficial
to support the process:
• Develop the protocol or agenda for the data meeting, including the specific questions the team
will need to answer when they meet. Create space in the meeting protocols for the team to clearly
document instruction and intervention decisions for at least the students in the “some” and “significant”
risk categories. Make this information accessible for key stakeholders (for example, stored in a shared
Google Drive).
• Identify the reports that will facilitate efficient decision-making and allow the team to answer the
questions of focus for the meeting. Most often, these will be pre-built reports in assessment platforms,
but may include other spreadsheets, trackers, or data dashboards.
• Identify the team members who will convene for the meeting. This may be a new structure that
requires scheduling and communication of purpose, or it may be situated in an existing meeting where
the team has agreed on the use of the time for this purpose.
• Inventory evidence-based interventions currently available on campus and the number of students that
can be served in each intervention slot or block. Consider how the team might plan for supplementing
as needed for gaps in skills addressed or spaces available for students. The following resources may
be helpful for researching evidence-based literacy interventions for secondary students:
– Evidence for ESSA
– The What Works Clearinghouse
– The National Center on Intensive Intervention’s Academic Intervention Tools Chart

– who will monitor assessment completion rates and follow up with make-up assessment needs,
– who will gather and prepare the data for the team to review,
– who will lead the meeting and prepare the agenda,

In preparation for data review meetings, a team or data leader gathers and audits assessment data to
ensure it is ready for team review and action planning. This includes:
• confirming there are universal screening scores for students (i.e., most or all students have
completed the screening, and make-ups have been attempted),
• following up on any errors or warnings on data quality, such as student scores that may not be
valid due to quick completion times, and
• cleaning data in preparation for reports to be run (e.g., removing duplicate scores or removing
students who have unenrolled).
Once the data is scrubbed as best as possible, assessment reports are generated using automated
tools or custom-built resources. Often reports can be run via the universal screening platform (e.g., a
report with student scores by grade level, sorted from high to low scores), while data from diagnostic
assessments require team-created tools or resources (e.g., a spreadsheet with rate and accuracy
scores from a curriculum-based measurement).
There are many ways to represent and prepare the data. A few helpful considerations include the
following:
• Apply cut points to the data to identify students who scored below established targets.
• Sort data by grade level or classroom.
• Filter data by subgroups (e.g., students with disabilities, multilingual learners, students who are
experiencing homelessness) to better understand performance strengths or gaps.

Step 2: Review Universal Screening Data
Literacy intervention planning begins by reviewing universal screening data. The focus of the team
will depend on the goals and priorities of an individual school or network, but may serve to answer the
following questions:
• Which students are reading below grade-level expectations?
• What percentage of our total student population does this represent?
• What implications do these numbers have for our tiers of support?
If administering the universal screening assessment multiple times yearly, use the screening data to
evaluate student growth. Consider the following questions as guidance for middle- and end-of-year data
meetings:
• What progress have students receiving supplemental interventions made, and how does that
compare to the general population of students? Does it represent accelerated, “gap-closing” growth?
• What is the performance and progress of disaggregated subgroups of students, and how does
this compare to the general population? For example, do students with disabilities grow at rates
commensurate with the general student population?

Summary
As a result of historically ineffective reading instruction across US K–12 schools, an alarming number
of secondary students are not proficient readers, and Black and Latinx students with disabilities
experiencing poverty are disproportionately represented within this population. Because low reading
skills are a barrier to accessing grade-level content and standards, this reality results in inequitable
access to the rigorous academic content students need to engage with in order to be prepared for
postsecondary success.
Multiple root causes contribute to low literacy rates for this population, and while evidence does
suggest that instruction can develop reading skills in older readers, reading interventions are most
impactful when they are aligned with individual student needs. Universal screening of reading ability
helps identify which students are reading below grade level. Additional diagnostic assessment is
necessary to illuminate why these students are not yet reading proficiently. This tiered approach to
reading assessment supports schools and networks to effectively and efficiently provide aligned and
evidence-based reading interventions at the secondary level.

Reading fluency is generally

Reading fluency is made up of three

Why is Repeated Reading Important?
Repeated reading directly builds oral reading fluency skills, which has the potential to unlock access
to grade level content for students. Fluent, accurate, and automatic word reading is strongly correlated
with reading comprehension, or the ability to make meaning from text, which is the ultimate goal of
reading.2 This is in part because, when readers process connected text smoothly and automatically,
cognitive resources such as working memory are freed up to devote attention to comprehension rather
than to lifting the words off the page.3 Conversely, the difficult work of slow, effortful decoding taxes a
reader’s working memory capacity, diminishing their ability to understand the content.4
Because of this, secondary students who are not yet reading text fluently and automatically often have
difficulty engaging with the rigor required to meet secondary ELA standards. What’s more, students
with reading disabilities are more at risk for presenting with difficulties in reading fluency,5 and the
subsequent lack of participation in grade-level reading instruction can contribute to the achievement
gap between students with disabilities and their typically developing peers.6
Repeated reading is one of the most studied and validated methods for increasing reading fluency and
overall reading proficiency. The application of this practice to authentic, grade-level texts — whether
through a partner reading protocol or a 1:1 teacher-directed protocol — has strong potential to increase
access to and comprehension of grade-level texts for students with and without disabilities.

Why It Works
READING FLUENCY IS CORRELATED WITH
READING COMPREHENSION.
Oral reading fluency has been consistently shown
to correlate with students’ ability to comprehend
both simple and complex texts at all levels.7
READING FLUENCY CAN BE POSITIVELY

Encouragingly, reading fluency has consistently

REPEATED ORAL READING
IS HIGHLY EFFECTIVE.
Repeated reading interventions for students with learning disabilities are associated with improvements
in reading rate, accuracy, and comprehension.9 A recent review of the literature suggests that repeated
reading is the most effective intervention for improving reading fluency in students with disabilities.10
This is not only true in the primary grades. While many studies of foundational reading instruction
do focus on the primary grades, repeated reading has been shown to have a positive impact on oral
reading fluency specifically with secondary students.11

Bringing this Practice to Life
INTEGRATING REPEATED READING INTO CONTENT COURSES
AT NOBLE SCHOOLS
Noble Schools, a public charter network in Chicago, IL, set out to dramatically improve student reading
skills across their high school campuses. Noble teachers collaborated with the Networked Improvement
Community (NIC) professional learning community to learn and apply evidence-based reading interventions
in core classes to support students to learn skills to improve their reading while learning course content. The
following case study highlights the efforts of one co-teaching pair at Chicago Bulls College Prep to embed
the repeated reading practice in their World History course.

Each day at Chicago Bulls College Prep, a school within the Noble Schools network, Megan, a
learning specialist, welcomes 10th grade students to their co-taught World History class. She
previews the topic of the day’s reading and reminds students of the protocol for repeated partner
reading. She thanks several students by name for their attention and preparation, while Derek, a
content area specialist, quickly reassigns students whose partners are absent.
Megan prompts the students who will read first to raise their hands, and a calm settles over the
classroom. When she announces the starting commands — “Ready...Set...Go!” — a cacophony
of reading voices fills the room. One hundred percent of student eyes are glued to the text, which
is connected thematically to the current unit. Students are either reading the passage aloud or
monitoring while their partners read. After one minute, students provide feedback to their partners
(skipped or misread words, for example), calculate and record a rate and accuracy score, and
switch roles. The entire procedure is wrapped up in eight minutes, after which Megan and Derek
transition to the content-focused objectives for the day’s lesson.

Step 5: Create a Plan to Explicitly Build Student
Motivation and Engagement
Secondary learners who are not yet reading proficiently have often established a self-concept of
being incapable readers. This low sense of self-efficacy can manifest as reluctance to participate in
instructional activities with which these students have not historically been successful. To proactively
address this, educators can create a plan for sharing the purpose and process of the oral reading
fluency intervention with students, along with the rationale for their assignment to the intervention. This
can be done as a whole class, in small groups, or one-on-one with students.
When first introducing the intervention to students, leverage the theory of malleable intelligence to
explain how practicing reading can build their reading brains and ultimately make reading a less
effortful, more productive, and more enjoyable experience. As part of this process, ask students to set
longer-term goals for themselves as readers, such as goals for the school year, for their educational
career, or for life. This can help them visualize their long-term goals and connect the short-term
intervention to their bigger picture.
Student conferencing during the intervention launch or during progress monitoring activities also
helps build motivation and engagement and can involve students in goal-setting activities. Educators
can use these 1:1 conferences to share baseline data with students to support them in developing
goals for progress, and later to train students to reflect on their goals and progress over time. (See
Strengthening Timely Interventions through Goal Setting and Progress Monitoring for more
guidance on student conferencing).

Put Repeated Reading into Practice
Research suggests that fluency protocols, whether conducted with an educator or with a peer
partner, can positively impact student reading, even when delivered in short increments of 5–10
minutes. Protocols should be implemented at least three days per week. A printable protocol for
repeated reading can be found in the Appendix.

Tips and Tools to Support Oral Reading Fluency with Students
Sometimes students need additional coaching, or tools, to support their reading tracking or selfmonitoring while reading. There are many useful strategies and tools educators can use with students.
A few common strategies include:
• Coaching for visual tracking: Prompt students to use a finger or pencil to point to the words and
track the text as they read, or use a sticky note or index card to help them build skills for reading
line over line.
• Accommodating visual tracking: Provide enlarged text and spacing as an accessibility
accommodation for students with visual tracking difficulties.
• Prompting to use strategies: Secondary students often have habits and routines as readers they
will need to learn or unlearn around reading. Teachers can remind and encourage students to use
their resources (e.g., error correction prompts) and strategies (e.g., re-reading text after correcting
errors) to build their reading habits and muscle.
• Providing tools: Calculators to compute reading rate and accuracy or timers for peer-partner
coaching use are both valuable tools for developing student skill and engagement.

Monitor the Effectiveness of the Intervention
Step 9: Analyze Student Progress
The ultimate goal of repeated reading practice is to improve student fluency and automaticity to
facilitate reading comprehension. Monitor the effectiveness and impact of the oral reading practice
by regularly collecting rate and accuracy data, as well as information about student prosody. After
6–8 weeks of practice, gather the data for review by individual educators or teams. To understand the
broader trends in progress and the impact of the practice on other metrics, teams can analyze student
progress on global reading measures, such as ELA assessments or benchmark reading assessments,
alongside students’ fluency data. For additional perspective, consider collecting and reviewing student
feedback, including student perception of the intervention’s impact on their skill development and
confidence as readers, so student insights and needs are part of the data discussion.
Use the data gathered to adjust the approach to implementation and to guide additional staff training
aligned to student and educator needs. Ongoing professional development for teachers might focus on
using student fluency data to inform additional instruction and intervention planning, working around
barriers to implementation, and leveling up the practice as routines and procedures are internalized.

BUILDING ORAL READING FLUENCY IN SECONDARY READERS

Summary
English Language Arts standards for secondary students ask readers to engage in deep meaningmaking work with complex texts. Still, many students enter our middle and high schools not having
had the opportunity to develop the foundational reading skills necessary to engage in this work.
This problem is particularly pronounced for Black and Latinx students, students experiencing
poverty, and students with disabilities. Many of these readers are able to apply phonics decoding
skills to sound out words, yet reading grade-level connected text is a slow, laborious process that
commandeers much of their working memory capacity while reading, leaving little availability for
meaning-making processes.
Encouragingly, decades of research suggest that explicit strategies to develop reading fluency —
including repeated reading, partner reading, and goal setting with progress monitoring — positively
impact reading fluency, which in turn leads to improved reading comprehension. These evidencebased practices are a critical component of a comprehensive plan for reading intervention in middle
and high schools. Implementing a structured protocol for reading fluency has strong potential to
improve student proficiency and automaticity in reading, ultimately providing greater and more
equitable access to grade-level instruction and rich, complex texts for all students.

PRACTICE TOOL

Repeated Reading Protocol
Spark Educational Consulting 3-Day Fluency Instruction Task Card
Day 1 (~10 minutes)
1. Students conduct “cold read,” calculate rate, and graph progress.
– Depending on group size, options include: the teacher times the student, a partner times the student,
or the student times her/himself.
2. The teacher previews and model reads the passage.
– Prompt the students to listen for expression and prosody — e.g.: “Good readers do not read word by
word with one tone of voice, like a robot. They read with expression and in phrases, or meaningful
chunks of words. As I read this passage, notice how I read in phrases, how I vary my tone, and how I
pause at the end of sentences.”
– Model making a prediction for reading based on a preview of the passage — e.g. “Previewing before I
start reading can help me better understand what I’m reading. This passage is about...I wonder if I will
learn about...”
3. Ask students to share what they noticed about the reading, and/or what they learned from the text.
4. Chorally read the passage. Explicitly notice and comment on expressive and prosodic reading.

Day 2 (~10 minutes)
1. Provide a review of the passage and reinforce the “why” — good readers read accurately, quickly, and with
expression, and fluent readers are better able to pay attention to and understand what they are reading.
2. Echo read the passage.
– The teacher reads a sentence, modeling expression and prosody, and students chorally read (“echo”)
the sentence. Repeat for the full passage.
3. Pair students for partner reading of the passage.
– Students will either be the reader or the coach.
– The job of the reader is to read accurately, quickly, and with good expression.
– The job of the coach is to listen for the reader’s mistakes, help with hard words, and give feedback.
Types of mistakes and corrective feedback include:
• Mistake: Saying the wrong word; Correction: Point to the word, provide the correction, and ask the
reader to read the sentence again.
• Mistake: Getting stuck on a word (more than ~3 seconds); Correction: point to the word, say the
word, ask the reader to say the word, and ask the reader to reread the sentence.
• Mistake: Skipping a word; Correction: Tell the reader a word was skipped and ask the reader to reread the sentence.
• Mistake: Adding an extra word; Correction: Tell the reader a word was added and ask the reader to
re-read the sentence.
• Students read the passage through one time, switch roles and read again.

Day 3 (~10 minutes)
1. Provide a review of the passage and reinforce the “why” — good readers read accurately, quickly, and with

5.3

Key Terms for this Practice
Syllabication and morphological awareness are two
important skills that proficient and fluent readers apply
in order to decode unknown multisyllabic words.
Syllabication refers to dividing multisyllabic words into
individual syllables, each of which contains one vowel
sound. Syllable division principles generalize how and
where to break a multisyllabic word into syllables. See
How To: Teach Syllabication for more.
Morphological awareness refers to an understanding
of how morphemes — that is, the smallest meaningful
parts of words, like prefixes, suffixes, and roots —
contribute to word meaning. See How To: Teach
Morphological Awareness for more.

Why is this Practice Important?
Often, older students who are not yet reading grade-level texts fluently and accurately can decode
single-syllable words but have difficulty decoding multisyllabic words.1 Because the majority of words
that secondary students encounter in grade-level texts are multisyllabic, slow and effortful reading of
these words is a significant impediment to comprehension of the overall text. This barrier to meaningful
participation in grade-level instruction grounded in complex texts contributes to the achievement gap
between students with disabilities and their typically developing peers.2
Word study instruction, or instruction in word analysis and word recognition, is often recommended
as an essential focus of instruction for secondary students with reading disabilities.3 Word study
intervention can improve reading outcomes for struggling students by teaching them to be flexible
decoders and to access word analysis and word recognition strategies.4 Explicit instruction designed to
build morphological awareness has been shown to be especially helpful for English language learners5
and secondary students with disabilities.6

Bringing this Practice to Life
HOW UNCOMMON USED WORD STUDY TO BUILD READING FLUENCY
At Uncommon Charter High School (UCHS) in Brooklyn, New York, Literacy Innovation Specialist Val Dutta
co-teaches Academic Prep with Giovanni (Gio) Zaccheo, a UCHS special education teacher and department
chair. Academic Prep is a reading intervention class for 9th grade students with reading disabilities designed
to build the foundational reading skills necessary to facilitate meaningful engagement with grade-level
content and texts. The case study below opens with a scene describing a typical interaction in Academic
Prep, where word study has become integral to reading instruction.

Uncovering a Need
Chance, a 9th grade student, is reading aloud a passage about social activism:
CHANCE: Social activism refers to the use of... dee... del... delibate?
GIO: [listening carefully, prompts Chance] Let’s pause and break this down. How many
consonants are between the two vowels?
Gio and Chance work to break the word deliberate into syllables, and soon the text Chance is
reading has been marked up with divisions: de.lib.er.ate. They continue:
GIO: Let’s read the word by syllables. Then try for the real word and be careful, there are a
few schwas in this one.

This interaction represents the instruction that Chance and his Academic Prep classmates are

It all began at the start of the 2022–23 school year when Val administered an Oral Reading
Fluency Curriculum-Based Measurement (ORF CBM) to the students enrolled in Academic
Prep to more deeply understand their reading skills and needs. She then met with the Networked
Improvement Community literacy content expert, Genevieve Thomas, to analyze the results.
Genevieve and Val noticed that students were largely successful with single-syllable and highfrequency words, and the vast majority of errors on the ORF CBM were made with multisyllabic
words. Students didn’t seem to have a strategy to attack these longer words and the “slow down”
caused by these inefficiencies was interfering with their ability to read the passage fluently. Val and
Genevieve acknowledged that, if students were encountering these roadblocks when reading a
relatively short, isolated passage, they were almost certainly facing even more challenges with the
longer and more complex texts they were seeing in their content area classes — challenges that
would prevent a cohesive understanding of the ideas in the text. It was clear that building proficiency
with multisyllabic word reading would need to be a key goal of instruction in Academic Prep.
Val and Gio decided to focus first on explicitly teaching syllabication — that is, the principles for
where to divide a multisyllabic word into syllables — starting with basics and, over the course of
several weeks, moving from less to more complex concepts. They worked with Genevieve to develop
a progression of instructional targets. (See How To: Teach Syllabication below for a detailed
instructional progression.)

Syllabication Basics
Open syllables end in a vowel sound,
and the vowel sound is long (like the
sound of a in the first syllable of paper:
[pā]). Closed syllables end in one or
more consonants, and the vowel sound
is short (like the sound of a in the first
syllable of rabbit: [rӑb]).
Open and closed syllables comprise
almost 75 percent of English syllables
and thus comprise “the building blocks of
polysyllabic decoding.”7

How to Implement this Practice
Lay the Groundwork
Step 1: Identify Students Likely to Benefit from Interventions
in Multisyllabic Word Reading
Use data from universal screening and diagnostic assessments to identify students who are likely to
benefit from intervention in multisyllabic word reading (see Guiding Literacy Interventions Through
Tiered Assessment for more guidance).
Identify students for whom a combination of the following statements are true:
• They perform poorly on grade-level assessments of reading comprehension.
• They read grade-level texts slowly (below a rate of 120 correct words per minute) and/or
inaccurately (below an accuracy rate of 95%).
• They have mastered single-syllable word reading (i.e., these students know the relationships
between letters and sounds and are able to apply this knowledge to read isolated single-syllable
words).
• They have difficulty reading words with more than one syllable fluently and automatically.

Step 2: Select or Design a Systematic Scope and Sequence
for Teaching Syllabication Skills
Advanced planning is required to select or design a systematic scope and sequence for teaching
syllabication skills. Instruction should progress from less to more complex concepts, generally starting
with teaching open and closed syllables and then teaching syllabication strategies. See How To: Teach
Syllabication below for a recommended scope and sequence of instruction and Further Learning for
additional instructional guidance.

Step 3: Determine the Setting for the Intervention
Determine the setting in which to implement the practice. Potential settings include the following:
• Within a scheduled reading intervention class (e.g., Read 180) for students who are not yet
reading at grade level
• In 1:1 conferences or in small groups of students with reading disabilities within a resource room,
learning center, or other environment dedicated to specialized instruction
• In core content classes, like science and social studies, using vocabulary words from the course

PROS AND CONS OF CURRICULUM VS. STRATEGIES
Some schools may decide to utilize a structured curriculum to build students’
multisyllabic word reading skills, rather than designing their own. When using a
curriculum, educators will sometimes need to adapt or supplement the curriculum
to meet the needs of students.

Plan the Intervention
Step 4: Identify Anchor Texts to Leverage for Instruction
Leveraging grade-level ELA or content area texts for word study instruction can support
comprehension of grade-level material, improve students’ ability to transfer syllabication skills and
morphological knowledge to unique texts, and support motivation to read increasingly complex texts.
Choose an anchor text (for example, an article related to a topic of instruction in a World History class
or a passage from a novel in an ELA class) from which target words for word study instruction will
be selected.

Step 5: Prepare for Daily Lessons
Carefully select, in advance, words from the selected anchor text. Ideally, after initial syllabication
strategies have been explicitly taught, students practice syllabication and morphological awareness —
explained further in the How To sections below — with three to five words daily. Consider the following
guidance when selecting words for instruction:
• Choose words that align with the specific syllabication strategies that have already been taught.
For example, if students have learned only one pattern for syllabication division, choose only
words that include that pattern.
• Once students have learned all of the syllabication division rules, choose words with different
patterns to provide practice with a variety of syllabication strategies.
• When initially teaching syllabication division rules, choose words that “follow the rules.” Once
students become more proficient with the division principles, introduce words that are “rule
breakers” (e.g., words that include a schwa).
• For teaching morphological awareness, choose words that include affixes that have high
instructional value. Consider using vocabulary words for syllabication and morphology work. For
example, if autocracy were a vocabulary word in a World History class, the teacher might have
students first practice syllabication — by dividing the word into syllables, reading the word by
syllables, then reading the full word — and then teach them the meaning of the prefix auto-.
When planning for daily lessons, practice syllabication of the target words to identify any potential
misunderstandings and prepare scaffolds and explanations. If teaching a specific prefix, suffix, or root
that has meaning, craft the language to explain the meaning (e.g., “The affix auto means self.”)

Implement the Intervention
Step 6: Introduce the Purpose of Multisyllabic Word Work
to Students
Explain to students that it can be challenging to read long words that are unfamiliar, especially when
taking on the entire word at once. However, when readers have strategies for breaking words apart,
they can more easily read those smaller “word parts” and then put them together to read the whole
word. Introduce the idea that many word parts appear frequently in text, so becoming fluent with those
commonly occurring word parts (or morphemes) can help build reading fluency.
A teacher might use the following sample script to introduce word study when taking the strategies
approach described above:
We’re starting a new practice — breaking words into syllables. We’ll learn rules for how to break big
words into smaller parts so we can tackle the words part-by-part.
We will practice breaking words apart three times a week using words from the stories we are
reading in class. The point of this is to help us become stronger readers and to help us better
understand what we are reading. Often, the first time we try to read a big word that we haven’t seen
before, we aren’t quite sure how to read it correctly. We spend a lot of our brain’s energy trying to
read those words, and we don’t have much energy left over to think about what the words mean.
I know that’s true for me when I come across complex words in my reading. If we have strategies
to break apart those big words, it’s a lot easier to read the small parts and then put them together
to read the whole word. There are also lots of word parts that show up over and over in lots of the
words we read. When we practice reading those common word parts, it becomes easier and more
automatic to recognize them even in big words.

Step 7: Deliver the Word Study Intervention
Using the chosen vocabulary words and selected texts,
deliver the word study intervention. Both syllabication
and morphology strategies should be employed in this
step, though the depth of focus on each skill may vary in
lessons. This step is broken into detailed explanations
for How To: Teach Syllabication and How To: Teach
Morphological Awareness.
Research suggests that word study instruction can
positively impact student reading, even in short
increments of 5–10 minutes daily. Explicit instruction
should be provided at least three days per week.

How To: Teach Syllabication
TEACH STUDENTS HOW TO READ OPEN AND CLOSED SYLLABLES.
Follow this sequence for teaching open and closed syllables:
1. Explain that a syllable is a smaller part within a larger word and each syllable contains
one vowel sound.
2. Write a closed syllable (e.g., [man]) on a board and ask students to read it.
3. Explain that [man] ends in a consonant, which makes it a closed syllable. Closed
syllables always end in a consonant, and the vowel sound in a closed syllable is always
short.
4. Write 3–5 more closed syllables on a board and ask students to read them. Point out
that each syllable ends in one or more consonants, and that the vowel sound in each
syllable is short.
5. Write an open syllable (e.g., [pā] as in paper) on the board. Tell the students that the
syllable is [pā] and ask them to read the syllable. Explain that [pā] ends in a vowel,
which makes it an open syllable. Open syllables always end in a vowel, and the vowel
sound in an open syllable is always long.
6. Write 3–5 more open syllables on a board and ask students to read them. Point out that
each syllable ends in a vowel, and that the vowel sound in each open syllable is long.
After teaching students the generalization, provide daily opportunities to practice reading
isolated open and closed syllables until students consistently read both syllable types
accurately. This can be a brief warm-up before engaging in syllabication division work.
Note that students are often more proficient in reading closed syllables and need more
practice reading open syllables. Some students may need to be retaught short and long
vowel sounds before they are ready for open- and closed-syllable instruction.

TEACH STUDENTS TO BE FLEXIBLE DECODERS.
Once students are proficient with the rules for dividing and reading multisyllabic words,
teach students the concept of “flexible decoding.”
Explain that the syllabication division rules will help them read many words, but in some
cases, it won’t lead to the exact pronunciation of a word. In such cases, readers need to
be flexible and think about whether the unfamiliar word sounds like a word they’ve heard
before. This is because English can be a complicated language and it doesn’t always follow
the rules.
Provide an example, sharing your thinking aloud. For example, a teacher might write the
word classify on a board, and use the following script:
Let’s look at an example of a word that doesn’t follow the rules. Now, you may know this
word, but remember that our syllabication rules help us read words we don’t know, so
I’m going to pretend I don’t know this one. Following my syllabication rules, I’m going
to divide this word like this: clas.si.fy. Based on my open and closed syllables, I would
read these syllables as [clăs-sī-fī]. Hmm... does that sound like a word I know? Oh,
it sounds like [clăs.sə.fī]! Breaking this word into syllables didn’t get me to the exact
pronunciation, but it got me close enough that I could match it to a word that I know and
that makes sense.
One reason the pronunciation of a multisyllabic word may not match its syllable-by-syllable
pronunciation is that it has a schwa. A schwa is a vowel sound that is distorted in the
unstressed syllable (such as the second syllable in the classify example above), and the
schwa occurs frequently in multisyllabic words. Teach students the definition of schwa and
include words with a schwa in practice.

How To: Teach Morphological Awareness
Morphemes are the smallest meaningful parts of words, like prefixes, suffixes, and roots.
Instruction in morphology should co-occur with syllabication instruction. When words
selected for syllabication practice include commonly occurring morphemes, use the
following process to build students’ morphological awareness (see Further Learning for a
resource listing commonly occurring morphemes):
1. Teach students the meaning and pronunciation of commonly occurring morphemes.
Focus on one morpheme at a time.
Isolate the morpheme from the full word and teach students its pronunciation and, if
applicable, its meaning. For example:
Today we are going to learn the word part rupt, which means “to break.”
Ask students to read the morpheme rupt.
2. Ask students to read a word that includes the morpheme by syllables then read the full
word.
Let’s read a word that has this word part in it.
Ask students to read bank.rupt by syllables.
3. If it is a morpheme that holds meaning, engage students in a conversation about what
the full word might mean based on the meaning of the morpheme.
If rupt means “to break,” what might bankrupt mean?
4. Provide students with 3–5 more words that include the target morpheme, and instruct
them to read by syllables, read the full word, and discuss.

Step 8: Provide Ongoing Opportunities for Practice
Once the discrete multisyllabic word reading skills have been taught, provide students with many
opportunities to practice applying the skills with new and authentic words. This is important whether
students have been taught syllabication using anchor texts or a structured, commercially available
program. Students should be explicitly encouraged to apply these skills not only during multisyllabic
word reading instruction but also in their independent reading when they encounter a multisyllabic word
they don’t know.

GREEN DOT’S WILSON INTERVENTION
At Ánimo Mae Jemison, a Green Dot Public Schools California middle school, students with disabilities with
the most intensive reading needs are scheduled into an Academic Prep course where they receive small
group instruction using the Wilson Reading System (WRS). The implementation of the WRS is enhanced by
Green Dot’s deep commitment to the program — shown in the collaboration between teachers, counselors,
and school leaders to prioritize the needs of this group of students — and by the supportive atmosphere
they have built around it. The case study below describes how Green Dot students work with the classroom
teacher or trained paraeducator to build word reading skills using the structured literacy program and
accompanying materials.

On a typical day, seven to nine students enter the reading intervention classroom, retrieve their
binders from a bookshelf, and sit down around one of two kidney-shaped tables. Looking to the
front of the room, students greet their teacher, Whitney, as they open their workbooks to the day’s
lesson warm-up activity.
The warm-up begins with a word study drill for students to break words into syllables and mark
known sounds and word parts (e.g., suffixes) following a routine they’ve been taught. They place
slash marks between the syllables within each word and use a coding system to mark the spelling
sounds and morphemes they recognize within the words. Whitney monitors this activity, using the
extensive training she has received to support students in their work and to reinforce the positive
culture of the classroom. She offers praise to individuals and the group while prompting students to
persist when they get stuck.
When this word analysis work is complete, Whitney asks each student to read the syllables aloud
and then read the full word. When Whitney notices a student error with a word, she provides an
immediate and brief correction. The student re-reads the word correctly and the practice continues.
This process allows a virtuous cycle to be created between students, their teachers, and one
another. Often, secondary students who are not yet proficient with reading also struggle with selfdoubt, frustration, and negative self-talk. Green Dot’s intervention method establishes a learning
environment in which taking risks and making mistakes is encouraged and valued as part of the
reading process. Students learn to complete the error correction protocol with their teacher’s
prompting, and eventually, with the support of peer coaches. Corrective feedback is a normalized
part of the process.
The Wilson Reading System is a teacher-directed instructional program that provides systematic
and explicit instruction in the key elements of reading, including word study. At the upper levels
of WRS, students learn strategies for reading multisyllabic words, progressing from less complex

Summary
English Language Arts standards for secondary students ask readers to engage in deep meaningmaking work with complex texts, yet many students enter middle and high school not having had the
opportunity to develop the foundational reading skills necessary to engage in this work. This problem
is particularly pronounced for Black and Latinx students, students experiencing poverty, and students
with disabilities. Many of these readers are able to apply phonic decoding skills to sound out words, yet
do not yet read multisyllabic words fluently and automatically. Because multisyllabic words make up the
majority of words that secondary readers encounter in text, weak or dysfluent multisyllabic word reading
can significantly impact a reader’s ability to access and make meaning from complex grade-level texts.
Encouragingly, research suggests that explicit strategies to build multisyllabic word reading skills,
including teaching syllabication principles and building morphological awareness, have strong potential
to positively impact reading fluency, which in turn leads to improved reading comprehension. These
evidence-based practices to support the development of multisyllabic word reading skills are a
critical component of a comprehensive plan for reading intervention in middle and high schools, and
implementing evidence-based multisyllabic word reading instructional strategies has strong potential to
improve student proficiency and automaticity in reading, ultimately providing greater and more equitable
access to grade-level instruction and rich, complex texts for all students.

Why are Goal Setting and Progress Monitoring Important?
When coupled with structures for reviewing the data and using data to guide intervention decisions,
consistent progress monitoring can ensure that (1) students receive more intensive support if they aren’t
responding adequately to intervention and (2) students are referred for a less restrictive plan when they
no longer demonstrate a need for the current level of service.
Students with disabilities often are not as responsive as students without disabilities to standard
administration of evidence-based academic interventions. For this reason, a primary function of
the specially designed academic instruction provided through special education is to intensify and
individualize instruction for this population. Using data to inform decisions while intensifying instruction
for students can result in improved outcomes for students with disabilities,2 and using established criteria
to inform decision-making can reduce bias and ensure equitable services and access for all students.
It is also critical that students don’t remain in an intervention when they no longer need the support.
While a primary goal of special education is to close academic achievement gaps between students
with disabilities and their typically developing peers, the majority of students, including students with
high-incidence disabilities, who are found eligible to receive special education services continue in
special education across the duration of their school careers, and students experiencing socioeconomic
disadvantage are even less likely to exit special education services.3 Consistent progress monitoring and
data-driven planning can serve to ensure instruction is provided within the least restrictive environment.

Why it Works
PROGRESS MONITORING ENABLES CONTINUOUS IMPROVEMENT OF INSTRUCTION.
Progress monitoring using tools such as curriculum-based measurements (CBMs) helps teachers
design more effective instructional programs that result in improved student outcomes.4 See Guiding
Literacy Interventions Through Tiered Assessment for more information on CBMs.
WHEN COUPLED WITH DATA-BASED DECISION-MAKING PROCESSES, PROGRESS
MONITORING DEMANDS A BALANCE OF SCAFFOLDS AND STUDENT INDEPENDENCE.
Data-based program modification is defined as a “sequence of decisions that are made when modifying
a student’s program and a parallel set of evaluation activities that are intended to increase the likelihood
that program modifications [will] be successful.”5 This regular monitoring and analysis of progress spurs
actions such as increasing scaffolds in response to low rates of improvement and increasing student
independence as mastery and progress are achieved.
ENGAGING STUDENTS IN GOAL SETTING AND PROGRESS MONITORING CAN
SUPPORT MOTIVATION TO READ AND BUILD BENEFICIAL SKILLS.
Many secondary students who are not yet proficient readers have a history of reading failure and a low
sense of reading self-efficacy. Being able to visualize their own progress toward a goal via a progressmonitoring graph can serve as a strong motivator and reinforce the benefits of reading practice.6
Oftentimes, progress monitoring is something that is done to students, instead of being a collaborative
process completed with students.7 When implemented appropriately, goal setting and progress
monitoring build self-determination skills and strategies like self-monitoring and self-evaluation, which
have been found to be especially beneficial for students with learning disabilities.8

DESIGNING A PROGRESS-MONITORING SYSTEM
AT UNCOMMON CHARTER HIGH SCHOOL
Uncommon Charter High School set out to redesign

6.5

Literacy

Early in the process of redesigning Uncommon Charter High School’s reading intervention
class, Val knew that she and Gio would need a way to quickly and efficiently monitor students’
responsiveness to the word study and fluency interventions they were implementing in the
classroom. Val set about designing a progress-monitoring system that allowed her team to
efficiently and accurately answer the following questions:
• Which students are responding as expected to interventions? Which students may no longer
need these interventions?
• Which students are not responding as expected to interventions? Which students may require
a more intensive level of reading support?
• How is our program working overall? Are most students responding as expected to
interventions, or do we need to redesign our overall approach to better reach students?
With an understanding of the importance of selecting progress-monitoring assessments that are
valid and reliable while also quick and easy to administer, Val decided to leverage curriculum-based
measurements (CBMs) as the primary assessment to support progress monitoring for the students
participating in the Academic Prep class.
Students’ reading progress in Academic Prep is now monitored monthly via ORF CBMs. Val and
Gio meet regularly to review student data and evaluate student progress in rate and accuracy. As
an example of the decisions that they reach in these meetings, upon noting that students were
misreading words that included inflectional endings (e.g., -er, -ing, -ed), Val and Gio included
explicit instruction and practice targeting inflectional endings in upcoming lesson plans. Consistent
progress monitoring allows Val and Gio to evaluate the effectiveness of their interventions and
adjust their instruction as needed, celebrate success with students, and report on student progress
to key stakeholders.

An oral reading fluency curriculum-based measurement (ORF CBM) is a one-minute
timed assessment of the rate and accuracy of a student’s oral reading fluency. ORF CBMs
are administered in a 1:1 setting, during which the student reads aloud for one minute from
a leveled passage while an adult listens and scores, noting errors and the number of words the
student reads in one minute. The administrator of the assessment calculates both accuracy (the
percentage of the total words read that were read correctly) and rate (the total number of words
read correctly, typically expressed as “correct words per minute,” or CWPM). ORF CBMs are one
of the strongest predictors of a student’s overall reading performance9 and are widely used as
screening, diagnostic, and progress-monitoring tools. Common commercial publishers of ORF
CBMs include DIBELS, Acadience, and AIMSweb. Explore the repeated reading chapter for
more detailed information on oral reading fluency assessment and intervention.

USING PROGRESS-MONITORING DATA TO GUIDE INTERVENTION
PLANNING AT GREEN DOT PUBLIC SCHOOLS

Green Dot Public Schools has worked to design a
suite of evidence-based reading interventions to support
their students who are not yet reading grade-level text
proficiently. For students who are receiving interventions
targeting word reading and/or reading fluency skills,
intervention teams are collecting ORF CBM data to evaluate
students’ responses to interventions.

Green Dot has developed a structured protocol to support collaborative instructional decisionmaking rooted in student data. This protocol asks teams to:
• individually review student data against Green Dot’s decision-making criteria, and
• discuss individual and group-level trends and determine which students are ready to exit
intervention, which students will continue in the current interventions, and which students
need adaptations or adjustments to their intervention plans to accelerate progress.
Specific decision-making guidance supports these conversations (see Guiding Literacy
Interventions Through Tiered Assessment for advice on creating decision-making guidelines).
Half of the students who participated in the reading fluency intervention during the 2022–
23 school year increased their oral reading fluency by over 30 words per minute from fall
to spring, and in a follow-up conversation using the protocol, a Green Dot intervention team
decided to exit these students from an oral reading fluency intervention because they had met the
benchmark. Structured processes to support collaborative, data-driven intervention decisions are
supporting Green Dot to ensure not only that students are responding to the reading interventions
they are participating in, but also that they are exited from those interventions as soon as they no
longer need them.

How to Implement this Practice
Before Implementing
Step 1: Select a Progress-Monitoring Tool
Choose a progress-monitoring tool that meets the following criteria:
• It is valid and reliable (see Guiding Literacy Interventions Through Tiered Assessment
for more guidance on assessment validity and reliability).
• It has a sufficient number of alternative forms (e.g., different passages that are equally leveled)
to allow for regular monitoring of progress.
• It provides performance benchmarks.
• It is efficient to administer and score.

Implementing Goal Setting and Progress Monitoring
Step 3: Collect Baseline Data
Assess students using the selected progress-monitoring tool at the onset of the intervention and record
this as the baseline score. When using CBMs for progress monitoring, common practice is to administer
three passages and use the median score as the baseline score. Subsequent progress monitoring only
collects data on one passage.
Baseline data should be gathered in the established data management system for staff to access
and review at the individual level, as well as at group levels (e.g., classroom or grade levels) and at
subgroup levels (e.g., students with IEPs, students identified as multi-lingual learners).
It is also important to consider how baseline data will be shared with students and families. While
conferencing protocols can account for sharing this information with students, schools will also want
to consider when and how baseline data from universal and progress-monitoring assessments will be
shared with families, and gather key resources that can aid families in understanding the information
shared. The University of Oregon’s Resource Page includes a number of handouts in English and
Spanish that can be shared with families for such purposes. Additional guidance for engaging with
students and families can be found in the Further Learning section below.

Step 4: Collaborate with Students to Set Goals
More often than not, goals are established by schools for students rather than in collaboration with
them. To ensure goals are meaningful and motivating to students, schools need to review student
baseline data, establish individual goals for student growth grounded in research, and engage
with students in goal-setting activities. Document these goals in a central location, such as a data
management system shared across the school, for team reference.
There are two primary evidence-based methods for setting student goals:
• Benchmarks: Progress-monitoring tools include internal benchmarks that can be used for goal
setting. For example, the benchmark rate for oral reading fluency for 8th grade students at the
beginning of the year using the DIBELS 8th Edition is 125 words correct per minute.
• Rate of Improvement (ROI): Use an expected rate of improvement to set a goal representing
gap-closing growth. For example, if typical 8th grade students increase their reading fluency at a
rate of .5 words per week, a goal might be set for a student to increase their fluency at a rate of .75
words per week.
See Further Learning below for detailed guidance on setting goals using both methods.
Schools can take a variety of approaches to goal setting with students, including 1:1 conferences,
small group conferences, or classroom goal-setting routines with a focused mini-lesson, like the one
highlighted in the Noble case study above. School teams may share the baseline score and growth
target they have established and are tracking internally. Alternatively, they may share suggested
reading growth targets for rate and accuracy and encourage students to establish their own growth
goals, as well as goals around reading confidence, habits, or personal accomplishments. It is highly
encouraged that students and staff share these goals with families to build buy-in and engage in
school-home partnership to support student growth.
For students with Individualized Education Plans (IEPs), align progress-monitoring goals with reading
goals in the IEP to streamline data collection and reporting.

Step 5: Conduct Progress Monitoring and
Enter Progress-Monitoring Data
MTSS frameworks recommend progress monitoring at least monthly for
students receiving Tier 2 support and weekly for students receiving intensive,
individualized interventions at Tier 3. While it may not always be possible
to progress-monitor at this frequency, establishing a progress-monitoring
schedule and calendar can ensure progress monitoring happens regularly.
Progress-monitoring data should be graphed to facilitate communication
of progress and data-based decision-making. Some assessment platforms
(e.g., Acadience, AIMSweb) have internal platforms that graph student
progress when data is entered. Free online tools such as EasyCBM or
the National Center on Intensive Intervention’s Student Progress
Monitoring Tool can also facilitate the graphing of progress-monitoring data.

Building Student Agency Through Progress Monitoring
Educators can use these activities regularly to involve and engage students in the
progress-monitoring process:
• Teaching students how to calculate their scores
• Asking students to complete progress-monitoring graphs
• Co-creating progress-monitoring goals with students
• Explicitly naming behaviors contributing to progress and gradually encouraging students to reflect
on similar behaviors
A specific six-step protocol for engaging students in progress monitoring suggests that schools:11
1. Provide explicit performance feedback (actual numbers).
2. Share true, specific, affirmative feedback (anecdotal feedback on growth/performance).
3. Work with every student to graph data.
4. Collaboratively analyze the data with the student.
5. Engage the student in a reflective discussion about their behaviors/actions that have contributed to
performance.
6. Work together to reflect on the current goal, adjust as needed, and discuss an action plan.
The Further Learning section includes the full six-step protocol, including a sample script for
engaging students in progress monitoring. Adaptations can be made by school teams to involve
families during or after these collaborative steps to bolster student agency and motivation.

Step 6: Analyze Progress-Monitoring Data, Engage in
Decision-Making, and Document Decisions
Review student data at the end of each intervention cycle (approximately every six to eight weeks). It is
recommended that six to eight data points be collected before engaging in decision-making.12 Identify
next steps for individual students based on progress-monitoring data, which might include the following:
• The student has made accelerated progress, has met their growth goal and the benchmark target,
and no longer needs this intervention.
• The student is making good progress but has not yet met the benchmark target and should
continue in the intervention as it is currently implemented.
• The student is making some progress but it could be more accelerated. They will continue in the
current intervention with adaptations.
• The student is not making progress and needs a more intensive level of intervention.
Team agendas and protocols can facilitate decision-making and support documentation of decisions.
Teams should document the intervention next steps in a central location and plan for how decisions will
be shared with students, families, and other staff members.

Diagnostic Data Informs Intensification of Interventions
One outcome of consistent progress monitoring and data-based decision-making can be the
identification of students for whom interventions are not working. Often, teams will need to collect
additional diagnostic data to guide the intensification of intervention for these students. The National
Center on Intensive Intervention provides technical assistance on the implementation of databased individualization, a framework for leveraging data and evidence-based interventions to design
intensive intervention plans.

Snapshot
WHAT IS THE PRACTICE?
Practitioner-focused professional development and aligned coaching that builds
the knowledge and skills of secondary teachers to effectively provide evidence-based
interventions (EBIs) aligned with the science of reading to adolescents with disabilities.

Key Terms for this Practice
What do we mean by practitioner-focused professional development?
For the purposes of this practice, practitioner-focused professional development refers to
professional development sessions that provide educators with structured opportunities to:
1. build knowledge of evidence-based instructional practices aligned with the science
of adolescent reading,
2. apply learnings through in-session reflection and practice,
3. engage in collaborative learning to build collective knowledge and establish mutual
accountability, and
4. adapt practices for the specific needs of the populations served by the participants
with a focus on culturally and linguistically diverse students with disabilities.
What do we mean by aligned coaching?
For the purposes of this practice, coaching refers to individualized teacher coaching that is:
1. content-specific, with the intended outcome of increasing teachers’ capacity to
implement evidence-based practices specific to improving reading outcomes for
adolescent readers with disabilities, and
2. job-embedded, meaning that coaching experiences occur during the teachers’
contracted school days and are directly aligned with their instructional responsibilities.

WHY IS IT IMPORTANT?
National assessments of reading consistently indicate that an alarmingly high number of
secondary students lack the foundational reading skills necessary to meet grade-level
reading benchmarks.1 These low levels of reading proficiency are particularly pronounced
for Black and Latinx students as well as students with disabilities. Low reading levels
present significant barriers to students accessing, making meaning from, and finding joy
in rich, complex, grade-level texts and ultimately limit students’ options for education and
employment.

Snapshot
CONTINUED

To improve literacy outcomes in students — particularly Black and Latinx students with
disabilities experiencing poverty — educators must be well-versed in how to teach
reading. Elementary teachers typically receive training in teaching reading as part of their
credentialing, but secondary teachers typically do not. Though a comprehensive body of
research points to practices that are most likely to close the gaps for adolescent readers
with disabilities, these practices are not consistently implemented in secondary schools
and classrooms, and secondary teachers consistently report that they do not feel equipped
to teach foundational reading skills.
Encouragingly, research shows that sustained, intensive professional development
that focuses on specific academic content and is connected to practice is associated
with student achievement gains.2 Providing professional development that meets these
evidence-based criteria has significant potential to improve reading outcomes for students.

WHY IT WORKS
Practitioner-focused professional development and aligned coaching bridge theory
and practice, supporting the consistent implementation of evidence-based interventions
in classrooms and schools. The professional development practices described in this
case study help secondary educators understand and apply the science of reading,
while equipping them with the tools and coaching to teach reading skills as part of their
core instruction.
The guidance contained in these stories shows
how schools can build the capacity of instructional
coaches and district staff to craft effective and
efficient professional development with the goal
of consistent implementation of evidence-based
interventions aligned with the science of reading.
These best practices emerged from Professional
Learning Communities (PLCs) on evidence-based
literacy interventions as part of the Networked
Improvement Community for Students with
Disabilities (NIC). Together with corresponding
recommended resources, the best practices
provide a blueprint for schools across the country
to build educator capacity for teaching reading.

The Challenge
How can schools support secondary literacy teachers to
implement evidenced-based reading interventions?
The NIC’s literacy-focused PLC was initially
established to build the capacity of systemslevel leaders to design and deliver literacyfocused professional development within their

transferring to classroom-based educators.
Further, participants consistently reported a

research on the topic, which finds that while
the science of reading provides a great deal
of guidance regarding how individuals learn to

What is the science
of reading?
The Reading League proposes this as a
common definition for the term:
“The science of reading is a vast,
interdisciplinary body of scientificallybased research about reading and issues
related to reading and writing.
This research has been conducted over
the last five decades across the world,
and it is derived from thousands of studies
conducted in multiple languages. The
science of reading has culminated in a
preponderance of evidence to inform how
proficient reading and writing develop;
why some have difficulty; and how we
can most effectively assess and teach
and, therefore, improve student outcomes

One contributor to this research-to-practice
gap may be a lack of knowledge of evidencebased practices on the part of secondary
teachers. This group consistently reports that both teacher preparation programs and job-embedded

address reading deficits for adolescent readers.5 To build confidence in their capacity to implement
evidence-based reading interventions and to ultimately improve reading outcomes, secondary teachers
need access to ongoing professional learning opportunities that focus on student learning, are contentspecific, and are connected to instructional practice.
Along with the Marshall team, Green Dot Public Schools, Noble Schools, and Uncommon Schools,
the three participating partner schools within the NIC’s literacy PLC, needed to find a way to close
these gaps and ensure that their adolescent students were receiving the evidenced-based reading
interventions that they needed.

The Practice
Year-Long Practitioner-Focused Professional Learning
Community to Build Educator Capacity
The Marshall team, in partnership with educators from the three networks and NIC content expert
Genevieve Thomas, decided to shift the focus of the PLC to be practitioner-focused and to include
classroom-based educators and the network-level staff from the participating networks. The intention
behind this shift was to build capacity and alignment across levels within the systems. The PLC
was redesigned to increase educator and staff capacity to implement evidence-based instructional
practices for adolescent readers with disabilities. A subset of PLC members participated in regular,
job-embedded coaching with a literacy content expert with the goal of increasing the transfer of
research to practice within their classrooms.
Because the intended outcome of the PLC was to bridge the research-to-practice gap by increasing
participants’ capacity to implement evidence-based reading practices, the PLC used a review of
the literature on effective reading practices for adolescent readers with disabilities — “EvidenceBased Strategies for Reading Instruction of Older Students with Learning Disabilities,” by Roberts
et al.7 — as the foundation for their scope and sequence of topics. This research summary outlines
five instructional priorities for adolescent readers with disabilities: word study, fluency, vocabulary,
comprehension, and motivation. The scope and sequence of the PLC was designed to build capacity
within each of these instructional areas.

Intended Outcomes
The goals of the PLC were to support participants to:
• connect with other educators from across the NIC to build community, increase capacity to
implement evidence-based instructional practices, collaboratively seek solutions to common
barriers, and share and celebrate successes;
• describe the evidence-based strategies for reading instruction of adolescents with disabilities,
including summarizing the essential research; and
• modify instructional approaches and materials to more effectively include evidence-based
practices for supporting reading development in secondary readers.

Professional Learning Community Structure
The PLC was designed and facilitated by Genevieve Thomas, literacy content expert, in partnership
with members of the Marshall Improvement team. PLC participants included teachers as well as
school- and network-level leaders from the participating networks. The PLC met monthly for 90-minute
virtual sessions, which included one hour of knowledge building and application around a specific
element of reading, followed by 30 minutes of opt-in small-group or one-on-one coaching with the
content area expert.

Coaching Structure
PLC members also had access to individual and small group coaching between PLC meetings. The
intended outcome of coaching experiences was to provide individual support to teachers as they worked
to apply the learnings from the content-focused PLC sessions to their local contexts and support them in
accelerating their improvement efforts for students with disabilities. Coaching activities included:
• co-creating instructional materials;
• analyzing student data in order to draw conclusions and inform planning;
• modeling, practicing, and receiving feedback on specific instructional routines; and
• designing instructional protocols.

Putting Word Study in Action at Uncommon Schools
In an early coaching conversation, Val and Genevieve worked together to co-analyze the results of
an oral reading fluency curriculum-based measurement that Val had administered to the students in
her reading intervention class. Val and Genevieve identified that one root cause of low fluency was
difficulty reading multisyllabic words.
As a participant in the literacy PLC, Val learned about effective word study instructional practices,
including teaching syllabication and building morphological awareness. In her coaching conversations
with Genevieve, she developed a hypothesis that explicitly teaching these strategies would improve
students’ skill in reading multisyllabic words, thus improving their reading fluency and ultimately
reading comprehension. Val and Genevieve collaborated over the course of several coaching
conversations to plan instructional strategies, debrief their effectiveness, and make modifications to
instructional plans based on learnings. After several coaching cycles, the instructional protocol for this
reading intervention class now consistently includes explicit word study instruction using evidencebased practices.

How to Implement this Practice
Step 1: Plan Professional Development Opportunities that
Build Teacher Capacity to Implement Evidence-Based
Reading Practices for Adolescents with Disabilities
The process for planning the professional development series is nonlinear. The order of the steps that
follow will vary based on each school or network’s local context, and some steps may not be needed.

Identify a content area expert.
Identify a content area expert who will lead the design of the professional development series and the
individual sessions. This should be an individual with expertise in the science of reading as it relates to
adolescents with disabilities, as well as experience designing professional development for adult learners.
If no internal team members have this expertise, source, vet, and contract with an external consultant.

Articulate a purpose for the professional development series.
Articulate a purpose that clearly connects to improved reading outcomes and increased access to
grade-level texts and content for adolescents with disabilities. Explicitly grounding the purpose of the
professional learning experiences within the school’s or network’s priorities for students with disabilities,
with a focus on increased access to grade-level text and content, ensures that equitable access and
outcomes for all learners remain the north star.

The following questions may serve as helpful catalysts for articulating the purpose of the professional
development series:
• What are the current, related school and/or network priorities, and how might this professional
development serve to further progress toward these priorities?
• What does the data currently tell us about reading achievement and progress for students with
disabilities? How does this compare to the general student population?
• What are teachers saying about their readiness to implement evidence-based reading practices
for students with disabilities? What do performance evaluations and/or instructional observations
suggest about the knowledge and skills teachers need to develop?
• How will building teacher knowledge and skill around evidence-based reading practices for students
with disabilities move the school or network closer to established goals and priorities?

Identify participants.
Start by determining the scale of the implementation. If practitioner-focused professional development
related to evidence-based reading practices for students with disabilities is new for the school or
network, consider testing the structure with a smaller group of users before spreading and scaling. When
starting small, select participants who can commit to participating in the experiences, express excitement
about the content, will work to implement the practices in their planning and instruction, and are willing to
transparently share their learnings and feedback in the interest of improving the structure before scaling.
To create the strongest possible experience, leaders can participate alongside practitioners. This adds
both support and accountability.
At scale, participants in the professional learning experiences should include not only the educators
who are responsible for planning and implementing reading instruction for adolescents with disabilities,
but also those who coach them. If students with disabilities are educated primarily in general education
settings, both special and general educators should be equally involved. Determine participation
expectations for educators, including who is required to participate and who can voluntarily participate.

Design the scope and sequence.
When designing the professional development scope and sequence of topics, consider both the intended
outcomes of the series and the current, relevant research related to those outcomes. For example, if an
intended outcome is for participants to implement valid assessments and analyze the results to identify
specific student reading needs, review the literature on this topic, and extract key findings to anchor
the scope and sequence of topics. See Further Learning for suggested articles summarizing the most
important science of reading concepts for secondary educators of students with disabilities.
Generate a list of learning needs for the target participants in relation to these outcomes. For example,
if the participants have a strong foundation in best practices for vocabulary instruction following a
previous school-wide focus on vocabulary, but little depth of knowledge related to word study and fluency
instruction, consider a focus on word study and fluency that explicitly builds on participants’ knowledge
about effective vocabulary instruction.
Determine the number of professional development sessions, aiming to convene at least monthly, and
map out the topic for each session.

Reimagining Reading Comprehension Instruction
One session of the practitioner-focused literacy PLC implemented within the NIC was focused on
evidence-based practices for supporting reading comprehension instruction. The purpose of the
session was articulated as “reimagining reading comprehension instruction as an opportunity to
explicitly develop the specific subskills that will support secondary students, particularly secondary
students with reading and language disabilities, in accessing and making meaning from complex,
grade-level texts in order to ensure equitable access to core content for our target population.”
After clarifying the purpose of the session, Genevieve designed activities to support participants in
achieving this outcome. These included:
• a reflection activity in which participants shared in partners their current understandings of reading
comprehension;
• a summary of the most relevant and current research related to reading comprehension instruction
for adolescents with disabilities, followed by an opportunity for participants to process and capture
learnings;
• breakout conversations where participants could choose one aspect of reading comprehension
instruction to dig into more deeply with peers; and
• time for participants to consider applications to their current instructional planning practices and to
commit to action-oriented next steps.

Step 3: Deliver the Professional Development Sessions
Facilitate professional development sessions.
It is important that sessions are led by strong facilitators who possess cultural humility and an
awareness of cultural and identity-sustaining pedagogy. This is particularly important for our target
population. Effective facilitators acknowledge the ways in which dynamics of race, class, and ability
impact reading achievement for the target population while simultaneously communicating high
expectations for both students and educators.8

Use feedback to assess and improve.
Gather feedback after each session, and use it to assess impact and inform the design of subsequent
sessions. While articulating a scope and sequence for the professional development series will aid in
planning individual sessions, it is also important to view the scope and sequence as a flexible roadmap

that will be adapted based on participant needs and feedback. Collecting feedback at the end of each

Plan for coaching.

Align the coaching approach with any relevant school or network coaching models or processes.

Determine the frequency of coaching sessions, ideally biweekly, but with at least one coaching
session occurring between each professional learning session. Schedule coaching sessions with
individual teachers early in the process of planning professional learning to support consistency and
accountability, but practice flexibility and allow for adjustments to the schedule as needed.

Facilitate coaching experiences.
Coaches should establish a student-centered outcome for each coaching touchpoint in collaboration
with the teacher, and outcomes should clearly align with the reading instructional knowledge and skills
developed in the professional development sessions. Coaching experiences should include active
practice of instructional planning and/or literacy practices, along with feedback whenever possible.
Both the coach and the teacher are responsible for documenting key decisions and next steps, and for
following through on their respective next steps between coaching touchpoints.

SPREADING PROFESSIONAL LEARNING AT NOBLE SCHOOLS
Several special education teachers at Noble Schools have been implementing a structured protocol for
developing reading fluency since the 2021–22 school year. After seeing consistent implementation of this
practice result in improved reading fluency for students, the special education leadership team decided to
provide a professional learning experience to other educators within the network in an effort to spread the
promising practice.

The team first set out to identify content area experts to design and deliver the professional
development session. They solicited an internal co-teaching team, special education teacher
Megan Murphy and general education World History teacher Derek DeHaan, who had been
successfully implementing a reading fluency protocol in their co-taught World History class for
several months, to lead the work.
Megan and Derek designed a professional development session and key training materials in
alignment with the recommended practices described within this chapter. First, they explicitly
shared the “why” by summarizing important research that clearly establishes the role of reading
fluency in the ability to make meaning from text. They thoughtfully connected the specific topic of
fluency to their overall network goals, sharing that “Literacy is the key to healthy, choice-filled lives
for our students.”
Within the session, Megan and Derek clearly communicated the evidence-based practices
for developing reading fluency by sharing a specific instructional protocol, showing a video of
their class engaged in the protocol, and reviewing the resources they developed to support
implementation of the practice. They built participant buy-in by sharing data that clearly suggested
an improvement in student reading outcomes as a result of participating in the reading fluency
protocol, as well as testimonials from students. The session concluded with opportunities for
participants to choose from several optional activities designed to support them in applying learning
to their own instructional contexts.
Megan and Derek facilitated the session twice during Noble professional development days,
in which teachers could choose sessions aligned with their instructional priorities. Across the
two offerings over 50 Noble teachers opted to attend. Feedback collected following the session
indicated that a significant number of attendees planned to incorporate reading fluency practices
into their instructional planning after attending the session.
See Repeated Reading: Building Oral Reading Fluency in Secondary Readers for
comprehensive guidance on how to implement instruction designed to improve students’ oral
reading fluency.

NIC partners:
• At Green Dot California, explicit professional development has been designed to build the
knowledge and skill sets of educators to implement oral reading fluency interventions as they
spread and scale efforts. This includes an initial team training with application activities, guidance
documents grounded in the research and practical knowledge from the PLC members, and a
gradual release coaching model. Additionally, PLC participation inspired conversations about
research on motivation for struggling readers, which led one school to implement an incentive
program that increased software engagement and Lexile growth.
• At Uncommon Schools, Val Dutta continues to apply the knowledge acquired from the PLC in
Academic Prep and other reading intervention spaces, focusing on building reading skills within the
context of grade-level instruction. Uncommon has spread their instructional framework and literacy
coaching practice to additional schools, with the intent to scale it further in the upcoming school year.
• At Noble Schools, PLC members are integrating the research and practice they gathered within
the PLC to construct and adopt district-wide professional development and related resources for
educators on building reading skills. They have trained classroom teachers and support staff in
the science of reading and the implementation of a structured repeated-reading protocol to apply
evidence-based interventions in core classes. The protocol helped students build reading
fluency while learning course content.

Introduction
Multi-tiered intervention models are effective frameworks for supporting students with reading
disabilities, including secondary students.1 At its core, the MTSS framework leverages data to plan,
monitor, and continually improve the academic, social-emotional, and behavioral instruction and
intervention provided to students. To accomplish this, educators are encouraged to plan for three
types of data collection: universal screening, progress monitoring, and diagnostic assessment.
Additionally, teams are tasked with designing infrastructure and protocols to support ongoing databased decision-making. Each of these components of assessment and decision-making within the
MTSS framework is defined and described below.

Universal Screening
Universal screening refers to the practice of administering valid and reliable screeners of proficiency to
all students within a school system at least once yearly.2 A system for screening all students in reading
can identify students at risk of not meeting grade-level ELA benchmarks and standards early in the
school year as the first step in designing and tailoring interventions to support reading acceleration.
Universal screening data can also be used to evaluate programs for disaggregated subgroups of
students and to assess the extent to which programs are successful in helping all students meet
grade-level ELA standards. This creates a system of accountability for ensuring access and progress
for all students.

The basic process for conducting universal screening of reading is as follows:3
1. Identify screening measures
2. Set proficiency cut points to identify students with differing levels of risk or need
3. Determine the frequency of screening (at least one time yearly)
4. Identify personnel to conduct screening
5. Collect universal screening data
6. Input, organize, and summarize the data
7. Respond to the results
When considering universal screening measures, schools need to select measures that are valid and
reliable, demonstrate classification accuracy, minimize statistical bias, and are representative of the
school’s population. The National Center on Intensive Intervention provides detailed guidance to support
schools in evaluating assessment options against each of these criteria.4 In some cases, schools may
need to adopt new assessment systems to conduct universal screening of reading. In other cases, they
may be able to leverage existing resources. Research has suggested that assessments such as statelevel assessments and computer-adaptive tests can reliably identify students who are not reaching
grade-level reading targets.5 Early warning systems, which use indicators such as course performance,
attendance, and suspension data to flag students at risk of not meeting academic or behavioral
expectations, are also recommended at the secondary level.6
Proficiency cut-points categorize students as at risk or not at risk of not meeting academic or behavior
benchmarks. Schools can utilize various methods to establish proficiency cutpoints (e.g., below a specific
percentile on a norm-referenced assessment or within a predetermined standard, such as “below basic,”
on a state-level assessment). Cut points must be established thoughtfully as they have a significant
influence on the number and characteristics of students who may be identified via the screening process.7
When planning for the administration of a universal screener, schools need to account for training both
those who will administer the screener and those who will interpret the results. Schools should also plan
to monitor implementation fidelity. Inconsistent assessment administration, scoring errors, and data entry
errors can impact the accuracy and usefulness of screening data. Schools can minimize these errors by
providing ongoing training, practice, and coaching in administration and data entry, using administration
checklists and protocols, and checking periodically for consistency.8

Diagnostic Assessment
While universal screening can serve to identify students who are nonproficient readers, it is insufficient
for informing the design of effective reading interventions. As established earlier in the Collection
Literature Review, nonproficient adolescent readers have diverse reading profiles, and the degree
and nature of support needs can vary greatly across individual students. Diagnostic assessment of the
specific reading needs of students identified as nonproficient readers is an important supplement to
screening measures and is necessary for effective intervention planning at the secondary level.9
Diagnostic data can be collected through formal (e.g., standardized measures) and informal
approaches (e.g., error analysis of student work samples). Curriculum-based measurements (CBMs)
of oral reading fluency are one reliable measure of reading rate and accuracy,10 and analysis of student
errors from CBMs can serve as a helpful diagnostic measure.

Educators and problem-solving teams can then use diagnostic data to develop hypotheses about
the causes of student reading difficulties. These hypotheses can then inform the development of an
intervention plan that targets those specific causes.11 For example, educators can analyze CBM errors
to determine if a student is reading accurately yet slowly and may benefit from intervention targeting
reading rate or if the student is reading inaccurately with errors in multisyllabic word reading and may
benefit from intervention targeting word study skills.
The National Center on Intensive Intervention recommends schools first confirm the fidelity of
instruction and intervention implementation before engaging in diagnostic assessment.12 This refers
to a process of evaluating the extent to which instruction or intervention was delivered as planned and
designed. This includes assessing the level of student engagement in instruction, adherence to the
instructional plan, duration of instruction, quality of instructional delivery, and the degree to which the
intervention aligned with identified student needs.

Progress Monitoring
Progress monitoring, or the ongoing collection of data to assess student responsiveness to instruction,
is an essential component of MTSS.13 The steps to implementing a progress-monitoring system include:
1. Designing the progress-monitoring process
2. Selecting progress-monitoring tools
3. Training staff
4. Developing student progress-monitoring plans
5. Collecting progress-monitoring data and monitoring fidelity
6. Analyzing progress-monitoring data
Progress-monitoring assessments should be administered at least monthly for students receiving
interventions at the Tier 2 level within an MTSS and at least weekly for students receiving interventions
at the Tier 3 level.14 Fidelity of administration, scoring, and data entry should be monitored using the
approaches described above (see Universal Screening).
As with universal screening measures, progress-monitoring measures must be valid and reliable.
Further, they should (1) have a sufficient number of alternative forms to allow for frequent
administration, (2) specify what would constitute sufficient progress, and (3) provide benchmarks
of progress.13 Curriculum-based measurements of oral reading fluency have been established as
a valid and reliable tool for monitoring the progress of secondary students participating in reading
interventions,15 although the frequency of progress monitoring may be more widely spaced for
secondary students than the weekly administration typically recommended for elementary students.16
Developing student progress-monitoring plans includes setting individual student goals. Strategies
for setting individual student goals include using benchmarks for middle- or end-of-year performance,
establishing a rate of improvement (ROI) based on normative data, or using the intra-individual
framework.17 See the Further Learning section of this appendix for a resource from the National
Center on Intensive Intervention that provides comprehensive guidance for each of these approaches.

Data-Based Decision-Making
The previous sections of this resource have outlined the types of data that are collected within a wellfunctioning MTSS, and data-based decision-making refers to the processes and protocols that schools
use to translate those assessment results into actions for students. Using data to inform decisions while
intensifying instruction for students can result in improved outcomes for students with disabilities,18 and
using established criteria to inform decision-making can reduce bias and ensure equitable services and
access for all students.
Data-based decision-making occurs at all levels of the MTSS. MTSS teams analyze universal
screening data to identify students requiring academic or behavioral interventions, evaluate the
effectiveness of programs, determine needs, allocate resources, and set schoolwide goals. Once
sufficient progress-monitoring data has been collected (typically six to nine data points19), MTSS
teams review data to determine whether students have met progress-monitoring goals and no longer
require intervention, are responding favorably to intervention and should continue to participate in the
intervention as it has been planned, or are not demonstrating sufficient progress and require a more
intensive or individualized intervention plan.
Ultimately, data-based decision-making is the vehicle for supporting continuous improvement from the
school to the individual student level.

Conclusion
Readiness for college and career in the twenty-first century requires the development of a broad
set of competencies ranging from critical thinking to information, media, and technology literacy to
communication and collaboration. Reading proficiency is not only a necessary contributor to the
development of these skills, but it is also correlated with educational and life outcomes, including high
school completion, employment and earning potential, and physical and social-emotional well-being.
Schools must do more than prepare some students for college and career in the twenty-first century;
they must ensure that all students are equipped with the competencies needed to succeed in their postsecondary lives.
The aim of the Marshall Street Networked Improvement Community for Students with Disabilities
(NIC) is to systematically improve the way our education system serves those furthest from these
opportunities with a focus on Black and Latinx students with disabilities experiencing poverty. Within
this work, one challenge is clear: students within our priority population are not developing the reading
skills necessary to support twenty-first-century college and career readiness. Addressing low reading
proficiency rates poses unique problems for secondary educators. First, nonproficient secondary
readers are a particularly heterogeneous group who differ greatly in both the degree and the nature
of their reading difficulties. This broad range of student needs necessitates an equally wide range of
interventions, an approach that is often missing in the “one-size-fits-all” reading intervention provided
in many secondary settings. Traditional assessment systems and practices in secondary settings fail to
illuminate individual student reading profiles, limiting
schools’ ability to align interventions with student
needs. Effective reading intervention in the secondary
setting depends on educators who understand how
reading develops, are knowledgeable about language
and literacy constructs, implement evidence-based
instructional practices, and are proficient with using
assessment data to drive instructional planning.
Yet, secondary educators report feeling woefully
unprepared by both preservice training and in-service
professional development to address students’ wide
range of reading needs. These structural challenges
are compounded by the reality that secondary
students who don’t read well have often experienced
years of reading failure, contributing to low motivation
to read.

Over the course of three years, the NIC, a group of 10 public charter school networks serving more
than 75,000 students nationwide, identified and piloted various best practices for improving outcomes
for our priority population. This collection — itself an outcome of the work these schools accomplished
— highlights the strongest and most promising of the practices implemented with the goal of improving
reading proficiency. It details four best practices: Guiding Literacy Interventions Through Tiered
Assessment, Repeated Reading: Building Oral Reading Fluency in Secondary Readers,
Developing Multisyllabic Word Reading Skills in Adolescent Readers, and Strengthening Timely
Interventions through Goal Setting and Progress Monitoring. It concludes with a case study
showcasing an ongoing, responsive approach to developing educator capacity to implement evidencebased practices for secondary reading intervention, and a guidance document for school and district
leaders on assessing adolescent readers.

Four principles guided the approach taken by schools
within the NIC:
• First, schools understood that effective secondary reading intervention had to be driven by databased decision-making. Recognizing that traditional “one-size-fits-all” reading intervention
driven by the availability of commercial programs has consistently failed to serve students in the
target population adequately, NIC educators worked to select valid and reliable assessments to
identify and understand nonproficient readers within their communities, develop decision-making
criteria and protocols to systematically plan interventions, and strategically align interventions with
individual student reading profiles.
• Second, schools in the NIC committed to the implementation of evidence-based practices
in secondary reading intervention. The problem is not that we don’t know how to serve
secondary students with reading skill deficits; in fact, “...enough is already known about adolescent
reading – both the nature of the problems of struggling readers and the types of interventions
and approaches needed to address these needs – in order to act immediately on a broad
scale.”1 Educators within the NIC continually worked to align their efforts with the best practice
recommendations from the field of research on secondary reading intervention.
• Third, our approach to secondary reading intervention maintained a focus on meaningful access
to and participation in rigorous, grade-level academics. As schools worked to address the
needs of students with foundational reading skill gaps, their work was guided by the “north star”
of grade-level content and texts. As such, NIC educators worked to integrate reading intervention
within core, general education curriculum and courses and, even in pull-out intervention models, to
leverage grade-level texts and content for instruction.
• Finally, the NIC’s approach to secondary reading intervention included a commitment to
responsive, ongoing educator professional development. The professional learning
opportunities provided to educators within the NIC were similarly driven by evidence-based best
practice recommendations from the field of research on adult learning and served to connect the
driving principles of our approach to secondary reading intervention for teachers and leaders.

The work driven by these guiding principles and highlighted across this collection has improved student
outcomes and meaningfully impacted both students and educators within the NIC. At Green Dot Public
Schools, the majority of students who participated in a supplemental repeated reading intervention
planned through the tiered assessment process increased their oral reading fluency, with half of the
students increasing fluency rates by over 30%. Students with IEPs who participated in the repeated
reading protocol implemented in a co-taught World History class at Noble Schools made growth in
reading proficiency rates commensurate with students without IEPs, and Uncommon students who
received reading intervention consisting of several evidence-based practices for secondary readers
increased reading fluency rates by an average of 20 correct words per minute over the course of a
school year.
These results are not minor; because of the strong correlation between reading fluency and reading
comprehension, the improvements mean that students are on the path to independent meaning-making
of grade-level texts. Educators also experienced lasting benefits from the practices implemented within
the NIC, with those who participated in the Professional Learning Community and individual coaching
structures consistently reporting that these opportunities increased their capacity to implement
evidence-based practices.

Key Takeaways
ADOLESCENCE IS NOT TOO LATE TO INTERVENE.
As members of the NIC engaged in the work of continually planning, implementing, evaluating, and
refining their approaches to secondary reading intervention, they learned that this work can result in
meaningful benefits for secondary learners and educators. They documented meaningful improvements
in students’ reading proficiency rates and attitudes toward reading and reported increased educator
capacity to implement the practices that are most likely to lead to these results. Given the role of
reading proficiency as a critical contributor to positive postsecondary outcomes, a key takeaway of this
work is that investing in these practices has the potential to yield life-changing impacts for the NIC’s
target population.
EFFECTIVE ASSESSMENTS AND DATA ANALYSIS PRACTICES PROVIDE THE
ROADMAP FOR DESIGNING GAP-CLOSING READING INTERVENTION PROGRAMMING
IN THE SECONDARY SETTING.
Like all secondary education systems, members of the NIC implemented mechanisms for collecting
student data long before engaging in the Networked Improvement Community. However, as they began
the work of refining their approaches to reading intervention, they learned that they weren’t always
collecting the right information, nor did they have in place the decision-making processes necessary
for effective intervention planning. Members of the NIC recognized that closing persistent reading gaps
for secondary students requires targeted and specific interventions and the ability to pivot quickly when
an intervention is no longer needed or isn’t yielding the expected results. This required an evaluation
of current assessment practices and a willingness to reimagine or replace current approaches with the
goal of developing systems that allowed them to know which students weren’t reading proficiently, what
specific areas of reading to address for those students, and how those students were responding to the
interventions provided.

SECONDARY READING INTERVENTION DOES NOT HAVE TO EXIST IN A SILO.
Many schools within the NIC had structures for providing reading intervention to students; however,
these approaches were generally characterized by standalone, non-credit-bearing courses driven by
commercially available programs. This approach failed to result in students transferring skills to gradelevel academic texts and content and contributed to low engagement and motivation from students
who had difficulty connecting the work of these classes to broader academic programs and goals.
Members of the NIC experimented with embedding and aligning reading intervention within gradelevel content, texts, and learning goals and learned that this approach yielded benefits. Leveraging
core, general education content as the bedrock around which reading intervention was designed
contributed to less social stigma for students receiving intervention, improved comprehension of
grade-level content and/or texts, and increased collaboration between general and special education
teachers for members of the NIC.
SECONDARY EDUCATORS WANT TO AND CAN DEVELOP THE KNOWLEDGE AND
COMPETENCIES NECESSARY TO EFFECTIVELY ADDRESS THE RANGE OF READING
NEEDS THAT PRESENT IN THEIR CLASSROOMS.
Educators within the NIC reported similar experiences and concerns to those that the larger field of
secondary educators has reported; namely, that they felt unprepared by their preservice and in-service
professional development opportunities to teach nonproficient secondary readers. While the challenge
of equipping teachers with the knowledge and competencies necessary to effectively intervene
with nonproficient secondary readers may seem daunting, members of the NIC learned that it is
possible. Responsive and collaborative professional learning grounded in evidence-based practices
and coupled with individualized coaching to assist educators with implementation led to increased
knowledge and skill in just one academic year.

The four chapters, case study, and guidance document
in this collection lay out the key components of effective
secondary reading intervention.
Together, these components set the stage for addressing the diverse needs of
nonproficient secondary readers while simultaneously growing the capacity of the
educators who serve them. The participating members of the NIC found that, as they
developed their capacity to implement evidence-based practices for nonproficient
secondary readers, student learning outcomes increased at a greater rate. However, It
is a misconception that these components cannot be implemented until each is carefully
manicured and perfected. Instead, we urge school and system leaders to start now.
Each school system has different needs, but prioritizing reading intervention will result in
improved student outcomes, more meaningful participation in core academic programming
for students with disabilities, and increased educator efficacy.

The Networked Improvement Community for Students with
Disabilities (NIC), a group of 10 public charter school networks
