Practice Overview
What is the Practice?
General and special education teachers
meet regularly to align on content, reflect on
student needs, remove barriers, and select
instructional approaches to plan lessons that
meet the needs of all students, particularly
those with disabilities.

How do collaborating
teachers and co-teachers
compare? For whom is
co-planning useful?
Collaborating teachers are one general
educator and one special educator who
share their expertise with one another in
order to support all students, particularly
those with disabilities.
Co-teachers are collaborating teachers who
plan and teach a course together. They are
equally responsible for the class, whereas
other collaborating teachers may support
from separate spaces.
Co-planning is relevant and important whether
instructional support is delivered via coteaching, push-in, pull-out, consultant teacher
support, or a combination of these services.

Components of a Strong Co-Planning Meeting
The goal of co-planning is to ensure that collaborating teachers develop high-impact, data-driven
adaptations to lessons that meet the needs of all students by drawing on the expertise of both the
general and special education teachers. Effective co-planning leverages four key components —
alignment, reflection, removal, and selection — to meet this goal.
The table below outlines these components at a high level for a strong, 45-minute co-planning meeting.
For additional information, see the detailed version of this table in the Appendix.

COMPONENTS OF A STRONG CO-PLANNING MEETING
Strong co-planning meetings that result in effective collaborative
teaching leverage four key components: alignment, reflection,
removal, and selection.

Bringing this Practice to Life
CO-PLANNING FOR DIFFERENTIATED INSTRUCTION
AT SUMMIT ATLAS
Planning for the most vulnerable subgroups of students positively impacts all students. At Summit Atlas,
co-planning with a focus on students with disabilities has led to improvements in academic outcomes for
students without IEPs, too. Strong co-planning that focuses on meeting the needs of students who struggle
supports mindsets and skills that adapt lessons to improve comprehension for all learners.

At Summit Atlas in Seattle, WA, Hannah, a special education teacher, and Jess, a general
education history teacher, meet biweekly to co-plan. Each week, they focus on a new subset of
Jess’s 21 students with IEPs. As a result of their collaboration in the spring of 2023, the percentage
of students demonstrating mastery of the course’s focal cognitive skills nearly doubled, increasing
from 36% to 67%.
The following case study demonstrates how their use of four key co-planning components —
alignment, reflection, removal, and selection — leads to strategic and aligned instructional planning
that meaningfully improves outcomes for students with disabilities.

Reconnect and Set Intentions
Hannah and Jess begin their meeting by reviewing the data across Jess’s two history classes, 11th
grade US History and 12th grade AP Government. Based on proficiency levels, they agree to focus
on 12th grade students with disabilities. More students are struggling in that class, and they are
approaching critical, end-of-high school assessments.

Step 2: Enable Teacher Investment
Co-planning requires teachers to learn many new skills, from strong collaboration to co-construction of
aligned plans that address a wide variety of students’ needs. A crucial component of leveraging coplanning as a viable practice is being realistic about and attentive to teachers’ other responsibilities,
making sure that they have the time to dedicate to this practice.
At Summit Atlas, the team approached co-planning with a deep commitment to change for both
students and educators. School leaders recognized evidence of a lack of shared ownership for
supporting students with disabilities and saw that special educators, in particular, were stretched too
thin to have maximum impact on their students’ experiences and outcomes. At the root of the problem,
they realized, was a lack of clear roles and responsibilities for general and special educators.
Leaders knew that in order to invest teachers in the work of cross-team collaboration they needed to
ensure that each person understood their role within the partnership. By doing that work, teachers were
able to reprioritize how they worked together and to delve deeply into co-planning. By leveraging the
expertise of special educators, the team was able to build general educators’ capacity to better serve
students with disabilities in courses where they spend most of their time. Subsequently, Atlas saw
significant improvements in outcomes for all students.
At Ednovate, network and school leaders invited collaborating teachers to engage in a nine-week cycle
of coaching focused on co-planning. By focusing on a cohort of three pairs who were highly invested in
the practice, the experience was well received, led to notable student improvements, and is now being
expanded to more teacher pairs in the network.

Step 3: Support Team Formation
Effective co-planning is reliant on teachers feeling comfortable openly sharing ideas and questions and
effectively navigating different working styles and inevitable conflicts.
• School leaders can support collaborating teachers as they develop their professional relationship
by providing them with time and resources to intentionally focus on that dynamic.
• Explicitly discussing norms for how teachers will co-plan will accelerate this work. Topics to
discuss and commitments to make can include the following:
Pre-Work: How will we support each other with completing pre-work?
How will we share relevant documents (e.g., lesson plans)?
How and where will we log our pre-work notes? (Google Doc comments? A separate Google Doc?
An email?)
How will we approach each other when, inevitably, someone does not complete a week’s pre-work?

Step 4: Prioritize a Campus Culture of Collaboration
The most common barrier to successful co-planning is that time alloted for co-planning is not held
sacred and used exclusively for that purpose. School leaders can remove this barrier by building a
campus culture and commitment to collaboration time. Training all staff in the vision and purpose of
collaboration time at the start of the year can help team members understand its intended use and
prioritization. It may also be helpful to establish accountability measures to ensure co-planning blocks
are used as intended.
Strong accountability measures may include the following:
• Co-planning times are held sacred. In other words, other required meetings that collaborating teachers
must attend, such as department or IEP meetings, are not scheduled during co-planning times.
• Collaborating teachers are assigned substitute coverage only during free prep periods, not during
co-planning blocks.
• Teachers' workloads are regularly evaluated so that non-essential commitments may be eliminated,
freeing up time to adequately prepare for co-planning meetings.
• Routines exist to check that co-planning time is focused on lessons.
• Teachers use the block and protocol as intended for instructional planning.

The Need for High-Level Plans
Co-planning is strongest when it is focused on the coming one to two weeks’ lessons. This allows
collaborating teachers to discuss and make adaptations that meet specific student needs rather than
speaking in generalities. This is only possible when both teachers are clear, in advance of the meeting, on
what is coming next for students. When this is not the case, co-planning meetings frequently devolve into
lesson creation meetings (which, because of limited time, do not involve detailed accommodations and
adaptations for students based on needs) or generic discussions about strategies that are theoretically useful
but not practical.
As the instructional leaders of school buildings, school leaders play a critical role in the effectiveness of coplanning. It is important that both collaborating teachers have access to course curriculum and scope and
sequences, whether through the procurement of high-quality instructional materials or via the provision of
time and coaching support to teachers who are crafting course plans. Co-planning leans on these materials
for smaller chunks of instructional planning and is best executed when high-level lesson plans are internalized
and shared between co-planning teachers at least 48 hours in advance of co-planning meetings. For the
purposes of co-planning, shared plans should, at minimum, consist of each lesson’s objective, end-oflesson assessment task, and lesson activities.

Step 7: Structure Coaching Support
Collaborating teachers benefit tremendously from coaching support and professional development
focused specifically on co-planning. Strong examples of this include:

Support for Co-Planning
Ideally, co-planning is observed or facilitated at least once a month to ensure high-quality
implementation, followed by a co-planned lesson observation to identify further development needs.
For schools in which direct coach facilitation is either unnecessary (as teachers are more experienced)
or not yet feasible given time constraints, coaches can utilize an observation tool aligned to co-planning
when they observe meetings:
• KIPP Northern California’s Co-Planning Observation Tool
• Blue Engine’s Co-Planning Look For Checklist

Lesson-Planning Support for General Educators
For less experienced general educators, creating the initial lesson plans necessary for strong coplanning can be challenging. Providing lesson planning support in advance of meetings helps ensure
that co-planning is focused on student-specific adaptations.

Content Support for Special Educators
For special educators who are unfamiliar with a co-planned course, meeting in advance to bolster their
content knowledge or providing additional professional development (e.g., curriculum training) allows for
deeper understanding of the lessons and for connections between IEP goals and course content to be
more easily made.

School Spotlight
In order for co-planning to truly take root, it is critical that school leaders prioritize building the school
culture, team routines and habits, and skill development needed to yield desired outcomes. At school
organizations in the NIC experiencing the most success with co-planning, the collaborating partnership
is highly prioritized by school leaders, particularly through the provision of ample coaching.
At Rosenwald Collegiate Academies, the school’s on-site director of scholar support (DSS) and the
network’s senior director of curriculum (SDC) each prioritized weekly co-planning coaching for pairs
new to teaching and/or co-planning. During the Spring 2023 semester, protected support prep blocks
were created for the coaches and teaching teams to engage in ongoing professional learning focused
on co-planning agendas. For example, the DSS met with one math co-teaching pair weekly, while the
SDC engaged in similar coaching work with other co-teaching pairs new to teaching or co-planning.

Summary
Lesson-focused collaboration between the general and special educators supporting
all students, especially those who are Black and/or Latinx, have a disability, and are
experiencing poverty, is key to providing the support they need.6 No single educator can
meet the needs of every student. By aligning on upcoming content, collaborating teachers
create a shared understanding of what grade-level mastery looks like so that they hold
a shared, high bar for students that is critical to students’ success.7 When co-teachers
collectively leverage their expertise to reflect on students’ needs and remove barriers to
access, students experience increasingly tailored lessons that move them closer to their
individualized and course goals. And by strategically selecting the ways in which they
will optimize their time with students in pursuit of the latters’ goals, co-planning teams
acknowledge the power of breaking down the silos that exist in our schools and creating a
holistic team that has the ability to do more for students together than alone.

Get Started!
Ultimately, the most important action educators can
take is to start co-planning. Launching the process will
uncover what works, and what doesn’t, in a school’s
context, which can lead to revisions and adaptations to
the co-planning protocol, agenda, and routine.

What Does Co-Planning Look Like for Co-Teachers?
Co-planning is critical to the learning of students with disabilities regardless of how their specialized
services are provided. It can and should be used for all collaborating teachers. Co-planning for coteachers requires a unique focus on which model(s) of co-teaching they will select, based on their
students’ data, to best support learning.
The following example illustrates what a co-planning meeting can look like for co-teachers.

CONTEXTUAL BACKGROUND
Maria is a ninth grade English Language Arts (ELA) teacher and Samuel is a special education
teacher supporting a caseload of ninth graders in our priority population. Together, they share a
section of co-taught ELA 9. They meet every Tuesday for 45 minutes during second period in their
classroom.

PRE-WORK (48–72 hours prior)
On Friday morning, two business days before their Tuesday meeting, Maria sends Samuel a draft
of the plans for the coming week so that Samuel can review, annotate, and internalize the content.
Samuel begins to think about ways in which students on his caseload might need support based on
the lessons’ content.
Both teachers complete the exit tickets students will take after the next lesson. This ensures that
they are aligned on what content mastery looks like, have exemplars to use for grading, and can ask
any questions on content they don’t completely understand.
Both teachers asynchronously collaborate on adding details to their co-planning agenda, which is
based on a standard protocol their school uses for co-planning meetings.

THE MEETING
ALIGN on the lessons’ content.
After exchanging pleasantries, Maria and Samuel begin their co-planning meeting by discussing the
upcoming week’s lessons. Samuel surfaces his confusion about whether or not students must cite
evidence directly to show mastery of the standard, to which Maria explains they must. Maria clarifies
that the students will be working on one piece of writing from Monday through Friday, revising as the
week goes on.
The teachers then review the five upcoming end-of-lesson assessments, discussing the key
knowledge and skills students will need in order to demonstrate content mastery.

REFLECT on student data.
Maria and Samuel look at last week’s exit tickets. They discuss the trends they notice in terms of
which students are struggling with each concept. They identify how these trends will affect future
lessons and which areas may need re-teaching or intervention for certain students who need the
content taught differently.
Maria shares what she is thinking about in terms of accommodations needed based on previous
lessons and experiences. Samuel shares additional, relevant accommodations and shares how
different students’ disabilities may need additional support and attention based on this week’s content.
The co-teachers then reference their learner profile data to ensure they’re accounting for their
students’ strengths and interests.

REMOVE barriers to access.
Maria and Samuel look back at each day’s lesson plan and adjust new content delivery and practice
based on the needs that they saw students demonstrate through their data.
This week, students will be developing arguments and organizing evidence for their thesis
statements. Maria and Samuel determine that a group of students will need additional support and
feedback to revise their claims, while other students begin the next step. They also agree that a
scaffolded organizer will help students who struggle getting started. Maria pulls a scaffold from a
previous project, and Samuel agrees to revise it for this project.
Finally, the co-teachers identify students who are prepared to advance more quickly and agree that
these students should also be working on finding counterevidence for their thesis statements.

SELECT high-impact, optimized co-teaching models.
After reviewing each day’s final plans, Maria and Samuel determine that a stations-model of
co-teaching will work best for the first three days of instruction, allowing them to create highly
targeted, small groups that each teacher monitors. Maria will work with a small group to help them
revise their claims while Samuel monitors and supports another station focused on developing topic
sentences and organizing evidence in body paragraphs.
Additional stations will provide opportunities for the students with more advanced work to develop
counterarguments and for other students to engage in peer feedback.
The co-teachers reflect on their conversation, agree on next steps, and close out their meeting.

PRACTICE TOOL

Protocol for a High Impact Co-Planning Meeting
The goal of co-planning is to ensure that collaborating teachers develop high-impact data-driven
adaptations to lessons that meet the needs of all students by drawing on the expertise of both the
general and special education teachers.
The table below details the components and guiding questions of a strong, 45-minute co-planning
meeting. The guidance in this document is expansive. Teachers and coaches can focus on specific
sections in more or less depth based on their current prioritized outcomes for co-planning,
incorporating more as they build capacity.

COMPONENTS OF A STRONG CO-PLANNING MEETING
Pre-work
30–60 min.

• Each teacher completes role-specific pre-work, enabling their co-planning time to be
efficient and impactful.

Connect and Set
5 min.

• Review (and refresh, as necessary) norms for co-planning.
• Reconnect about what has happened since the last co-planning session.

What changes or progress for students resulted from our last co-planning
meeting?

–

What lesson adaptations were most impactful? Why?

• Set intentions for this meeting.

Align
10 min.

–

our time together?

–

Determine which students will be the focus of planning.

• Discuss the objective(s).
–

–

What is being asked of students? What prerequisite skills, including language
and vocabulary, does it demand?

–

What content confusions exist for either teacher that need to be clarified?

• Align on end-of-class assessments.
–

What does exemplary work look like?

–

What confusions or challenges are visible that instruction needs to address?

• Review or create criteria for success (CFS).
• Review the most challenging portions of lesson activities.

Overcoming Barriers to Implementation:
Key Leader Moves and Mindsets
The need to expedite growth for Black and Latinx students with disabilities experiencing poverty is
significant. At the same time, school leaders are often faced with difficult choices about where and how
to allocate resources to support educators to enable that progress. Given co-planning’s efficacy for all
students, particularly those with disabilities, we encourage leaders to reflect on which of the following
barriers may impact their implementation of co-planning and to seek out the aligned resources to
support next steps forward.
SPECIAL EDUCATION IS VIEWED AS A “SEPARATE” STREAM OF WORK WITHIN A SCHOOL
RATHER THAN A PRIORITY FOR GENERAL EDUCATION DEPARTMENTS OR TEACHERS.
• Provide staff with literature focused on the value of all educators supporting students with special
needs; create time and space for the school community to discuss implications for their school
community; and give examples of proof points from the same or similar contexts.8
• Intentionally create and invest the entire school team in a vision for inclusive co-planning that places
responsibility for the success of all students, including those with disabilities, on all educators.
• Generate explicit roles and responsibilities for general and special education teachers outlining
how each is responsible for contributing to instruction that supports the learning of students with
disabilities, recognizing the specific expertise of special educators.
COMPLIANCE IS PRIORITIZED OVER INSTRUCTIONAL PLANNING AS A FOCUS FOR
SPECIAL EDUCATORS.
Create alternative models of staffing for case management, such as the following:
• Dispersing case management responsibilities across a broader set of staff, including members of
the school support team (assistant principals, instructional coaches, special education department
chairs, etc.). This might include balancing timing needs, recognizing that not all cases require the
same amount of dedicated time.
• Strategically aligning special education teachers’ IEP caseloads to include only students for
whom they co-plan and/or co-teach, thus minimizing the amount of relationships and specialized
knowledge they are responsible for.
• Creating a school schedule in which all case managers have consolidated, dedicated time for case
management. For example, scheduling such that on Fridays, case managers have the full morning
to focus on IEP work and meetings. Remaining prep time during the week is then allocated to
instructional work such as co-planning.
CO-PLANNING REQUIRES GUIDANCE AND COACHING FOR ADULTS TO WORK
EFFECTIVELY TOGETHER.
• Incorporate team formation activities into the preservice schedule for any teachers who will coplan, enabling them to begin building a trusting relationship early on.
• Prioritize coaching focused on co-planning for a subset of collaborating teachers. Consider building
the practice within their partnership to ensure strong capacity and confidence before moving on
to other teachers. For additional guidance, see How-To: Establish Coaching for Collaborative
Teams.
