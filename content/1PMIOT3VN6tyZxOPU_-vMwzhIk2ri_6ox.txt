Preface
In Opening Doors to Collaboration: Interdisciplinary practices for building collaboration between general
and special education teams, we share a collection of best practices for improving learning outcomes
in secondary students — particularly Black and Latinx students with disabilities experiencing poverty
— using collaborative teaming structures between general education and special education staff. The
Networked Improvement Community for Students with Disabilities (NIC), a group of 10 public charter
school networks serving more than 75,000 students nationwide, identified the best practices through their
work between 2019 and 2023.
Led by Marshall Street at Summit Public Schools, in partnership with technical assistance providers
NIRN, RTI International, SRI International, and SWIFT Education Center, the purpose of the three-year
NIC was to systematically improve the way our education system serves Black and Latinx students with
disabilities experiencing poverty and share these solutions with school systems everywhere. This report is
funded by the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation. The findings and conclusions contained within are those
of the authors and do not necessarily reflect positions or policies of the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation.
The NIC used continuous improvement to translate existing research for improving learning outcomes
into best practices and to iterate the practices to ensure they worked in a variety of school contexts.

In Opening Doors to Collaboration: Interdisciplinary practices for
building collaboration between general and special education teams,
we share the results of this vital work:
A literature review, summarizing the key research related to teacher collaboration
Three best practices that schools used while in the NIC to successfully improve
educator collaboration. Each best practice includes:
– Case studies highlighting how schools in the NIC implemented the practices
– Step-by-step implementation guidance
– Key prerequisites and accelerants for successful implementation in any context
One case study that details how one network utilized a best practice within their own
unique context
Two guidance documents that provide school and network leadership with
recommendations for establishing and maintaining crucial structures that support
collaboration

We share these best practices so that any school in the country can leverage the learning from the
NIC to accelerate dramatic gains in learning outcomes for their students with disabilities. We hope that
administrators and educators across the country can use the stories within this collection to build their
school cultures of continuous improvement as well.

Introduction
The Challenge
America’s public schools fall short in meeting the needs of students
with disabilities.
Schools across America struggle to meet the diverse needs of today’s students, especially those
furthest from opportunity. When marginalization intersects with disability, students often face low
expectations, are systematically segregated, are denied access to college and career curricula, and do
not receive sufficient support to obtain a high school diploma and pursue their dreams. In 2022, fourth
and eighth grade students with disabilities were more than twice as likely as those without disabilities
to score “below basic” in reading and math on the National Assessment of Educational Progress,1
illustrating just how deeply schools are failing students with disabilities. For Black and Latinx students
with disabilities experiencing poverty, the challenges are even greater. Yet, research shows that
85–90% of students with disabilities can achieve at grade level when they receive high-quality
instruction and appropriate accommodations.2 Educators need better resources and more support to
help students with disabilities achieve their full potential.

Siloed educator training and student services are root causes of
the disparity.
Most teacher preparation programs continue to omit coursework focused on students with special
needs,3 leaving general educators feeling ill-equipped to support students with disabilities.4 Further
compounding the silo are schools’ problematic placement structures for students with disabilities.
Whereas 55% of white students with IEPs spend the majority5 of class time in general education
settings, only 33% of their Black peers do,6 despite research that shows inclusive educational settings
benefit all students.7 The result for these students is two different tracks and approaches: one for
students with disabilities and one for students without. This kind of segregation leaves all students at a

disadvantage. Students with IEPs lack access to grade-level content and peers, and students without
IEPs lack an approach to instruction that enables accessibility. As a result, both groups miss crucial
opportunities.

High turnover, particularly for special educators, leads to large numbers of
inexperienced teachers.
The teacher staffing shortage in America’s public schools further disadvantages students with
disabilities.8 Within the field of special education, shortages are even more pronounced. In the 10 years
between 2006 and 2016, the number of special education teachers dropped by 17%.9 In the 2017–18
school year, nearly 60% of first-year special education teachers in California were working without
being fully credentialed.10 As larger numbers of new teachers are filling roles, schools are presented
with a special education workforce in need of tremendous support, both to ensure that they are
equipped to meet students’ needs and so that they feel effective and satisfied such that they continue
to work in the field.11 This support is not just related to how to support students best. Because special
educators are asked to collaborate on a daily basis, specific training and support focused on effective
collaboration, in addition to guidance on how to open access for all learners, is crucial to their success.

Exclusionary mindsets at all levels of a school inhibit collaboration.
While most educators enter the field with the desire or intention of supporting all students, the education
system as a whole has been designed for students without disabilities. The siloing or “othering” of
students with disabilities feeds into a pervasive teacher mindset that students with disabilities are
“someone else’s” responsibility, denying students access to general education content and teachers,
which in turn limits students’ educational opportunities.12 At the highest levels of school leadership,
special education is often set to the side, left out of key discussions and decisions. This same mentality
negatively impacts general education teachers’ perceptions of their special education colleagues, often
causing them to view special education teachers as “assistants” or less qualified educators.13 Together,
these mindsets inhibit the collaboration between general and special educators and students that is
necessary to improve outcomes for students with disabilities.14

Co-teaching is the gold standard of collaboration but can be difficult
to do well.
Co-teaching is the practice of two certified teachers, traditionally a general educator and a special
educator, sharing a classroom to open access to learning for diverse populations of students. Coteaching is considered to be a best practice within the scope of collaboration between educators for
a number of reasons, including that it halves the student-to-teacher ratio and that it allows teachers
to share their different expertise with each other in the service of giving all students the support that
they need. When done well, co-teaching includes a great deal of time outside of class dedicated to
collaboration in the form of co-planning, data analysis, and receiving feedback through instructional
coaching. Co-teaching also requires intentional staffing and scheduling to ensure that all special
educators have time to meet their various compliance needs, in addition to teaching and collaborating.
Because of this, many schools find themselves implementing less effective methods of co-teaching or
looking for other ways to collaborate. For a thorough explanation of co-teaching and its challenges, see
the Collaboration Literature Review.

Our Approach
NIC participants embraced an "all means all" philosophy for their work to improve outcomes,
experiences, and environments for Black and Latinx students with disabilities experiencing poverty. As
they did so, they acknowledged that existing structures and ways of working didn't include all students
— or all staff. In response, the NIC endeavored to retrain mindsets and beliefs that othered students
with disabilities and special educators; to revise the systems, structures, and routines that limited
inclusion; and to offer models of excellence so that members could see exemplars of what is possible
for inclusive and collaborative educational systems. Central to this work was strengthening, or in some
cases establishing for the first time, collaboration between general and special educators to break
down the silos leading to exclusion for our priority population. This was a focus of efforts at all levels of
the education system — from classrooms to school leadership teams to district office decision-making
bodies. Several guiding principles became the foundation for these efforts.

When general and special education collaboration
improves, so do learning outcomes.
For the NIC charter networks featured in Opening Doors to Collaboration, it became clear that until
collaboration and alignment between general and special education teams dramatically improved,
results for students would suffer, a perspective supported by research from the National Center for
Learning Disabilities (NCLD).15 In the NIC, where collaboration improved, outcomes followed, and not
only for students with disabilities. When teams became more aligned in their actions and decisions,
they refined their operations and established teaching and coaching structures that centered on the
needs of students with disabilities, for the benefit of all students. At every level, habits changed and
mindsets shifted. These changes removed barriers that may have otherwise limited the achievement of
students in vulnerable subgroups, particularly students with disabilities.
Indeed, in many cases, collaborative structures at both the school and district levels were revised to
include special education expertise and perspectives on teams and in decision-making. Changes to
routines, communication, and culture together built capacity at all levels of school organizations and
reshaped how member schools met the diverse needs of all their students.
The value of a system-wide commitment to collaboration was further highlighted for NIC members
during a March 2022 visit to WISH Charter Schools and CHIME Institute in California, two charter
schools nationally renowned for their inclusive, collaborative approach to supporting students,
particularly those with disabilities. School leaders described “micro and macro” systems that ensure
that structures at every level of their schools — from prep schedules and case management loads,
to co-planning agendas and curriculum sharing structures — are crafted in service of all educators
and staff working together to meet all students’ needs. Following this NIC Learning Tour, participating
organizations refined a diverse range of processes and routines to further develop educators and their
support team members like coaches and school leaders to collaborate in service of meeting the needs
of their most vulnerable students.

The quality of teacher collaboration determines learning
outcome improvement.
Research on inclusive practices repeatedly cites collaboration time as the essential component of
successful collaboration.16 However, as they engaged in this work, NIC members came to realize that
what happens during collaboration time is the deciding factor in the extent to which that collaboration
will drive change. The key is not simply doing it, but the deeply challenging work of doing it well.17
Consequently, NIC members examined models, designed tools, and implemented coaching structures
to improve the quality of collaborative efforts — efforts that both protected time and adopted or adapted
systems and routines to look at student data and plan aligned instruction across educators. Regardless
of the specific collaborative teaming structure in place at each NIC school, NIC members worked
toward more impactful forms of collaboration, building capacity for continued improvement.

Co-teaching with co-planning is the most direct
approach to collaboration.
We considered co-teaching to be the gold standard for collaboration that improves outcomes for our
priority population, and we aspired to establish or improve co-teaching wherever possible. Accordingly,
some of the schools in the NIC implemented and refined co-teaching structures, focusing on effective
co-planning time in order to optimize the presence of two adults sharing a classroom. For schools that
choose to invest in co-teaching, intentional co-planning creates the conditions necessary for teachers to
share their perspectives and ideas with each other, allowing all students greater access to the teachers’
shared expertise.
For example, KIPP Northern California created protected co-planning blocks within their school
schedule, giving teachers time to engage in effective collaboration. Collegiate Academies chose
to implement data-driven co-planning cycles supported by intentional collaboration between special
education leaders and school instructional leadership. Additionally, Collegiate and Green Dot Public
Schools recognized that in order for their co-teachers to
internalize and implement complicated co-teaching models
effectively, they needed to use a gradual release model in
their instructional coaching. By introducing a new co-teaching
model only once the previous one was mastered, Collegiate
and Green Dot coaches supported their teachers’ success.
Other schools supported co-teaching through instructional
coaching as well. STEM Preparatory Schools introduced
a tiered coaching model, varying levels of coaching support
based on the needs of the team as opposed to the individual.
They utilized a lead coach to support each collaborating pair.
Math and Science College Preparatory doubled down on
collaboration, with two coaches collaboratively coaching each
co-teaching pair.

Schools must understand their own unique structures
and needs and work to improve collaboration within
those bounds.
NIC participants all joined the network with their own unique contexts. Some schools were determined
to improve collaboration but recognized that given their context, current resources, or site readiness,
co-teaching was either not feasible or not sustainable. Through the collaborative efforts of the NIC —
including virtual professional learning communities, in-person learning tours, and the sharing of bright
spots and related resources from partners across the community — these participants were able to
identify best practices for collaboration and bring ideas back to their own contexts, where they looked
for ways to adapt, refine, and apply the practices to existing systems. In other cases, teams piloted new
ideas at a smaller scale. No matter their starting point, schools demonstrated a deep commitment to
building and improving collaboration systems, routines, and mindsets, even when traditional co-teaching
was not their model.
For example, at Ednovate, most special educators’ caseloads make co-teaching full-time (for the entire
class period, every time it meets) a challenge. To maximize their time spent supporting students with
disabilities in their general education classes, special educators utilize a “partial co-teaching” model in
which they either co-teach for a full period two to three days out of the week or co-teach for some portion
of a course every day (e.g., the first 45 minutes for the introduction to new material and guided practice).
At Summit Atlas, special educators primarily provide instruction in a separate classroom setting, yet
they also regularly collaborate with their general education peers during co-planning sessions that are
driven by the needs of a data-informed subset of students with IEPs. Focusing on that collaborative
structure has dramatically improved the general education course outcomes for students with disabilities
and built the capacity of general educators to design instruction to meet the needs of all learners.
Improving the ways in which general and special educators collaborate is always positively impactful for
students.18 Regardless of the service delivery models utilized within a school, there are myriad ways in
which this work can occur, and each is worthy of time and resources.

Collaborative Teaming Structures
Schools within the NIC took various approaches to collaboration. Each approach defined specific
structures and routines to allow educators to collaborate effectively, focusing intently on team
membership, clear roles and responsibilities, and student-centered outcomes.

CO-TEACHING
Many of the NIC participants featured in this collection have heavily invested in co-teaching
as a service delivery model for teachers to collaboratively plan for and execute instruction
to meet the needs of all learners in a classroom. Co-teaching structures included regular
time for planning, review of data, and instruction. The deep partnership that is possible
for students and educators in a well-supported co-teaching model creates an exceptional
environment for very high-level collaboration. If co-teaching is sustainable and appropriate
for your school and students with disabilities, we encourage leaders to explore its
implementation.

COLLABORATIVE TEACHING
This collection refers often to collaborating teachers, that is, general and special educators
who share their expertise with one another to best support all students. Although not always
in the same classroom, educators working together to improve the learning outcomes of
a group of students can benefit from using the practices that have been tested and honed
by the schools that participated in the NIC. If traditional co-teaching is not possible in your
school’s context, we encourage leaders to consider how these practices might be applied to
collaborative teaching in some form across instructional settings where students are served.

SYSTEM-LEVEL COLLABORATION
Sometimes, especially at the early stages of collaboration efforts in a school or network, the
most crucial adjustment happens when school leaders establish dedicated collaboration
space between special education and general education curriculum leaders. Oftentimes,
special education leaders are not included in decision-making processes but rather are
informed of decisions after the fact. Improving collaboration at the leadership level sets an
example for others while also strengthening the systems and structures required for systemwide collaboration.
When leaders and teachers invest in partnerships between their general and special education
team members they often see students’ experiences and academic results improve in exciting
ways. Save for Chapter 3: Achieving High-Impact Co-Teaching: A Gradual Release Model,
every practice featured in this collection is applicable across a wide spectrum of collaborative
partnerships. We encourage school leaders to engage expansively with the practices and
guidance, identifying inroads for stronger collaboration that are manageable for them, in their
contexts, at present, and building upon them as skills improve and relationships deepen.

Enabling Conditions
Each of the practices in this collection strengthens and leverages strong general and special education
collaboration in service of improved experiences for Black and Latinx students with disabilities
experiencing poverty. The effective implementation of these practices was made possible because
of three core enabling conditions that were in place in NIC members’ schools: dedicated time,
strategic coaching, and strong professional relationships between educators. Without these three
conditions, the practices’ impact is dramatically reduced.

Dedicated Time
Research shows that when asked what makes their work feasible, special educators in particular cite
time to collaborate with their general education peers.19 Dedicated collaboration time within the school
schedule communicates to teachers that school leaders deeply value and prioritize staff collaboration,
and its protection ensures that collaboration can occur.
Schools in the NIC prioritized building school-wide schedules around meeting times for collaborating
teachers and other supporting team members. They included dedicated time for team formation, team
development, and team coaching, as well as regular, protected planning time that eliminates conflicts,
ideally during the school day. Because such scheduling is so crucial and can get quite complicated,
administrators may want to seek assistance from an outside organization or expert who can guide them
through the process of building that dedicated time into the school schedule.

Strategic Coaching
Collaborating teachers, particularly new teachers, need explicit guidance and training in how to plan
instruction, analyze student work and data, and plan together across their instructional areas of
expertise. Instructional coaching is proven to be beneficial,20 and in the context of collaboration between
general and special educators, coaching takes on a particularly important role. It is essential for effective
collaboration between teachers because it offers the guidance, training, feedback, and modeling
necessary for teachers to build their capacity and skill when it comes to collaboration. Coaches also
provide skill development and accountability that relieves the special education teacher of needing to
be the primary driver of collaboration, reducing teacher burnout and a “your students, my students”
mentality. Furthermore, when general education and special education coaches combine their efforts,
they build their expertise in the other’s area, thus increasing their collective capacity to support the
diverse needs of all students. For more, see How-To: Establish Coaching for Collaborative Teams.

Strong Professional Relationships
While all three enabling conditions are critical to successful collaboration, time and coaching are moot in
the absence of well-established, trusting relationships between collaborating educators.
Trusting, productive relationships play a huge role in educators’ ability to engage in the three layers of
collaboration. Deep collaboration requires all collaborators to know each other and each other’s ways

Chapter 1: Co-Planning for Differentiated Instruction
For collaborating teachers to have as much impact as possible, they must have dedicated co-planning
time focused on ensuring that their upcoming lessons are both accessible and rigorous for all students,
especially their students with disabilities. Teams at Summit Atlas and Collegiate Academies honed
this work over time, identifying the core practices that make efficient and effective use of collaborating
teachers’ often limited time together. This chapter highlights the importance of each of the key
components of co-planning, providing an understanding of the contributions from collaborating teachers
that yield the strongest plans.

Chapter 2: A Collaborative Approach to Student Work Analysis
Once outcomes-driven co-planning is in place, teams are ready to analyze their students’ work to
identify strengths and make necessary instructional changes to benefit students. Collaborative student
work analysis dramatically deepens both teachers’ understanding of their students’ performance and
progress in course content. This second chapter walks readers through Green Dot’s protocol, showing
how both the generalist and the specialist contribute their unique vantage points and skills to deeply
understand students’ work and plan to proactively remove barriers to access, particularly for students
with disabilities.

Chapter 3: Achieving High-Impact Co-Teaching: A Gradual Release Model
For schools utilizing co-teaching as a service delivery model, the final chapter focuses on how teachers
can maximize their shared presence with students. Two educators can accomplish twice what one
could on their own, but shifting to that level of practice is immensely challenging. For teachers to
increasingly employ high-impact co-teaching models, they need targeted, supportive coaching that aids
in their gradual adoption of new models based on students’ needs. Collegiate and Green Dot’s work
provides a roadmap for how other schools and networks can enable their co-teachers to grow in similar
role optimization.

Case Study: How Quarterly Professional Development Cycles Helped a
Network Maximize Data-Driven Co-Planning for Co-Teachers: Green Dot
Public Schools
In order to build the skill, mindset, and habits of collaborating teams, districts need to provide training
and ongoing coaching in co-planning. This case study describes how Green Dot piloted and refined
professional development days for co-teachers to step away from their classroom once a quarter
for a day-long co-planning workshop. These sessions include a standardized student work analysis
protocol for teachers to work through with their coach and choice-driven mini-professional development
sessions on high-leverage models of co-teaching for ongoing capacity building.

Guidance Documents: Team Formation and Coaching
These guidance documents, written for school and network leadership, answer some of the most
common questions leaders face when implementing collaborative practices. They draw on work done
by the schools that participated in the NIC and elevate strong practices that can be used right away.

Literature Review
ABSTRACT
Collaboration between general and special educators across all levels of the school
system has long been seen as a best practice when considering how to serve students
with disabilities effectively. There is evidence in the research base that stronger
collaboration is correlated with better results for both educators and students. One
particular form of collaboration, co-teaching, has emerged as a leading approach to
collaboration at the classroom level. Co-teaching approaches are buoyed by a strong body
of research and have the potential to serve students with learning and thinking differences.
This literature review synthesizes the existing research on educator collaboration broadly
as well as the specific practice of co-teaching, focusing on both defining the practices
and explaining their various uses. Research selected for inclusion in this literature review
includes seminal texts as well as literature published from 2008 to 2023. This review is
designed for school district administrators considering adopting or improving collaborative
practices in their districts.

Introduction
In this literature review, we share a concise overview of collaboration practices and methods with a
particular focus on co-teaching, an emerging best practice and service delivery model grounded in
the philosophy of inclusive education. Our purpose is to equip school district staff who are considering
adopting or improving collaborative practices between general educators and special educators with a
summary of the research on collaboration so that staff can make informed and thoughtful implementation
decisions for their districts. The literature review was conducted by Marshall Street, a coalition of
educators working to systematically improve opportunities for students across the United States.
This literature review is part of a larger collection of improvement tools for districts. The Networked
Improvement Community for Students with Disabilities (NIC), a group of 10 public charter school
networks serving more than 75,000 students nationwide, launched in 2019 to make dramatic gains in

What is educator collaboration?
Collaboration in education has been defined as a “mutual effort to plan, implement, and evaluate the
educational program for a given student” (Correa et al. 2005 in Hernandez 2013) and as “individuals
with diverse expertise working together to achieve mutually agreed upon objectives” (Snell and Janney
2000 in Hernandez 2013). When collaboration focuses on supporting students with disabilities, it takes
on many forms, including both direct and indirect collaboration. In direct collaboration, a specialist
works directly with a child. In contrast, in indirect collaboration, a specialist works directly with another
teacher or teachers, taking on an advisory, consultative, inspirer, or sparring partner role (Hansen et al.
2020), including sometimes working in a co-teaching capacity.
Sometimes, collaboration in schools happens within larger teams, where general and special educators
work together to make decisions about students (Friend et al. 2010; Blackwell and Rossetti 2014;
Education Resource Strategies 2021) during dedicated collaboration times. Ripley (1997) describes
how collaborative planning must happen at the district, building, and classroom levels for schools to
achieve inclusive practices. However, much of the literature about collaboration in education eventually
hones in on co-teaching as a best practice service-delivery model (Friend 2008; Villa et al. 2005;
Ronfeldt et al. 2015; Scanlon and Baker 2012). As the most discussed service delivery model situated
within collaboration, literature on co-teaching is covered in more detail later in this review.

Why is Special and General Educator Collaboration
Important?
In the literature, the most discussed reason for educators to collaborate is that it leads to improved
teacher practice and knowledge. As teachers collaborate, they can share specialist approaches,
forms of knowledge, and various skills and strategies (Hansen et al. 2020; Winn and Blanton 2005).
Additionally, as educators spend time working with one another, they begin to develop shared beliefs
about students, teaching, and learning (Winn and Blanton 2005). In a study of over 8,000 teachers,
Ronfeldt et al. (2015) found almost 90% of teachers surveyed viewed being on an instructional team
as either helpful or very helpful and specifically saw collaborating on the development of instructional
strategies to be the most useful. Teachers in another study reported that collaborative planning allowed
them to include instructional accommodations in their lessons by balancing the content-area expertise
of general educators with pedagogical knowledge and specialized supports from special educators
(Scanlon and Baker 2012). There is even evidence that when teachers change schools, moving to
a school with more collaborative practices, the rate at which their teaching skill improves increases
(Ronfeldt et al. 2015).
There is also evidence that stronger educator collaboration leads to improved student outcomes.
Ronfeldt et al. (2015) found that schools with instructional teams that engaged in better collaboration
had higher achievement gains in both math and reading than schools that did not collaborate as well.
This supports previous research showing that schools with higher levels of collaboration also have
higher levels of student achievement (Goddard et al. 2007; Goddard et al. 2010 in Ronfeldt et al. 2015).
Collaboration between educators often leads to greater inclusion of students with disabilities in the
general education classroom (Bausela 2003, Boavida and da Ponte 2011, and Lozano et al. 2010 in

Sanahuja-Gavalda et al. 2016), and learning outcomes for students on the autism spectrum are more
positive in mainstream placements than in specialist placements (Sanahuja-Gavalda et al. 2016).
Beyond specific gains in student achievement, collaboration can enhance the development of important
life skills and abilities of students with disabilities (McWilliam and Young 1996 and Barnes and Turner
2001 in Hernandez 2012).

What is Co-Teaching?
A widely used form of collaboration, co-teaching is a service-delivery option that supports the
philosophy of inclusive education (Alnasser 2020; Anderson 2008). The most commonly cited definition
of co-teaching in the literature is that of Friend (2008, 9): “Co-teaching occurs when a general education
teacher and a specialist — often a special education teacher, reading specialist, speech/language
therapist, or bilingual teacher — work as partners to teach a diverse group of students.” Further, coteaching presumes:
• Instruction for diverse learners primarily occurs concurrently and within the same
classroom space. (Basso and McCoy 2007 in Anderson 2008; Friend 2008; Friend and Cook
2003 in Simmons and Magiera 2007; Gately and Gately 2001; Cook and Friend 1995 in Murawski
and Swanson 2001).
• Teaching partners are both two equally licensed adults. One is a generalist with expertise
in the grade band or content area and the other is a specialist who is licensed as a specialist
education teacher, reading specialist, or the like, whose background is in adapting and modifying
instruction for students with special needs and learning disabilities (Basso and McCoy 2007 in
Anderson 2008; Friend 2008; Friend and Cook 2003 in Simmons and Magiera 2007; Cook and
Friend 1995 in Murawski and Swanson 2001).
• Both teachers are jointly responsible for the academic outcomes of all the students in the
classroom. (Basso and McCoy 2007 in Anderson 2008; Friend 2008; Gately and Gately 2001;
Keefe and Moore 2004; Murawski and Swanson 2001).
• Both teachers engage equitably in joint collaborative planning, instruction, assessment,
and reflection. (Dove and Honigsfeld 2018; Friend 2008; Gately and Gately 2001; Murawski and
Lochner 2010).
Importantly, Dove and Honigsfeld (2018) and Anderson (2008) note that co-teaching is NOT:
• The simple placement of two teachers in a classroom such as:
– one person teaching while another person has a prep period,
– one teacher conducting a lesson while the other watches,
– one teacher dictating over the other what is to be taught and how it is to be taught, or
– one person being assigned as a tutor.
• The combining of two different content classrooms (e.g., ELA and History).
Co-teaching typically refers to the service-delivery model of collaboratively educating students with
disabilities alongside their general education peers, though co-teaching is also being implemented
for multilingual learners and gifted and talented students (Friend et al. 2010). Some students with

and thus may receive instruction in special education settings in addition to the instruction provided in
co-taught general education classrooms (King-Sears et al. 2021).

Why is Co-Teaching Important?
For decades, the public education system in the United States has failed to effectively serve students
with a diverse set of needs, particularly students with disabilities. Inclusion classrooms — classrooms in
which students with and without disabilities are served together — are both an educational requirement
and a best practice for serving students with disabilities due to governmental mandates such as No
Child Left Behind (NCLB), the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA), and the Every Student
Succeeds Act (ESSA) and research showing academic gains from inclusion classrooms (Alnasser
2020; Brendle, Lock, and Piazza 2017; Cole et al. n.d.; Cramer et al. 2010; Weiss, Glaser, and Lloyd
2020). Yet, too often, schools integrate students rather than fully including them. Integrated education
places students with diverse needs in general education classrooms without providing them with
additional support to open access to learning. Inclusive education, on the other hand, is the systemic
change of an educational institution to provide an equitable and participatory learning experience for all
students (Hehir, Pascucci, and Pascucci 2016).
According to a recent nationwide survey of education data, 67% of students in special education
receive instruction in general education classrooms for at least 80% of the school day (National
Center for Education Statistics 2023). The large number of students with special needs integrated in
general education classrooms — who, due to the disproportionate rates of Black and Latinx students
being referred for special education services, are often Black and Latinx (National Center for Learning
Disabilities 2020) — leaves educators with the important question of how to implement inclusion
classrooms well (Alnasser 2020; Soslau, Gallo-Fox, and Scantlebury 2019 in Alnasser 2020).
Co-teaching has emerged as a leading strategy for effectively meeting the needs of students with
disabilities in an inclusive environment. While co-teaching is not required as a method of instruction by
governmental mandates (Cramer et al. 2010; DeMartino and Specht 2018), schools across the United
States have embraced co-teaching due to:
• A wide research base on co-teaching
implementation models (Brendle, Lock, and
Piazza 2017; Friend et al. 2010; Kim et al. 2006

et al. 2010 in Brendle, Lock, and Piazza 2017;
Thousand, Villa, and Nevin 2006 in Brendle 2017).
• Co-teaching’s potential to help teachers
simultaneously meet accountability standards
for students with disabilities, prepare students
for postsecondary placement, and provide
instruction to diverse students within the
general education classroom (Basso and McCoy
2007 in Anderson 2008; Cramer et al. 2010).

Limitations of Co-Teaching
Few mentions were made in the literature regarding limitations (limitations are defined as
shortcomings or weaknesses of the approach) of co-teaching, or of collaborative practices in
general. One such mention was that students with disabilities received less specially designed
instruction (ASCD 2015). Additionally, anecdotally, schools transitioning to co-teaching noticed an
increase in academic outcomes for students with disabilities after introducing co-teaching, and
then experienced a leveling off of academic gains after a period of time (ASCD 2015). Notably, for
multilingual co-teaching classrooms, ESOL teachers “felt restricted from using their greatest assets
in the mainstream classroom. In particular, the use of native language and the ability to focus on the
complexities of language development were not leveraged to the highest potential” (McClure and
Cahnman-Taylor 2010 in Bauler and Kang 2020, 4).

While limitations were less frequently discussed in the literature, implementation challenges were
frequently cited. The main challenges of co-teaching include a lack of co-planning time, lack of
administrative support, need for ongoing training/lack of training support, relationship difficulties
between co-teachers, and increased workload for general education and specialist teachers (Alnasser

Because these challenges to implementing co-teaching are significant, they are examined more

Common Implementation Frameworks
The six models of co-teaching as defined by Friend et al. (2010) is the most commonly cited framework
for co-teaching in the literature. An additional framework is Dove and Honigsfeld’s seven models of coteaching for multilingual learners (2018).
Friend et al.’s (2010, 12) six models of co-teaching are listed below. Of the six models, Friend et al.
(2010) notes that models 1, 2, and 3 are far more beneficial to students than models 4, 5, and 6:
1. Station Teaching involves dividing students into three groups and rotating the groups from
station to station, where they are taught by the co-teachers at two stations and working
independently at the third;
2. Parallel Teaching requires each of the co-teachers to instruct half of the students, presenting
the same lesson to provide instructional differentiation and increased student participation;
3. Alternative Teaching involves one teacher providing instruction to the majority of students
while the other teacher works with a small group for remediation, enrichment, or assessment;
4. Teaming requires that co-teachers lead large-group instruction, with both teachers lecturing,
representing different viewpoints and multiple methods of solving problems;
5. One Teach, One Assist, also identified as Supportive Teaching, involves one co-teacher
leading instruction, while the other teacher circulates among the students providing individual
assistance;
6. One Teach, One Observe involves one of the co-teachers leading large-group instruction
while the other teacher gathers academic, behavioral, or social data on specific students or the
class group.
Dove and Honigsfeld’s (2018) seven models of co-teaching for multilingual learners closely map
to Friend et al.’s (2010) six models of co-teaching. The chart that follows is a crosswalk of the two
frameworks, combined with corresponding benefits and challenges compiled by Anderson (2008, 39).

The literature notes that the best results for students occur when teaching teams use a variety of
models (Scruggs, Mastropieri, and McDuffie 2007 in Alnasser 2020; Solis et al. 2012 in Alnasser
2020), in particular, station teaching, parallel teaching, and alternative teaching. Despite this guidance,
supportive teaching is the most commonly used co-teaching model, followed by parallel teaching,
because they require less co-planning time (Alnasser 2020; Friend et al. 2010; Keefe and Moore 2004;
King-Sears and Strogilos 2020; Scruggs, Mastropieri, and McDuffie 2007 in Alnasser 2020; Solis et al.
2012 in Alnasser 2020).
Additionally, the literature notes that over-reliance on supportive teaching can have the disadvantage
of placing one teacher in an assistant role, negatively affecting co-teaching efficacy and teacher morale
(Basso and McCoy 2007 in Anderson 2008; Dove and Honigsfeld 2018; Scruggs, Mastropieri, and
McDuffie 2007 in Alnasser 2020) and can lead to student views that students with disabilities are a
separate group and not an actual part of the general education classroom (Embury and Kroeger 2012).

Efficacy of Co-Teaching on Academic Outcomes
and For Whom
There is definitive evidence that inclusion classrooms (either solo with pull-out and push-in services
or co-taught) increase academic outcomes for students with disabilities (Cole et al. n.d.). Because of
the wide variability of implementation, there is inconclusive data regarding the extent to which teacher
practices, including co-teaching, cause or are correlated with the increase (King-Sears et al. 2021;
Hanover Research 2012; Murawski and Swanson 2001). Primarily, this is due to datasets that do not
lend themselves to large-scale standardized research. For example, large datasets reviewed in the
Murawski and Swanson (2001) meta-analysis lacked disaggregated data for co-taught vs. non-cotaught classrooms as well as disaggregated data for students with disabilities compared to students
without disabilities. In addition, the definitions of co-teaching, implementation structures, and fidelity
of co-teaching vary wildly, not only from district to district, but also across individual classrooms within
schools (Hanover Research 2012). Further, many studies lack specific descriptions of the types of
co-teaching approaches used (King-Sears et al. 2021). These study limitations have so far precluded
conclusive results about the efficacy of co-teaching.
In the most recent meta-analysis on co-teaching, King-Sears et al. (2021) compared the academic
achievement of students with disabilities in co-taught classes to the achievement of students with
disabilities in special education settings such as resource rooms or self-contained classrooms. The
study authors conducted a comprehensive search of published and gray literature, which resulted in
26 eligible studies for a total of 3,714 students with disabilities. The analysis found a positive moderate
effect size (g = 0.47) for students in co-taught settings compared to students in special education
settings. Yet, crucially, the studies used for the meta-analysis lacked information about co-teaching
instructional practices and equivalency of students assigned to co-taught or special education settings.
Thus, it is unclear how much of the positive moderate effect size is due to co-teaching, as opposed to
the positive effect inclusion classrooms have on academic outcomes. More research is needed that
compares the academic performance of students with disabilities in solo-taught inclusion classrooms to
those in co-taught inclusion classrooms.

The lack of conclusive evidence of efficacy means there is also a lack of evidence on sub-group
performance, particularly for target populations of Black and Latinx students with disabilities
experiencing poverty. No literature surveyed had subgroup data for Black students, Black students
experiencing poverty, or Black students with disabilities experiencing poverty. Similarly, no literature
surveyed had subgroup data for Latinx students, Latinx students experiencing poverty, or Latinx
students with disabilities experiencing poverty. However, a 2020 study on the effects of co-teaching for
multilingual learners in K–4 classrooms in New York found that language proficiency scores increased
over two years of co-teaching instruction (Bauler and Kang 2020). As 78% of the United States’
multilingual learners are Hispanic (National Center for Education Statistics 2021), this study may be
used as a partial proxy, though the study did not report on disability or income status.
Although the meta-analyses did not provide conclusive evidence of the efficacy of co-teaching, they
also did not provide conclusive evidence that co-teaching, regardless of fidelity, has negative effects on
academic outcomes (Murawski and Swanson 2001; King-Sears et al. 2021). Thus, co-teaching appears
to be a strategy for effectively implementing inclusion classrooms with limited downsides and the
potential for positive academic gains when executed with integrity, though more research is needed.
Given the limited downsides and potential benefits of co-teaching, it is important to note that Black
and Latinx students in special education programs are more likely to be removed from inclusive general
education classrooms than their white peers (de Valenzuela et al. 2006, Fierros and Conroy 2002,
and Sullivan 2011 in Waitoller and Artiles 2013), leading to situations where Black and Latinx students
with disabilities are less likely to see the benefits of collaboration and co-teaching. This is especially
troubling because co-taught classrooms often implement aspects of Universal Design for Learning
(UDL), which helps to make them inclusive not just of students of varying abilities, but also of different
races and socioeconomic statuses as well (Sullivan and Thorius 2010). By removing Black and Latinx
students with disabilities from these co-taught classrooms, schools are pushing students of color further
from opportunity.

Necessary Conditions for Successful
Implementation
Successful collaboration requires a school- and district-level belief in the value of true inclusion. With
this vision, districts and schools can create the policies, staffing, structures, schedules, professional
development, and culture changes that undergird effective collaborative relationships and processes,
including but not limited to co-teaching.
High-quality, high-impact co-teaching and other forms of collaboration can be achieved through
thoughtful strategic planning on the part of districts, school administrators, and teachers (Ripley 1997).
When time and intention are used to purposefully plan for the structure of multiple adults working
together across a school community, excellence is possible. Yet implementing collaborative practices
with fidelity can be difficult, especially because of the traditional siloing of general and special educators,
particularly in teacher education (Winn and Blanton 2005). The literature specifically highlighted several
challenges to implementing co-teaching effectively. When these challenges were not addressed,

Conclusion
In conclusion, collaboration between general and special educators is crucially important for schools
that are working to become more inclusive of all learners. Within the broad landscape of collaborative
practices, co-teaching is currently the best strategy for effectively meeting the needs of all students
within classrooms. Co-teaching can be effective for a myriad of students with learning and thinking
differences, including students with disabilities and multilingual learners; thus, creating a district culture
of co-teaching can benefit many subgroups of diverse learners. District staff looking to adopt or improve
their collaborative practices must recognize that many system-level changes — including scheduling,
professional development, culture, and staffing — will be necessary to implement co-teaching, coplanning, and other collaborative teams effectively.
While the literature has not yet been able to provide conclusive evidence of co-teaching efficacy on
academic scores, the literature confirms the efficacy of inclusion (solo and co-taught) classrooms
and suggests collaboration has positive benefits on classroom culture, student sense of belonging,
and teacher job satisfaction, which are all foundational elements to increasing outcomes for diverse
learners. In short, collaboration between educators traditionally tasked with serving students in silos
has the potential, if the necessary conditions are met, to dramatically increase academic outcomes for
diverse learners. If nothing else, co-teaching is an approach to meeting the inclusion requirements of
IDEA and ESSA with a whole host of benefits for both students and teachers.
Best practices for collaboration in schools and networks are discussed in the companion collection,
Opening Doors to Collaboration.

Components of a Strong Co-Planning Meeting
The goal of co-planning is to ensure that collaborating teachers develop high-impact, data-driven
adaptations to lessons that meet the needs of all students by drawing on the expertise of both the
general and special education teachers. Effective co-planning leverages four key components —
alignment, reflection, removal, and selection — to meet this goal.

Bringing this Practice to Life
CO-PLANNING FOR DIFFERENTIATED INSTRUCTION
AT SUMMIT ATLAS
Planning for the most vulnerable subgroups of students positively impacts all students. At Summit Atlas,
co-planning with a focus on students with disabilities has led to improvements in academic outcomes for
students without IEPs, too. Strong co-planning that focuses on meeting the needs of students who struggle
supports mindsets and skills that adapt lessons to improve comprehension for all learners.

At Summit Atlas in Seattle, WA, Hannah, a special education teacher, and Jess, a general
education history teacher, meet biweekly to co-plan. Each week, they focus on a new subset of
Jess’s 21 students with IEPs. As a result of their collaboration in the spring of 2023, the percentage
of students demonstrating mastery of the course’s focal cognitive skills nearly doubled, increasing
from 36% to 67%.
The following case study demonstrates how their use of four key co-planning components —
alignment, reflection, removal, and selection — leads to strategic and aligned instructional planning
that meaningfully improves outcomes for students with disabilities.

Hannah and Jess begin their meeting by reviewing the data across Jess’s two history classes, 11th
grade US History and 12th grade AP Government. Based on proficiency levels, they agree to focus
on 12th grade students with disabilities. More students are struggling in that class, and they are
approaching critical, end-of-high school assessments.

Step 2: Enable Teacher Investment
Co-planning requires teachers to learn many new skills, from strong collaboration to co-construction of
aligned plans that address a wide variety of students’ needs. A crucial component of leveraging coplanning as a viable practice is being realistic about and attentive to teachers’ other responsibilities,
making sure that they have the time to dedicate to this practice.
At Summit Atlas, the team approached co-planning with a deep commitment to change for both
students and educators. School leaders recognized evidence of a lack of shared ownership for
supporting students with disabilities and saw that special educators, in particular, were stretched too
thin to have maximum impact on their students’ experiences and outcomes. At the root of the problem,
they realized, was a lack of clear roles and responsibilities for general and special educators.
Leaders knew that in order to invest teachers in the work of cross-team collaboration they needed to
ensure that each person understood their role within the partnership. By doing that work, teachers were
able to reprioritize how they worked together and to delve deeply into co-planning. By leveraging the
expertise of special educators, the team was able to build general educators’ capacity to better serve
students with disabilities in courses where they spend most of their time. Subsequently, Atlas saw
significant improvements in outcomes for all students.
At Ednovate, network and school leaders invited collaborating teachers to engage in a nine-week cycle
of coaching focused on co-planning. By focusing on a cohort of three pairs who were highly invested in
the practice, the experience was well received, led to notable student improvements, and is now being
expanded to more teacher pairs in the network.

Step 4: Prioritize a Campus Culture of Collaboration
The most common barrier to successful co-planning is that time alloted for co-planning is not held
sacred and used exclusively for that purpose. School leaders can remove this barrier by building a
campus culture and commitment to collaboration time. Training all staff in the vision and purpose of
collaboration time at the start of the year can help team members understand its intended use and
prioritization. It may also be helpful to establish accountability measures to ensure co-planning blocks
are used as intended.
Strong accountability measures may include the following:
• Co-planning times are held sacred. In other words, other required meetings that collaborating teachers
must attend, such as department or IEP meetings, are not scheduled during co-planning times.
• Collaborating teachers are assigned substitute coverage only during free prep periods, not during
co-planning blocks.
• Teachers' workloads are regularly evaluated so that non-essential commitments may be eliminated,
freeing up time to adequately prepare for co-planning meetings.
• Routines exist to check that co-planning time is focused on lessons.
• Teachers use the block and protocol as intended for instructional planning.

The Need for High-Level Plans
Co-planning is strongest when it is focused on the coming one to two weeks’ lessons. This allows
collaborating teachers to discuss and make adaptations that meet specific student needs rather than
speaking in generalities. This is only possible when both teachers are clear, in advance of the meeting, on
what is coming next for students. When this is not the case, co-planning meetings frequently devolve into
lesson creation meetings (which, because of limited time, do not involve detailed accommodations and
adaptations for students based on needs) or generic discussions about strategies that are theoretically useful
but not practical.
As the instructional leaders of school buildings, school leaders play a critical role in the effectiveness of coplanning. It is important that both collaborating teachers have access to course curriculum and scope and
sequences, whether through the procurement of high-quality instructional materials or via the provision of
time and coaching support to teachers who are crafting course plans. Co-planning leans on these materials
for smaller chunks of instructional planning and is best executed when high-level lesson plans are internalized
and shared between co-planning teachers at least 48 hours in advance of co-planning meetings. For the
purposes of co-planning, shared plans should, at minimum, consist of each lesson’s objective, end-oflesson assessment task, and lesson activities.

Lesson-focused collaboration between the general and special educators supporting
all students, especially those who are Black and/or Latinx, have a disability, and are

acknowledge the power of breaking down the silos that exist in our schools and creating a
holistic team that has the ability to do more for students together than alone.

Get Started!
Ultimately, the most important action educators can
take is to start co-planning. Launching the process will
uncover what works, and what doesn’t, in a school’s
context, which can lead to revisions and adaptations to

What Does Co-Planning Look Like for Co-Teachers?
Co-planning is critical to the learning of students with disabilities regardless of how their specialized
services are provided. It can and should be used for all collaborating teachers. Co-planning for coteachers requires a unique focus on which model(s) of co-teaching they will select, based on their
students’ data, to best support learning.
The following example illustrates what a co-planning meeting can look like for co-teachers.

CONTEXTUAL BACKGROUND
Maria is a ninth grade English Language Arts (ELA) teacher and Samuel is a special education
teacher supporting a caseload of ninth graders in our priority population. Together, they share a
section of co-taught ELA 9. They meet every Tuesday for 45 minutes during second period in their
classroom.

PRE-WORK (48–72 hours prior)
On Friday morning, two business days before their Tuesday meeting, Maria sends Samuel a draft
of the plans for the coming week so that Samuel can review, annotate, and internalize the content.
Samuel begins to think about ways in which students on his caseload might need support based on
the lessons’ content.
Both teachers complete the exit tickets students will take after the next lesson. This ensures that
they are aligned on what content mastery looks like, have exemplars to use for grading, and can ask
any questions on content they don’t completely understand.
Both teachers asynchronously collaborate on adding details to their co-planning agenda, which is
based on a standard protocol their school uses for co-planning meetings.

THE MEETING
ALIGN on the lessons’ content.
After exchanging pleasantries, Maria and Samuel begin their co-planning meeting by discussing the
upcoming week’s lessons. Samuel surfaces his confusion about whether or not students must cite
evidence directly to show mastery of the standard, to which Maria explains they must. Maria clarifies
that the students will be working on one piece of writing from Monday through Friday, revising as the
week goes on.
The teachers then review the five upcoming end-of-lesson assessments, discussing the key
knowledge and skills students will need in order to demonstrate content mastery.

REFLECT on student data.
Maria and Samuel look at last week’s exit tickets. They discuss the trends they notice in terms of
which students are struggling with each concept. They identify how these trends will affect future
lessons and which areas may need re-teaching or intervention for certain students who need the
content taught differently.
Maria shares what she is thinking about in terms of accommodations needed based on previous
lessons and experiences. Samuel shares additional, relevant accommodations and shares how
different students’ disabilities may need additional support and attention based on this week’s content.
The co-teachers then reference their learner profile data to ensure they’re accounting for their
students’ strengths and interests.

REMOVE barriers to access.
Maria and Samuel look back at each day’s lesson plan and adjust new content delivery and practice
based on the needs that they saw students demonstrate through their data.
This week, students will be developing arguments and organizing evidence for their thesis
statements. Maria and Samuel determine that a group of students will need additional support and
feedback to revise their claims, while other students begin the next step. They also agree that a
scaffolded organizer will help students who struggle getting started. Maria pulls a scaffold from a
previous project, and Samuel agrees to revise it for this project.
Finally, the co-teachers identify students who are prepared to advance more quickly and agree that
these students should also be working on finding counterevidence for their thesis statements.

SELECT high-impact, optimized co-teaching models.
After reviewing each day’s final plans, Maria and Samuel determine that a stations-model of
co-teaching will work best for the first three days of instruction, allowing them to create highly
targeted, small groups that each teacher monitors. Maria will work with a small group to help them
revise their claims while Samuel monitors and supports another station focused on developing topic

Additional stations will provide opportunities for the students with more advanced work to develop
counterarguments and for other students to engage in peer feedback.
The co-teachers reflect on their conversation, agree on next steps, and close out their meeting.

Overcoming Barriers to Implementation:
Key Leader Moves and Mindsets
The need to expedite growth for Black and Latinx students with disabilities experiencing poverty is
significant. At the same time, school leaders are often faced with difficult choices about where and how
to allocate resources to support educators to enable that progress. Given co-planning’s efficacy for all
students, particularly those with disabilities, we encourage leaders to reflect on which of the following
barriers may impact their implementation of co-planning and to seek out the aligned resources to
support next steps forward.
SPECIAL EDUCATION IS VIEWED AS A “SEPARATE” STREAM OF WORK WITHIN A SCHOOL
RATHER THAN A PRIORITY FOR GENERAL EDUCATION DEPARTMENTS OR TEACHERS.
• Provide staff with literature focused on the value of all educators supporting students with special
needs; create time and space for the school community to discuss implications for their school
community; and give examples of proof points from the same or similar contexts.8
• Intentionally create and invest the entire school team in a vision for inclusive co-planning that places
responsibility for the success of all students, including those with disabilities, on all educators.
• Generate explicit roles and responsibilities for general and special education teachers outlining
how each is responsible for contributing to instruction that supports the learning of students with
disabilities, recognizing the specific expertise of special educators.
COMPLIANCE IS PRIORITIZED OVER INSTRUCTIONAL PLANNING AS A FOCUS FOR
SPECIAL EDUCATORS.
Create alternative models of staffing for case management, such as the following:
• Dispersing case management responsibilities across a broader set of staff, including members of
the school support team (assistant principals, instructional coaches, special education department
chairs, etc.). This might include balancing timing needs, recognizing that not all cases require the
same amount of dedicated time.
• Strategically aligning special education teachers’ IEP caseloads to include only students for
whom they co-plan and/or co-teach, thus minimizing the amount of relationships and specialized
knowledge they are responsible for.
• Creating a school schedule in which all case managers have consolidated, dedicated time for case
management. For example, scheduling such that on Fridays, case managers have the full morning
to focus on IEP work and meetings. Remaining prep time during the week is then allocated to
instructional work such as co-planning.
CO-PLANNING REQUIRES GUIDANCE AND COACHING FOR ADULTS TO WORK
EFFECTIVELY TOGETHER.
• Incorporate team formation activities into the preservice schedule for any teachers who will coplan, enabling them to begin building a trusting relationship early on.
• Prioritize coaching focused on co-planning for a subset of collaborating teachers. Consider building
the practice within their partnership to ensure strong capacity and confidence before moving on
to other teachers. For additional guidance, see How-To: Establish Coaching for Collaborative
Teams.

Why is Student Work Analysis Important?
Black and Latinx students with disabilities experiencing poverty have been underserved for decades.
To reverse the effects of learning and opportunity gaps, students must receive the learning support
they need, when they need it. When teachers engage in rigorous student work analysis, they are
enabled to meet students’ exact needs in a rapid manner.1

Why it Works
Collaborative student work analysis using an instructionally focused protocol meets the needs of Black
and Latinx students with disabilities experiencing poverty by:
BUILDING EDUCATORS’ CAPACITY TO UNDERSTAND ROOT CAUSES
OF MISUNDERSTANDINGS
Reviewing student work with an explicit focus on students’ “misconceptions, errors and learning
difficulties” allows teachers to identify the best instructional practices to address said needs. Doing this
review with a colleague who has a different area of expertise allows a teacher to see a wider and more
nuanced set of root causes.2 This was demonstrated by collaborating teachers in the NIC, who, after
multiple rounds of collectively engaging in a student work analysis protocol, began to “take on” each
other’s lenses. This allowed them to expedite students’ growth because they were gradually learning
from one another and “thinking” like their colleagues.
ENSURING CRITICAL ALIGNMENT BETWEEN TEACHERS
Collaborative work analysis helps teachers gain a shared understanding of grade-level rigor so that
both teachers can teach toward it. This makes instruction from each teacher and toward IEP and
course goals more efficient and more impactful.
EXPLICITLY ACKNOWLEDGING THE IMPACT OF STUDENTS’ LEARNING NEEDS
ON THEIR WORK
By including specific knowledge of students’ learner profiles and IEPs (including their disabilities and
accommodations) in the analysis of student work, educators reduce blind spots around the “why” of
students’ approach to content and learning activities.
LEVERAGING MULTIPLE FORMS OF DATA
Focusing regular analysis on students’ work in addition to quantitative data allows teachers to
supplement numbers with rich, illustrative examples of students’ work and processing.3 For many
teachers, this can reinvigorate their investment in data-informed instruction as it is highly studentcentric.4

Bringing this Practice to Life
INTEGRATING STUDENT WORK ANALYSIS INTO ROUTINES
AT GREEN DOT PUBLIC SCHOOLS CALIFORNIA
In 2020–21, the principal at Green Dot Public Schools’ Ánimo Watts College Preparatory Academy (“Watts”)
came into her role with a deep investment in engaging teachers in student work analysis in service of
proactively addressing student needs. As she was iterating on that practice, co-teachers were simultaneously
beginning to participate in quarterly co-planning days.
A year later, though survey data showed that teachers loved the co-planning days, student outcomes data
continued to display large achievement gaps. The improvement team hypothesized that, perhaps, teachers
weren’t making substantial changes to their instruction based on student work. They grappled with how
to address this discrepancy without overwhelming teachers with a “new” initiative and completed a larger
stepback to look at their performance data. This led the team to identify a bright spot in grade improvements
at Ánimo Watts. Upon investigation, the team learned that the site had adopted a new student work analysis
practice at the school that worked well with the schoolwide collaboration and coaching structures. Based on
data and stakeholder interviews, the team decided to adopt Watts’s student work analysis protocol (SWA) for
further iteration and spread across programs.

In the fall of 2022, Green Dot made the SWA the central focus of the professional development
hour that launches each co-planning day. The focus on student work analysis has three core goals:
• Ground teachers’ collaboration time in students’ learning
• Provide a direct link to the subsequent co-planning time
• Enable teachers to intentionally plan to address the newly uncovered needs

How to Implement this Practice
Before School Begins
Step 1: Determine Analysis Cadence
Regular student work analysis dramatically improves the quality of student interventions when paired
with collaborating teachers’ co-planning time. As school leaders are working to carve out time for
multiple collaborative practices, they may consider creative ways to structure in student work analysis
time, such as:
• using a portion of regular professional development time for collaborating teachers to meet;
• supporting teachers to use 5–10 minutes of longer instructional blocks for daily work analysis; or
• having collaborating teachers use an alternating schedule for their weekly common prep,
focusing on student work analysis in week A and co-planning in week B.
Note: This structure does not work for co-teachers who need to co-plan weekly.

Step 2: Select a Student Work Analysis Protocol
Having a standardized protocol is essential to help teachers understand how to analyze student work.
School leaders can choose to adopt a strong, existing protocol, such as Green Dot’s, or they might
modify or create their own protocol. Successful protocols include the following elements:

Step 4: Establish
Coaching Support
The impact of coaching on the efficacy of
student work analyses is highly beneficial. When
a supportive third party joins the discussion,
collaborating teachers can be pushed to amplify
their individual expertise in reviewing students’
work and to think more creatively about how to
address the root causes of students’ needs.
Before launching this practice with collaborating

the initial success of the collaborative student

Ideally, coaches join every student work

• as the protocol is launching at their site, or
• if either collaborating teacher is very new to

Coaches join meetings monthly or quarterly:
• if coach availability and portfolio do not

• once teachers are effectively going through the protocol with minimal facilitation.
When coaching collaborative teaching teams in the use of student work analysis, there are two common
needs for coaches to keep in mind to ensure teams leverage their individual and collective expertise
and knowledge of students:
• Push teachers to “notice and wonder” deeply. A coach at Green Dot noticed that, when
learning how to use the SWA protocol, collaborating teachers often have the inclination to move
quickly to instructional next steps. By encouraging collaborating teachers to linger on the notice
and wonder step and go deeper in the analysis of students’ work — especially through each
teacher’s unique lens and expertise — the collaborating teachers have more time to bounce ideas
off of one another. This back-and-forth builds rapport and understanding and gets to the most
accurate and pressing needs for students’ learning.
• Inquire about accommodations. Coaches should ensure that students have received their
appropriate accommodations before the analysis begins. This inquiry creates a space for
necessary dialogue to occur if accommodations are not being provided, and also ensures that
teachers are looking at the most accurate representation of students’ learning.

Collaborative Student Work Analysis Protocol
STEP 1: CELEBRATE SUCCESSES
Collaborating teachers reflect on ways in which their collaborative student
work analysis and planning has positively impacted their practice and their
students’ outcomes. Teachers ground themselves in the work’s criteria for
success and revisit the instructional support that was provided prior to and
during the work sample or assessment, including accommodations.
STEP 2: REVIEW DATA
Collaborating teachers review each representative set of students’ work
and elevate what is seen in the work itself without planning solutions. Both
teachers are taking an investigative stance, seeking to note objectively what
they see, and an inquisitive stance, seeking to determine why and how
these results came about. They use guiding questions to push themselves to
elaborate on noticings and wonderings, identifying causes of strengths that
can be replicated and using their unique expertise to get to the root causes of
misconceptions or challenges.
STEP 3: PLAN INTERVENTIONS
Collaborating teachers identify specific next steps for each group of students
reflected in the representative samples, ensuring that there is an explicit focus
on the needs of students with disabilities. Teachers articulate what they want
to see next in students’ work.
Collaborating teachers discuss what they need to address next, based on the
root cause reflections and aligned to their articulated foci, as well as how they
can best proceed in ways that are accessible to students.
STEP 4: ASSIGN NEXT STEPS
Collaborating teachers explicitly connect their proposed intervention
responses to their next steps for planning and instruction, confirming what role
and specific responsibilities each will take to enact their plans.

Collaborative student work analysis has benefits beyond highlighting students’ most pressing needs. The

should be expected of their students, regardless of IEP status.

working with a coach to help them optimize
their collaborative partnership. Prior to
that time, Katie was inconsistently scoring
work and inputting the information into
Gradebook, their grades platform, but Jerry
neither helped with evaluating work nor

Jerry’s class saw an increase in their course
pass rate from 79% to 97%.

A COLLABORATIVE APPROACH TO STUDENT WORK ANALYSIS

Summary
The practice of analyzing student work to inform future instruction is relatively common in schools.
However, when general and special teachers collectively analyze students’ work through their unique
lenses, they are able to build a rich and detailed understanding of strengths and needs in a way that a
single educator cannot. In order to best serve the needs of all students, particularly Black and Latinx
students with disabilities, instruction needs to be highly tailored and targeted to individual student
needs. When students’ work and learning are evaluated simultaneously by content experts (general
education teachers) and accessibility experts (special education teachers), students’ needs are
identified more precisely, and aligned supports are crafted with both high rigor and access in mind.
All teachers are then able to more effectively respond to the needs of individual students as well
as the whole class. This response might look like implementing high-impact small groups based on
academic data, rather than simply grouping students based on their ability designations. Alternatively,
this collaborative student work analysis could lead educators to identify important moments for wholeclass instruction. Analyzing student work as a team ensures that all teachers in a classroom are deeply
knowledgeable about student needs and the gaps of the class as a whole. This leads to a greater
feeling of shared responsibility for all learners.

Practice Overview
What is the Practice?
Coaches intentionally train co-teachers to use
the highest-impact models of co-teaching by:
• incrementally introducing co-teachers to
different co-teaching approaches,
• supporting co-teachers to use student data
to drive instructional decisions, and
• developing co-teachers’ skill and
confidence with each new model before
introducing more.

Why is this Practice Important?
The use of high-impact co-teaching models benefits students with disabilities by:
PROMOTING TRUE INCLUSION
A truly inclusive environment exists when all students are together, in the classroom,
getting the support they need to excel.1
ALLOWING FOR TAILORED INSTRUCTIONAL OPPORTUNITIES
Higher-impact models enable teachers to use differentiated instructional methods and targeted
interventions to meet a wider variety of students’ needs.2
DECREASING STUDENT-TO-TEACHER RATIOS
Research shows that lower student-to-teacher ratios can have a significant impact on students’
experiences and outcomes in school, particularly for students in our priority population.3 All highimpact co-teaching models lower the student-to-teacher ratio dramatically.
AFFORDING STUDENTS CONSISTENT ACCESS TO BOTH OF THEIR TEACHERS
Effective use of varied co-teaching models gives all students the opportunity to work with each
teacher, naturally providing them access to both teachers’ expertise and minimizing stigma.
Using an incremental method to coach on high-impact co-teaching models benefits teachers by:
PROVIDING EXPLICIT SUPPORT FOR AN APPROACH THAT IS NOT INTUITIVE
Lower leverage co-teaching models — like One Teach/One Assist — are often familiar, making
them easy for co-teachers to default into without explicit modeling and coaching. Dedicated
coaching acknowledges that sharing instructional responsibility in nontraditional ways is new and
requires direct support.4
TRULY VALUING EACH EDUCATOR
When both co-teachers’ roles are optimized
in the classroom, higher job satisfaction
follows.5 This is particularly relevant in the
current landscape in which special education
teachers are in tremendous demand and
their retention is a top priority for the field.

The Continuum of Co-Teaching Models
Not all co-teaching models are equal in terms of their impact on students’ learning. Marilyn Friend, one
of the foremost co-teaching researchers and developers, explains that models in which both people
have active teaching roles should be designated for “high use” based on their outsized impact on
student outcomes.6

SIX APPROACHES TO CO-TEACHING

Source: Teaching Tolerance Seamless Teaching Toolkit

Often, co-teachers receive professional development on new co-teaching models through
workshops or resources presenting and explaining all six models at once. Unfortunately, this kind of
professional development typically does not explain how student needs should drive the use of each
model. Within the Networked Improvement Community (NIC), leaders found that this professional
development approach was ineffective. Teachers were overwhelmed by new information and lacked
support for planning for the logistics and instruction associated with each model.
By incrementally building teachers’ confidence with more effective co-teaching models, teachers’
openness to incorporating a variety of models grew tremendously. At both Collegiate Academies and
Green Dot Public Schools, where an incremental approach was used by coaches, the prevalence
of One Teach/One Assist decreased significantly in favor of higher-impact, lower student-to-teacher
ratio models.

Bringing this Practice to Life
OPTIMIZING CO-TEACHING AT GREEN DOT
PUBLIC SCHOOLS CALIFORNIA
At Green Dot Public Schools, a coach facilitates the introduction of new co-teaching models to co-teaching
teams. The process begins with a student work analysis, followed by a brief “lesson” about a high-impact
co-teaching model that is appropriate for addressing the identified needs. This approach — as opposed
to professional development that presents every model at the same time — provides co-teachers with a
measured, accessible, and applicable introduction to each new model as it is needed in practice.
The following case study presents an example of this process with 9th grade ELA co-teachers Quentin
(general education) and Jesse (special education), who have been co-teaching for nearly a year. Glynis is a
special education instructional leader at Green Dot who facilitates their conversation, utilizing adult learning
techniques to push the teachers’ practice in a way that is engaging.
In the introduction described below, Quentin and Jesse have just finished their student work analysis and
have determined that students need support on personification. Quentin wants to provide more overt
examples of it so that students are clear on the purpose of the technique and when and how to use it.

Strategically Selecting a New Model
After the group completed the student work analysis, Glynis posed a question: what co-teaching
model would best meet their newly uncovered student needs? Quentin suggested a whole group
approach, because all students need to understand how to apply personification in their own
writing. Jesse called attention to the small group of students who did not yet fully understand the
concept of personification and suggested that perhaps he could work with them during part of an
upcoming lesson on this foundational content.

Glynis listened to the teacher’s analysis and built upon their insights, highlighting that they had an
opportunity to optimize both teachers through a specific co-teaching model: Alternative Teaching
in small groups. To support her suggestion, she shared stories from other Green Dot schools where
the incorporation of groups had led to dramatic improvements in students’ grades.
Jesse and Quentin were enthused by the prospect of trying it out, but also shared that they
generally relied on One Teach/One Assist or Teaming, so the new model would be outside of their
comfort zone. Wanting to ensure that they would be able to gradually incorporate the model, Glynis
recommended that they begin by focusing on just one of their co-taught periods, and by planning
for Jesse to work with their focal group of students — those who would benefit from foundational
content — for 10 minutes. Upon hearing this measured approach, Jesse and Quentin agreed that
they were ready to dig in and learn more.

Glynis opted to prioritize the second need — culture — during their time together in this session.
She coached the duo on how they could talk to their students about small groupings. She shared
effective approaches used by other co-teachers to destigmatize the new structure, including
the language previously used by a different co-teaching pair at another Green Dot high school:
“We’re trying this new thing because we really want to make sure that you’re successful on your
finals. We know there are a lot of kids in our class, and that can be distracting! Small groups help
everyone get the time and attention that they need.” Glynis reassured Jesse and Quentin that,
overwhelmingly, fellow Green Dot teachers found that students were comfortable if they knew
alternative small groups were helping everyone.

Shifting to Logistics
As the meeting neared its end, the group turned their attention to Jesse and Quentin’s other
coaching need — logistics — as well as next steps. To support the team’s practice and
development, Glynis shared a resource focused specifically on effective structures for running small
groups in co-taught spaces. She encouraged Jesse and Quentin to review the resource together
and to note questions or additional needs they found. The following week, Glynis would make sure
that the team felt prepared for their first attempt at Alternative Teaching, as well as meet with them
after their lesson to reflect on the result for students and their own learnings.
This incremental approach to professional development provided Jesse and Quentin with a useful,
applied introduction to a new co-teaching model in a way that was accessible and tied to their
students’ current needs.

Pairing with Purpose

The Role of Trust

Certain models, such as Parallel Teaching, require that
both teachers are able to effectively lead lessons for
the entire class, so it’s beneficial for special education
co-teachers to be focused on a content area that aligns
with their expertise or interests.7 If the special education
teacher lacks sufficient content expertise, they are less
likely to be able to teach a full lesson independently.
At Green Dot’s Ánimo Watts College Preparatory
Academy, co-teachers were initially paired without
prioritizing content expertise. A special education
teacher with years of math experience was working
in an ELA class, while a colleague with a degree in
English was co-teaching math. Both educators spent the
year lacking the content-based confidence to venture
into higher-impact co-teaching methods. When these
teachers’ assignments were flipped, they were able
to implement stronger co-teaching models, increasing
their satisfaction and improving the learning experience
for all students. See Co-Planning for Differentiated
Instruction to learn how strong co-planning addresses
content alignment between co-teachers.

Co-teaching requires a
deep level of trust between
educators, particularly when
using models that require
teachers to relinquish some
control and share instructional
responsibility with their
colleague (e.g., through Parallel
Teaching). For the practices
detailed in this chapter to be
effective, it is imperative that
leaders support co-teachers to
establish strong relationships
built upon mutual respect
and the trust necessary to
fully share responsibility for

How to Implement this Practice
Create Structures for Gradual Release
of High-Impact Co-Teaching
Step 1: Allocate Time for Coaching and Co-Planning
In order for co-teachers to advance their co-teaching practice and effectively utilize new, higherleverage co-teaching models, structures and routines need to be in place to support learning,
application, and reflection specific to the practice of co-teaching and the mindsets and skills for
collaborative teaming between educators.
These structures should include time for coaches:
• to train teachers to build knowledge of co-teaching models, along with when and how to use
them in response to formative and summative student data,
• to observe or collaboratively plan and prepare with teachers (taking a gradual release
approach), and
• to debrief and guide reflection.
In addition to regular coaching sessions for co-teaching pairs, co-teachers should be allocated time for
routine co-planning between coaching cycles to work as a team to apply their skills and knowledge with
greater independence and confidence. Regular co-planning lets teachers plan for upcoming lessons,
reflect on students’ needs, remove barriers to access, and incorporate new high-impact models as the
year progresses.
To adequately support this practice, time should be reserved
for the following:
• For coaches, at least 90 minutes per month per
co-teaching pair in each coach’s schedule, to provide
at least 45 minutes for coaching sessions and 45
minutes for preparation and aligned observation.
• For co-teachers, at least one 45-minute coaching
session per month, and at least 45 minutes of
co-planning time per week.

SETTING CLEAR GOALS ON CO-TEACHING MEASURES
Goals should encourage the effective incorporation of higher-impact models. (See The Continuum of
Co-Teaching Models, above.) Set goals related to the following:
• Increased use of high-impact co-teaching models. For example:
– After two weeks of coaching, Team A will decrease their use of One Teach/One Assist during the
introduction to new material from five lessons (of five total) to four lessons per week, replacing it
with Alternative Teaching for students scoring below 50% on the previous week’s lessons.
– After four weeks, decrease further to three lessons; after six weeks, two lessons.
• Improved student outcomes. For example:
– After six weeks of using Alternative Teaching, exit ticket averages for students with disabilities
will increase from 30% to (at least) 50% and for the class overall from 50% to (at least) 65%.
• Co-teachers’ roles during instruction. For example:
– After four weeks, the general educator will lead the alternative group during introduction to new
material for at least one-third of lessons.

Step 3: Begin Coaching Session with Student Data
Different models of co-teaching (and the groupings used within them) serve different instructional
needs. By beginning a coaching session with an analysis of students’ most recent data, coaches can
help to ensure that teachers are building their capacity with a new method that will effectively address
the needs presented. This analysis could be either a student work analysis or a review of other
recent quantitative and qualitative data.

Step 6: Implement Short-Term Observation and Coaching Cycles
It is imperative that coaches observe and debrief with co-teaching teams as they try new co-teaching
models, swiftly addressing barriers, gathering data, and celebrating successes. Quick and targeted
feedback allows teams to tweak the implementation of models and gradually build their skill and
knowledge based on what has previously been tried.
Coaches should also help co-teachers progress monitor as they work through and try each new model:
1. Regularly review student academic outcomes data to assess whether changes to the coteaching model are positively impacting student learning. This ideally happens in weekly coplanning meetings between teachers for more rapid review and response to student data, rather
than monthly coaching cycles.
2. Gather students’ feedback on their learning experiences, inquiring about how the use of
higher-impact co-teaching practices is impacting their student experience (e.g., by administering a
brief, biweekly student survey).
By ensuring confidence and efficacy with one model before moving on to the next, each new structure
becomes an established tool for co-teachers to use. Coaches can help their co-teachers determine
if they are ready to progress to another model, using resources to help assess mastery, such as
Collegiate Academies’ co-teaching models scope and sequence.

Ongoing: Build Teachers’ Cross-Department Knowledge and Skill
For co-teachers to effectively implement co-teaching models, they need to share instructional
responsibility during lessons and communicate rapidly with one another throughout instruction. Further,
both teachers need deep knowledge of course content and accessibility strategies that allows them
to tailor instruction to individual student needs. Special education teachers should receive targeted
professional development that builds their content knowledge and fluency with the course curriculum.
General education teachers should similarly receive development focused on techniques for effectively
supporting students with disabilities. It is important that school leaders and coaches ensure they are
providing co-teachers with this essential training in an ongoing fashion.

Summary
High-impact co-teaching models — specifically Parallel, Alternative, and Station Teaching — allow
co-teachers to leverage their doubled capacity to meet the needs of all students, especially those
with disabilities. These models lower student-to-teacher ratios, allow for more universally designed
instruction, and create opportunities for each educator to have an active, strategic role with students,
thereby ensuring their learning and engagement.
While higher-impact co-teaching models yield stronger outcomes, teachers often experience a variety
of barriers to their implementation: knowledge of their different purposes and components, sufficient
content expertise, time necessary for effective co-planning, and support that helps them develop in all
of the above.
In order to implement these models well, co-teachers need to plan for different types of lesson delivery
as well as more complicated logistics. When coaches gradually introduce co-teachers to new models
and ensure their success with each approach before introducing another, they are significantly more
likely to see confident pairs who optimize their shared presence in the classroom and, in turn, see
students grow.

The Challenge
How can schools best protect and use co-planning time?
Glynis, the improvement lead and instructional coach at Green Dot Public Schools in Los Angeles, CA,
knew that when Green Dot’s co-teaching teams were able to regularly step away from the classroom
and strategically co-plan instruction, learning outcomes improved for their students with disabilities.
Despite this fact, and even though a weekly co-planning period for co-teaching teams was scheduled,
it was increasingly challenging for the co-teaching teams to use the planning periods to actually plan
their instruction. This was especially true during COVID-19, when new demands regularly took over
planning periods and teachers no longer had the flexibility to check in informally throughout their days.
Additionally, Glynis observed that when coteaching teams could carve out a planning
period — whether a weekly planning block or
quarterly planning day — the teams did not
consistently use data to inform the design of
instruction, leading to less effective co-teaching
in the classroom.
She needed a new solution.
Glynis needed to find a way to ensure that
regular planning time for co-teachers was held
sacred, that co-teachers felt supported by
the school in their skill development, and that
co-teachers were deepening their data-driven
instructional planning and co-teaching practices
through bite-sized coaching.

The Practice
Quarterly co-planning days give teachers
much-needed time to co-plan.
Green Dot’s quarterly co-planning days were designed as a pilot to gather teachers for protected time
for development and instructional planning. Now, after several years of implementation and revision, the

most critical for co-teacher success:
1. Completion of a student work analysis protocol. The co-teaching pair starts the day together
with a student work analysis facilitated by their coach (either in-person or virtually), analyzing
student data to inform needs for instruction. The coach guides the teaching pair to select either the
most effective model of co-teaching to meet the needs that appear in the student work analysis

How Green Dot Implemented
the Practice
Creating a Schedule
To begin the pilot, Glynis first tackled scheduling. She collaborated with school leaders to confirm that
co-teachers had common planning days in the school calendar. To ensure co-teaching teams had the
coverage needed to consistently attend quarterly, full-day co-planning, school leadership worked closely
with Green Dot network staff to build a large substitute teacher pool.
Later, with learning from the pilot, Glynis created a systemized process for scheduling co-planning days
that has become part of the infrastructure for the practice. The process requires close collaboration with
school leadership and co-teachers to schedule the days well in advance. It has five basic steps:

Green Dot’s Scheduling of Co-Planning Days
Step 1: Coach sends scheduling options to school site administrators.
Between August and September, Glynis sends site administrators a list of options for co-planning days for
each quarter. The options are based on coach availability, school vacations, other school and network events,
and school culture (for example, Mondays are avoided in order to keep a consistent schedule for students).
Step 2: Site administrators confirm dates.
The site administrators confirm whether the list of optional dates will work for their sites in two waves: by late
August for the first quarter and by mid-September for quarters 2–4.
Step 3: Teachers sign up for co-planning days.
Teachers receive the list of options for co-planning days and select their preferred dates via a Signup
Genius. If the available dates do not work for a teaching pair, they can email the coach directly, who will
coordinate a separate date with the co-teachers and their administrator.
Step 4: Teachers request substitute teacher coverage.
After selecting their preferred dates, co-teachers email their school’s office manager to inform them of the
upcoming co-planning professional development days. The office manager then coordinates with substitute
teaching agencies to secure coverage.
Step 5: Teachers craft coverage plans.
Finally, co-teachers craft coverage plans for any classes they will be missing and reserve a private space in
which to work together during their planning day.

Refining the Co-Planning
Day Structure
In their third year using the practice, and with the new scheduling
system in place, Glynis turned her attention to fine-tuning
structures to help co-teachers build their data-driven instructional
planning practices. The Green Dot team had previously
identified a bright spot in student grades at Ánimo Watts College
Preparatory Academy, which, once investigated, appeared to be
connected to the development and integration of a student work
analysis protocol with school-level teams. Glynis adapted the
pilot quarterly co-planning day structure and coaching agenda to
integrate the student work analysis protocol so that co-teachers
could drive instructional planning from that data. Glynis also
added a mini-lesson component to her morning sessions with
co-teachers that allowed teams to choose to learn more about
an instructional practice they wanted to build. These changes
meant that the professional development provided by co-planning
days aligned to school-level tools and routines, reinforcing their
standard use across teams, and remained highly informed by
and tailored to the unique needs of each co-teaching pair. Coteaching teams then worked to design lessons using the new
practice during their co-planning time later in the day.

How the Student Work Analysis Protocol is
Embedded in the Co-Planning Day
• Step 1: Teachers select recent student work representative of all student subgroups, such as
recent exit tickets, quizzes, or assessments.
• Step 2: Teachers describe the work sample, the context behind the work, and the standards they
aimed to address in their instruction.
• Step 3: Teacher teams review the work samples and descriptions to identify what they “notice” and
“wonder” about the student work.
• Step 4: Teacher teams select a high-leverage co-teaching model that addresses the needs
identified in the student work analysis, and the coach delivers a targeted, on-demand minilesson on the selected model to deepen co-teachers’ understanding and to help ensure effective
implementation.
• Step 5: Utilizing the selected co-teaching model, teacher teams plan instruction and/or
interventions to address the identified student needs.
• Step 6 [Optional]: Teacher teams create an artifact that represents their instructional plans, which
they can use for reflection with their coach later in the day.
For more information on the practice of student work analysis, see A Collaborative Approach to
Student Work Analysis.

The Impact: Spreading Within and
Beyond Green Dot California
The practice of quarterly co-planning days, with embedded student work analysis and customized
professional development, has been a huge success. Since its implementation, class passage rates
of a C+ or higher nearly tripled for students with disabilities.
Further, recent survey results show teachers value the practice. One hundred percent of the
participating co-teachers stated that the day was a constructive use of their time. Additionally,
100% of the co-teachers noted that the co-planning day improved their relationship with their
co-teacher.
Glynis has continued to facilitate quarterly co-planning days to a growing positive reception. One
participant described co-planning days’ many benefits:

“We are able to be much more intentional with our planning. [It’s] really helpful to have
actual sit-down time to strategize and reflect on student learning. With dealing with
new curriculum and new grading practices and policies, it helps to have a thought
partner in how to approach student learning, what to cut, what to include, and how to
include reteach opportunities in the class.”
In addition, quarterly co-planning days have positively influenced the culture across the Green Dot
network. At the end of the 2022–23 school year, staff, administrators, and teachers shared, via survey
results, that co-planning days are considered to be highly valuable and that co-planning is a positive
practice.
This may be because, as Glynis and the co-teachers have observed, the quarterly co-planning days
provide a different type of professional development than what teachers had previously received from
their coaches and administrators, all of whom have different expertise, passions, and needs. Because
co-planning during the week can often be cut short by the length of prep periods or competing priorities,
the quarterly co-planning days provide the teachers with deep, focused practice and support for using
the student work analysis protocol that they may not otherwise
receive. Teachers also appreciated the extent to which their needs
and preferences drove the focus of the professional development
provided within their co-planning days, rather than it being directed
solely by Glynis.
Based on this success, Green Dot will continue to build the culture
of co-planning across the network. In the 2023–24 school year,
Green Dot will spread the practice of co-planning with student
work analysis to two more campuses, increasing the number of
co-teaching pairs from 10 to 18. Additional co-teachers will try
using student work analysis protocols on a more frequent, biweekly
basis. Finally, Glynis will work to determine the most aligned
measurement of efficacy between co-planning days and student
learning outcomes.

Introduction
The push for more inclusive and collaboratively planned and delivered instruction for students with
disabilities has gained significant momentum over the past 20 years. While the theoretical outcomes
of this type of teaching are positive, research shows that putting two teachers together and assigning
them the task of collaboration without careful attention to their professional dynamic leads to an
underutilized partnership.1 This finding is validated by the experience of the charter management
organizations (CMOs) featured in this collection of research-to-impact practices, Opening Doors to
Collaboration.
Effective teaming is a critical enabling condition for the success of collaboration efforts. School teams
within the Networked Improvement Community (NIC) dedicated substantial resources and energy to
ensuring that thoughtful attention was paid to the relationships between pairs of educators implementing
the instructional practices detailed in the collection. By doing so, the CMOs ensured that the ongoing
planning and teaching done by collaborating educators was highly effective and supported by strong,
trusting relationships between the two professionals.
Critical to the success of collaborative teams were the supportive mindsets of the leaders, coaches,
and staff across school and district offices, and the relationships between these professionals and
collaborating educators. The cross-network collaboration and learning within the NIC led school teams
to develop resources to refine the structures and routines for collaboration across their organizations to
proactively support the formation of their teams. This was, in large part, due to the recognition that the
formation of their teams was an essential element of accelerated progress for students. Further, they
found team formation efforts were most dramatically effective when led by school leaders, particularly
principals, in partnership with other leaders at the school and network level.
School leaders can intentionally support the
formation of effective collaborative teams by:
• attending to strategic staffing,
• building shared inclusive beliefs,
• developing trust between collaborating
teachers, and
• establishing clear role delineation.
This guidance document provides school leaders
with recommendations and resources that
have been used to form effective school teams
at Collegiate Academies, Green Dot Public
Schools, KIPP Northern California, and Summit
Public Schools.

Common Questions Leaders Face When Supporting
Effective Collaborative Teams
How do we create a shared vision of strong collaboration between
general and special educators?
ARTICULATE A CLEAR VISION FOR EXEMPLARY COLLABORATION.
Leaders, at either the school or network level, generate a vision for exemplary collaboration between
general and special educators. Ideally, the generation process includes other school-based staff,
particularly general and special education support team members.
ESTABLISH A CULTURE OF STRONG GENERAL AND SPECIAL EDUCATION
COLLABORATION AT ALL LEVELS OF THE SCHOOL.
School leaders develop inclusive structures at all levels of their school, beginning with their leadership
team. Guidance for this collaboration should come from the vision for collaboration discussed above.
At Green Dot’s Ánimo Watts High School, the principal’s weekly instructional team meeting serves
as the incubator for all decisions related to instruction and major school happenings. By including
the special education department chair on that team, the top levels of the school administration are
dismantling the silos between general and special educators and programming.
ESTABLISH CLEAR ROLES AND RESPONSIBILITIES.
Collaborative teams often struggle with a lack of clarity concerning roles and responsibilities in their
partnerships, causing confusion and placing stress on their working relationships. This can lead to
inefficient collaboration, delays in services and core instruction, and widening of existing gaps in
students’ learning. School leaders can prevent these effects by explicitly outlining what general and
special education teachers are responsible for, individually and collectively, within their collaborative
partnership.
Examples of strong guidelines for roles and responsibilities for collaborating teachers include the
following:
• Stetson Associates’ Roles and Responsibilities of Collaborative Teachers: A DecisionMaking Exercise
• Longwood Preparatory Academy’s (a Blue Engine partner school) Responsibilities of CoPlanning and Co-Teaching
• Collegiate’s Livingston Collegiate Academy’s Co-Teaching Expectations
As partnerships are forming, share the roles and responsibilities documents with teams so that they can
incorporate them into their team-building conversations. This is also a valuable tool for coaches to apply
to initial coaching meetings with teams to confirm how the team members will work together to ensure
their collective success for students.

How do we intentionally staff collaborative teams?
HIRE WITH INCLUSIVITY AND COLLABORATION IN MIND.
When hiring, leadership teams have an opportunity to steer their school’s culture toward stronger
collaboration across departments in service of all students’ needs.
When interviewing:
• Share your team’s vision for collaboration, including your rationale for it. Invite candidates to
share their reactions to the vision and what they see as the benefits of this type of partnership.
• Seek examples of strong, professional relationships with colleagues across departmental
lines. How did the candidate establish those relationships? How did they work through challenges
in them?
• Seek examples of collaboration in service of supporting students with special needs. Ask for
examples of how a candidate has collaborated with general or special education colleagues. What
did that collaboration entail? How did they leverage colleagues’ strengths to support students?
PAIR TEACHERS STRATEGICALLY.
Gather and use teachers’ input to create thoughtful collaborative teams.
Using questions such as these (co-created by teams from Green Dot and STEM Preparatory Schools),
seek input from every teacher on your staff about their experience with collaboration, their working style,
and their content comfort and expertise. Use that information to determine which teachers are best
suited to be partnered together.
Prioritize pairing special education teachers in content areas where they feel confident.
Content expertise is consistently cited as a key factor in special education teachers’ ability to be and
feel effective in their collaborative work. Whenever possible, pair them with content that they are
confident with to expedite and strengthen the collaborative process.
Introduce collaborating teachers to one another as early as possible.
Our partner schools found that, whenever possible, early introductions help collaborating teachers
establish rapport and get to the heart of their work sooner.
Maintain strong partnerships over multiple years.
Wherever possible, keep collaborating relationships and partnerships in place over a number of years
to allow individuals to build skill and develop their collaborative practice. During the 2022–23 school
year, co-teachers at Green Dot began to see substantial improvements in their students’ classwork
scores in rooms where co-teachers had been working together for multiple years. Teams cited stronger
professional relationships and an ability to understand and leverage each other’s styles and strengths
as key components of that improvement. At Summit Atlas, general education teachers cited their
multiple years’ worth of collaboration with their special education co-planner as a key factor in their
willingness and ability to seek and take feedback about serving students with disabilities from their
colleagues.

a growth mindset and less experience. To help assess candidates’ shared mindsets and skills, they incorporate
scenario questions like these into their interviews:

Co-Teaching Staffing Best Practices
Students in priority populations — such as the NIC’s focus on students at the intersection of race, class,
and ability — need their schools and classrooms to maximize instruction, particularly in general education
courses where they are expected to master rigorous content. Co-teaching requires a particularly deep level of
collaboration given that two teachers are jointly responsible for the facilitation of an entire class.
School teams must pay special attention when forming co-teaching partnerships, taking key considerations
about mindset, beliefs, and expertise into mind:
• Willingness to collaborate. Co-teaching requires a high degree of collaboration and communication
across the pair of teachers, which some educators are more naturally inclined to than others. It can
be helpful to reflect on teachers’ mindsets by asking whether both teachers are actively invested in
collaborating with a colleague and sharing responsibility for all facets of the classroom, including the
outcomes for all students.
• Classroom culture skills. It is helpful for co-teachers to have a similar level of competence with
establishing and upholding a strong classroom culture. An imbalance in ability or attitude toward
classroom management may create a lead/assistant dynamic, resulting in teachers defaulting to the
low-impact “one teach, one assist” model more frequently. Not only does this lead to lesser outcomes
for students, it also creates a lack of parity in the classroom, which tends to create feelings of frustration
and erodes trust within the relationship.
• Content and teaching experience. Ideally, both teachers have a solid handle on the content and
on their instructional role. Sometimes, staffing realities mean that two less experienced teachers are
paired together as co-teachers. However, whenever possible, administrators should work to pair special
and general educators who are both a few years into their craft. This allows for power dynamics to be
relatively even as both can equally contribute to their shared work.
Green Dot special education administrators created this guide for school administrators to help them think
through a variety of other considerations as they staff co-taught courses.

Introduction
Collaboration between general and special educators has the ability to transform the experience and
learning of both students and teachers.1 However, collaborative work between these two historically
siloed departments is neither intuitive nor easy to achieve. Teachers need specific knowledge and skills
in order to effectively co-plan, co-assess, and for some, co-teach. When collaboration is not supported,
research shows, promising practices fall by the wayside.2 To meet the diverse needs of students, teams
need coaching on their collaboration mindsets, systems, and routines.
Instructional coaching as a form of professional development for teachers has been proven to have a
positive effect on both instruction and student achievement.3 In order to complete the complex task of
effective teaching, all educators, regardless of skill level or experience, need support. Coaching is one
way to ensure the improvement in both instruction and achievement that leaders desire.
The priority population for the Networked Improvement Community for Students with Disabilities (NIC)
— that is, Black and Latinx students with disabilities experiencing poverty — often has significant
learning gaps, and closing those gaps requires educators to leverage a wide variety of expertise.
Leaders must vigilantly support collaborating teachers4 to harness their shared talents so that students
receive the academic rigor they deserve and the supportive instruction they need to fully access it.
Significant time and resources are necessary for collaboration, and coaching ensures that those
allocations yield better results.
Coaching was integral to the success of teachers’
collaboration at schools across the NIC, including
at Collegiate Academies, Green Dot Public
Schools, STEM Preparatory Schools, Summit
Public Schools, KIPP Northern California, and
Ednovate. Teachers enjoyed working together and
had time to do so, but they struggled to leverage
their shared expertise. Once regular, targeted
coaching was added to teachers’ collaborative
work, students’ scores jumped dramatically.
This guide addresses some of the most common
questions leaders face when setting up effective
coaching of collaborative teams, which we define
as supporting general and special education
teachers in their collaborative practices to
ensure that their partnership results in excellent
outcomes for all students, particularly those with
disabilities. Leaders are encouraged to identify
and read the most relevant sections or to read
straight through like a step-by-step guide. The
suggestions and resources in this guide have
been used by NIC school teams to effectively
keep their students with disabilities at the forefront
when planning and teaching.

Common Questions Leaders Face when
Establishing Coaching for Collaborative Teams
How do we determine collaborative teams’ needs for coaching?
The NIC found that all collaborating teacher pairs benefited from coaching on their collaboration
mindsets, systems, and routines, yet there were different levels of need for this coaching. Most often,
collaborating teachers newer to this work required more frequent coaching to teach skills and build
routines for collaboration, while established partnerships needed less frequent coaching focused on
higher-level skill development and strategic problem-solving.
Coaching for collaborative teams is different from one-on-one instructional coaching in its focus on
building effective and efficient partnerships between educators. To define the needs for coaching, the
following actions are essential:
ARTICULATE A CLEAR VISION FOR EXEMPLARY COLLABORATION.
School and network leaders generate a vision for exemplary collaboration between general and
special educators. This vision defines one or more student-centered outcomes, as well as mindsets
and behaviors for collaboration across the team.
GATHER DATA ON CURRENT PRACTICES.
School leaders use their vision for strong collaboration to guide walkthroughs and conversations
with teachers to gather data on current practices and to identify teachers’ strengths and needs. This
data can inform what supports might be universal for all collaborating teachers, and what coaching
supports or strategies might be targeted to specific teaching pairs or subgroups, such as content-area
or grade-level partners.
ESTABLISH SPECIFIC GOALS AND OUTCOMES RELATED TO COLLABORATION.
In order for collaborative practices to receive the attention that they need in order to progress, it
is important for leaders to be clear on what they want the focus of this coaching time and work to
be, keeping in mind both the instructional growth they’re hoping to see and the impact on student
outcomes that they want the growth to lead to. Once this clarity is established, coaches can narrow in
their collaboration-focused coaching on these goals and priorities. For example, a coach might lead a
team toward dual goals of instructional growth and student achievement by focusing their time on the
specific knowledge and skills needed to effectively communicate mid-lesson on shifts in the lesson
design to meet the data-driven needs of learners.

TAKE STOCK OF COLLABORATING TEACHERS’ NEED FOR COLLABORATIONFOCUSED COACHING.
It is important for leaders to be realistic when prioritizing which collaborating pairs receive coaching,
how much collaboration-focused coaching pairs receive, and when the coaching will take place. School
leaders should consider all of the following factors:
• Teacher Content Experience Levels
How much experience does each teacher in the collaborating/co-teaching pair have with their
content area and/or curriculum? If they are newer to either or both, more intensive coaching of coplanning will likely be beneficial.
• Teacher Collaboration Experience Levels
How much experience does each person have working in close partnership with another teacher?
For teachers who are very new to these practices, explicit coaching and facilitation can help
educators work through critical team dynamics and challenges as they arise.
• Course Types
Are there gateway courses for graduation that have been producing vastly different and lower
results for students in our priority population than for students on average? These courses and
their teachers may be excellent candidates for coaching.
• Teacher Mindsets
When considering a teaching pair’s priority level for coaching, consider their mindsets about
collaboration. If either teacher has negative mindsets about general education and special
education collaboration, it is important for leaders to think carefully about that teacher’s
placement and readiness for this work and/or their place at the school.
• Teacher Efficacy
If a teacher struggles significantly with management, curriculum, and/or instruction, they may not
yet be the ideal candidate for this level of intentional and targeted collaboration and coaching.
Rather, they may need to develop their skills with a single coach for the current year and aim to
be ready for a collaborative partnership the following.

EACH PARTNERSHIP.
Leaders determine which collaborating teachers are most in need of collaboration-focused coaching.
This information is then paired, as described in the following section, with an understanding of
coaches’ capacity.

THE COACHING CADENCE OF EACH COLLABORATING PAIR
After determining who is receiving what level of coaching, leaders must determine the cadence of that
support. At STEM Preparatory Schools, leaders “tier” their coaching support based on what the coach
is seeing from the co-teaching team. “Exploring” teachers engage in a cycle every one to two weeks;
“developing” teachers, every two to three weeks; “experienced” teachers, every three weeks. Using
this approach allows school leaders to differentiate the coaching that they provide to their collaborating
teachers, guaranteeing that everyone receives coaching support tailored to their readiness and skill level.
THE DETERMINATION OF WHO WILL COACH
Because coaching for collaboration is often an additional layer of support, it is important for school
leaders to be as expansive as possible in their thinking about who can provide that support. Consider
who else may have valuable expertise that they can lend to this work to help manage the workload,
for example:
• Special education department chairs
• Grade-level team leaders
• Department chairs (if they do not already coach)
• Deans (who have instructional backgrounds and/or significant expertise in special education)
• Curriculum team members or district content specialists
Once leaders are clear on who can coach, they can think more creatively about which coaching
structure(s) they will employ.

ONE LEAD COACH for a collaborating pair
One coach supports both the general and special educator in a
collaborating pair, serving as each person’s primary content coach as well
as their coach for collaborative work.
At STEM Prep School’s Math and Science College Preparatory (MSCP)
High School, the assistant principal coached a math co-teaching pair,
providing individual coaching as well as coaching for the team.
Benefits
Consistent Messaging: Collaborating teachers receive feedback from the same
person. The coach is simultaneously coaching the teaching pair on their individual
roles and content expertise and on the mindsets and behaviors for meeting the
needs of shared students. This model decreases the likelihood that teachers
receive conflicting feedback.
Staff Capacity: This model streamlines the coaching of teams, reducing the
number of staff members required to coach the team and simplifying the number of
schedules to coordinate for coaching.
Considerations
Coach’s Confidence: The coach must feel confident in their ability to navigate
the needs of both teachers and to coach across general education and special
education content areas. Though MSCP’s assistant principal did not have a math
background, she was highly experienced with special education and co-teaching
and regularly collaborated with STEM Prep’s director of mathematics to support her
teacher teams. Thus, she felt capable of leading the teachers who had each taught
math for two years.
Collaboration Among Coaches: A culture of shared support and responsibility
must exist with this model so the lead coach and collaborating teachers can
supplement their coaching when needed. At MSCP, when the assistant principal
recognizes a need from her team related to complex math content, she collaborates
directly with STEM Prep School’s director of mathematics or invites her to join a
coaching conversation to provide support.

A SUPPLEMENTAL COACH for a collaborating pair
Each collaborating pair has a dedicated coach for their partnership in
addition to their own individual coaches.
At Rosenwald Collegiate Academy, the school’s director of scholar
support coached the Geometry co-teaching team once a week on
adapting instruction for their students with disabilities. She also met
weekly with the special education teacher for an individual coaching
session, as the school’s director of curriculum and instruction did with the
math teacher.
At Ednovate, a Blue Engine coach met with co-teaching pairs weekly.
Each Ednovate teacher also had their own individual, school-based coach
for instruction.
Benefits
Focus on Collaboration: Collaborating teachers are able to focus exclusively on
their work together in service of students’ needs. Coaching time is uniquely tailored
to their work as a team and focused on team formation, data analysis, instructional
planning, and when relevant, co-teaching.
Staffing Flexibility: Because the coaching role can be performed by an external
coach, this model provides flexibility for school leaders looking to incorporate
expertise and support even if they do not have staff capacity.
Considerations
Scheduling: This model requires thoughtful scheduling. Leaders need to
develop site-based schedules that protect time for teachers to engage in routine
supplemental coaching focused on collaboration with their teaching partners.
Coach Communication: Coaches need clear lines of and systems for
communication so that all staff supporting the two teachers are aligned on what is
being discussed and worked on in individual and supplemental coaching sessions.
Without this system for communication, teachers are likely to receive conflicting
feedback on their instructional next steps and will struggle to prioritize their next
moves.

TWO COACHES collaboratively supporting a collaborating pair
Two different coaches (one focused on special education, one focused
on general education content) come together to co-coach a collaborating
teaching pair.
At MSCP, the director of special education co-coached a math co-teaching

Benefits

of a coach until they are confident in their ability to support outside of their own
expertise. STEM Prep’s director of mathematics cited her co-coaching with the
director of special education as key to her ability to effectively coach special
educators in future years in a one-lead-coach model.
Ensuring Deep, Aligned Support: In this model, everyone remains fully aware of
and aligned on the work that is happening with both teachers. This allows coaches
to identify trends and needs across pairs that can inform professional development
areas of focus or guide the development of new or updated guidance to benefit
educators in serving all students.
Considerations
Coaches’ Relationships and Pairings: School administrators will need to
invest time and energy in the formation of these coaching teams similar to their
investment in the creation of teaching teams. It is important to plan proactively and
strategically to develop and support these coaches to work together in service of
their teaching pairs, and ultimately, the students they aim to serve.
Coach Communication: \Coaches need clear lines of communication and systems
to ensure that all who are supporting the two teachers are aligned on what they are
discussing and working on with teaching pairs. Coaching teams will want to develop
norms and routines around communication and follow-up so they have clear roles
and responsibilities that reinforce their collective goals for team development.
Staff Capacity: This model is resource-intensive. It requires significant amounts of
coaches’ time. Coaches’ workloads, including the number of individuals and teams
they coach, need to be planned thoughtfully so they have the time and capacity to
reach their desired outcomes.

How do we create the necessary infrastructure for coaching

System infrastructure for coaching needs to be considered at the individual, teacher-team, and school

BUILD PROTECTED TIME INTO THE SCHOOL TRAINING AND TEACHING SCHEDULES.

• Collaborating teachers to meet regularly. Ideally, teams will meet at least one period weekly if not

• Clear roles and responsibilities for collaborating teachers
• Coaching agendas
• Rubrics and criteria for exemplary co-planning and co-teaching
• Observation tools (like Blue Engine’s and Collegiate Academies’)

What does it look like to launch a coaching relationship?
DISTINGUISH COLLABORATION COACHING FROM OTHER, ROLE-SPECIFIC
COACHING.
It is the responsibility of coaches to let collaborating teachers know that their work together is
specifically focused on the team’s collaboration in service of meeting the needs of all students,
particularly those with disabilities. Coaches have a responsibility to call attention to disparities in
experiences and outcomes for subgroups of students like students with disabilities, multilingual
learners, and students experiencing homelessness, and to work with the teacher teams to identify ways
in which they can lean on and use each other’s presence and expertise to provide high-impact support.

The unique role of collaboration-focused coaching is to support general and special educators’:
• establishing and upholding a strong and trusting relationship;
• utilizing student data to understand the needs of all students, particularly those with disabilities;
• leveraging each other’s expertise to bolster their lessons and supports for all students;
• effective co-planning to ensure alignment, rigor, and accessibility; and
• for co-teachers, planning for and implementing high-impact co-teaching models that make optimal
use of their shared presence in the classroom.
ESTABLISH COMMUNICATION EXPECTATIONS AND STRUCTURES.
Teachers and coaches — particularly collaborating teachers who are working with multiple coaches
— should proactively align on communication methods and expectations to ensure feedback is
manageable and aligned across coaches for the team. Ideally, this happens before the start of the
school year during summer professional development preservice and focuses on systematized
methods of collaboration and communication across coaching domains (i.e., collaboration and
content-area guidance) using model resources to establish norms. Such systems should consider
how coaching feedback will be prioritized for teacher teams and how it will reinforce content and skill
development in all domains.

How do we regularly check in on coaching relationships?
ENSURE COLLABORATING TEACHERS FEEL SUPPORTED THROUGHOUT THE YEAR.
As the school year progresses and as collaborating teachers establish routines with each other and
with their coach, it is important to step back from the day-to-day to monitor the progress of the coaching
relationship. This can take place in a variety of ways, for example:
• Coaches or school leaders can have one-on-one meetings with teachers at the middle of the
year to monitor their progress, ongoing capacity, and satisfaction with both coaching and their
collaborating teacher.
• School leaders can administer a teacher survey to gather feedback on coaching relationships and
use the data to make adjustments to how coaching is executed.

Summary
Instructional coaching has often focused on content expertise, not collaboration across educators
serving the same students. Collaborating with colleagues around data and instructional planning isn’t
often covered in teacher preparation programs, and it’s not necessarily intuitive for educators. As
more collaboration across special education and general education occurs in classrooms, teaching
teams need support to learn how to collaborate effectively to leverage their joint expertise and build
their mindsets and skill to move their practice forward together. This document addresses some of the
key components of effective coaching of collaborating teachers, which requires special attention to be
paid to how co-teachers work together while teaching and planning. Regular, vision-aligned coaching
of collaborating teachers is the key way to reinforce all aspects of good co-teaching and ensure every
student has their needs met in the classroom.

Conclusion
Too often, special education is siloed within schools. Special educators, tasked with meeting the varied
needs of students with disabilities, are made to do so largely on their own, which leads to a great
disparity between the content expertise that students with and without disabilities receive on a daily
basis. Additionally, because being a siloed special educator is particularly challenging, teacher turnover
rates in this field continue to rise, meaning that the students who are often furthest from opportunity are
being taught by more and more inexperienced teachers. While special education teachers have specific
training, students with disabilities benefit most from a collaborative approach that engages multiple
educators to meet their unique needs. In order to improve outcomes for Black and Latinx students with
disabilities experiencing poverty, schools must adopt inclusive mindsets and practices and improve
collaboration between general and special educators at all levels of America’s education system.
Over the course of three years, the Marshall Street Networked Improvement Community for Students
with Disabilities (NIC) saw teachers and leaders from 10 participating charter networks implement,
study, and adjust various best practices for improving outcomes for the target population of students.
This collection — itself an outcome of the work these schools have done — describes some of the
strongest of these practices, giving particular attention to collaboration between general and special
educators, based on the understanding that
improving this collaboration leads to improved
educational experiences and outcomes for all
students. It highlights three best practices: coplanning, student work analysis, and a gradual
release model to optimize co-teachers. There
is one case study showing what this work can
look like when done well. The collection also
includes guidance documents about effectively
forming collaborative teams and supporting
those teams with strong coaching.
The work highlighted in these pages has
improved student outcomes over the course
of the NIC because of the implementation of
strong collaborative practices and support
structures. Intentional co-planning, in particular,
led to strong measurable improvements in

Key Takeaways
COLLABORATION STARTS NOW.
It is very rare for a school system to consider a new approach and have the structures in place to
flawlessly implement that new approach right away. Collaboration is no exception; in fact, creating the
conditions that enable highly effective collaborative practices requires a great deal of time, energy, and
staff. However, by delaying implementation until all of those systems are in place, leaders rob students
of the opportunity to be supported by teachers working together. Instead, start now! Collaboration is a
spectrum, and while not all schools are in a place where all classrooms can be co-taught, all schools
can ensure that general and special educators have some protected co-planning time, which is a
start. Collaboration, in any form, is the first step on the road to more equitable support for all students,
particularly students with disabilities.
ADDITIONAL EDUCATORS MEANS ADDITIONAL OPPORTUNITIES FOR STUDENTS.
Effective teacher collaboration is a gift for students. When done well, it allows teachers to adjust small
groups and use differentiated instruction in many more ways than they would be able to solo. Coteaching increases the amount of expertise in the classroom, particularly when general and special
educators are effectively collaborating with one another on a regular basis. All of this means that more
students, particularly those with disabilities, are given increased access to learning opportunities.
CO-PLANNING MUST BE A DELIBERATE ACTION.
In order for collaboration between educators to be effective, there must be specific time set aside
for deliberate co-planning. Not only that, but the time must be used intentionally to increase access
to learning for all students. The collaborating teachers and co-teachers in the NIC found that simply
meeting together was not enough. Rather, meeting with a plan and with support led to the growth in
practice and student impact that they were looking for.

IMPROVING COLLABORATION MUST BE A WHOLE-SCHOOL (OR NETWORK) EFFORT.
Putting two or more educators in a room together, while a step in the right direction, will not lead to
improved student access or outcomes. Improving collaboration requires a systematic approach, starting
at the leadership level. Just as general and special educators need to work together intentionally,
instructional leaders must work together with special education directors to ensure that inclusive
mindsets and enabling conditions are in place. Only when all individuals within a school are working
together will true classroom-level collaboration be at its most effective.

The three chapters and two guidance documents
in this collection lay out the key components of
effective collaboration.
Put together, these components set the stage for collaborative work that supports all
teachers to better support all students, while simultaneously growing the capacity of
leaders within the system. The participating members of the NIC found that as they honed
their collaborative skills, student learning outcomes increased at a greater rate. It is a
misconception, however, that these components cannot be implemented until each one
is carefully manicured and perfected. Rather, we urge school and system leaders to start
now. Each school system has different needs, but prioritizing collaboration is an invaluable
use of time and resources that will lead to improved equity and student outcomes.
