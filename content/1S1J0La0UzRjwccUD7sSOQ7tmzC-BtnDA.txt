Building the improvement playbook:

Overview

▸ What it is: At its core, Continuous Improvement (CI) is problem-solving. It is a
rigorous process predicated on the belief that every problem exists within a system.
Systems are the interdependent set of elements in the context, including people,
processes and structures. If there is a problem, the system is designed to produce it.
Thus, we may examine the system, locate the problem, and continuously improve the
whole.

05
06

▸ Why it matters: When applied in education, CI ensures that groups and individuals
most marginalized by the system receive attention and support. In doing so, we work
towards a more equitable education system for everyone.

Overview of the Continuous Improvement Process
The tools of CI can be used in any field. In education, CI is used to to increase equity, effectiveness,
and efficiency in schools in service of students and educators.

01 Problem Statement

DEFINE THE PROBLEM. What specifically is the problem we are trying to solve? • Gather Data with key stakeholders and
engage diverse voices in the process. • Avoid solutions. • Understand variation in outcomes as the core issue to address. •
Define the problem from the perspective of those experiencing it. • Conduct Root Cause Analyses to visualize the system
producing current outcomes. • Precisely articulate the Problem Statement.

02 Aim

CRAFT AIM STATEMENTS TO GUIDE AND MOTIVATE THE IMPROVEMENT. What do we want to improve? • Generate
consensus to galvanize team commitment and enroll the community around a specific Aim. • Specify how much, by when,
and for whom. • How will we track progress towards the Aim? Identify outcome measures and collect and review data to
understand baselines and set targets.

03 Driver Diagram

DEVELOP A WORKING THEORY OF IMPROVEMENT. What specifically are we trying to accomplish? Build a Driver
Diagram to visualize the theory of action. • Primary Drivers: What needs to change to reach the Aim? • Secondary
Drivers: Where in the system can we make change? • Change Ideas: How specifically will we target change for
improvement?

PLAN CHANGES ACROSS THE SYSTEM. What change might we make and why? • Implement changes at various levels
of the system (classroom, school, network) • How will the change happen? • Consider changes of different size and scope.

Problem Statement
▸ What it is: A Problem Statement is a single sentence that clearly defines a systemic
problem experienced by students. In education, these problems highlight the
inequitable experiences and outcomes for a student population often positioned
furthest from opportunity. Problem Statements explicitly state failures and gaps
between the current reality and the desired future. They are derived from relevant data
telling a comprehensive story of how environments and experiences have directly
impacted students, families, communities and the educators within the schools that
serve them.

05
06

▸ Why it matters: Carefully considering the problem to solve can help teams avoid
replicating those historical inequities built into education. A team that ensures that
they are solving the “right” problem by unpacking it and collecting data and
information wastes less time, effort, and will in the long run be more likely to see
improvement in experiences and outcomes. Organizing and aligning teams on a
specific problem focuses improvement efforts directly on both the technical and
adaptive conditions of those experiencing it.
▸ In This Guide: Process, Examples and Checklist, Tools, References

Aim Statement

▸ What it is: An Aim Statement is the ultimate desired outcome of an improvement
effort. It is a specific, measurable answer to the core improvement question, What are we
trying to accomplish? Every improvement effort requires an Aim that organizes the work
of everyone involved. Aims are inspiring and ambitious, simply stated and highly specific
about the problem being solved. Aim Statements clearly define success for the
improvement effort by specifying:
❏
What will be improved?
❏
By how much?
❏
By when?
❏
For whom?

04
05
06

▸ Why it matters: Having an Aim Statement provides a ‘north star’ that guides and
motivates collective effort. Having a common definition of success provides common
purpose to those working towards it. Taking the time to craft and generate consensus
around a well-defined Aim Statement galvanizes a team’s commitment. It serves as an
inspiring communication tool to get others into the work of solving the problem. An Aim
that is a top-level organizational priority empowers teams and individuals to focus their
efforts where it will have the greatest impact.

Overview

▸ In This Guide: Process, Examples, Checklist, Tools, and References

An Aim Statement explicitly names the target population for improvement
efforts (defining “for whom”). Continuous Improvement (CI) is most
powerfully used in service of students historically positioned furthest from
opportunity, such as Students with Disabilities.
Taking a Targeted Universalism approach to CI allows solutions designed for
the most vulnerable students to also improve the learning environment and
outcomes for all. Aim Statements must be clearly communicated and
consistently referenced amongst all stakeholders and appropriate for the
time and resources dedicated to meeting it.

Defining the Aim is
the most important
task - everything
falls from it and
nothing happens
without it.

Measures

Measures within an Aim Statement
Improvement teams must be able to know whether they are making progress towards an Aim and whether they have
accomplished that Aim. Thus, measurement is critical when drafting an Aim Statement. Outcome measures are most
often used to track overall progress towards an Aim.
❏
Lagging measures are output oriented and let teams know if the Aim has been achieved. They are easy to
measure but hard to quickly influence (e.g graduation rate, end-of-year grades, external assessments).
Lagging measures are reported infrequently and after-the-fact.
❏
Leading measures define inputs that inform whether a team is or is not on track to meeting the Aim (e.g.
increased performance on interim assessments or number/percentage of students moving in/out of bottom
quintile from Fall --> Spring). Leading measures are more predictive in nature and allow an organization to
make adjustments based on results.

Example Aim Statements

S.M.A.R.T.E Characteristics

Aim Statements are both precise and concise, capturing a single
focus that is clear for all stakeholders working to achieve success.
When crafting an Aim, it is crucial to capture the problem being
addressed in language that does not place blame on individuals, but
rather, on the system that produces those inequities. Frame the
success of the improvement effort around actually making
improvement for students who are furthest from opportunity.
Consider how using a S.M.A.R.T.E. framework provides clarity and
focus to the following Aim Statement:
We will increase graduation rates from 56% to 90% by
January 1, 2025 for students experiencing housing insecurity.

Measureable: Answers how much by when
Aspirational but attainable: 90% is aspirational but attainable if

Consider this Example
By June 15, 2022, decrease the achievement gap between our students with disabilities and general
education students by 50%.
Upon an initial read, this seems like a specific (SWD and general education students), measureable (50%) and
aspirational (achievement gap) desired outcome. However, this Aim Statement is problematic. Framing challenges by
using broad terms like ‘achievement gap’ can too often locate the improvement effort on students as opposed to the
systems (structures and processes) that created those results. Furthermore, if additional resources are allocated to
improve outcomes for SWD and as a result, the outcomes for SWD increase while outcomes for Gen Ed students
decrease, then it would be difficult to call this Aim a success. Also, if the gap is small, but overall outcomes are low,
that is problematic. Revising the above Aim as written below reframes this desired outcome as something all key
stakeholders can commit to and then celebrate when accomplished:
By June 15, 2023, we will increase the average end-of-year GPA of our Black and Latinx students with
disabilities experiencing poverty by 50% (from 1.70 to 2.55) while maintaining the average GPA of our general
education students.
The revised Aim Statement makes the aspirational desired outcome more attainable by extending the deadline 1
year while still keeping it time-bound to a firm end date. It also specifically addresses equity gaps by focusing on
Black and Latinx students with disabilities experiencing poverty as the target population. Accomplishing this Aim will
result in improved outcomes for the target population while also improving systems that impact all students
(Targeted Universalism). By including a baseline GPA metric of 1.70 and a target GPA goal of 2.55, the revised Aim is
more specific and more measurable. Lastly, the revised Aim explicitly addresses maintaining the average GPA of
general education students while working towards the desired outcome for the target population, thus ensuring both
student groups thrive.

Aims, Problem Statements and Goal Statements

Dimensions

When defining Aim Statements, it is helpful to juxtapose them with Problem Statements and Goal Statements
to develop a shared understanding among the team and in the Aim crafting process.

Aim Statement | Problem Statement
An Aim Statement is different from a Problem
Statement because the Aim Statement
states the desired outcome of the
improvement effort while the Problem
Statement defines the systemic issue that
needs to be addressed or changed.

Aim Statement | Goal Statements
All Aims are goals, but not all goals are Aims. An Aim
Statement is very specific, as described above. Aim
Statements can motivate the creation of additional
goal statements to help clarify and break down how the
team will achieve the Aim. Additional Goal Statements
can help team members further define measures of
improvement, set interim milestones, prioritize initial
changes to pursue, and determine levels of support(s)
that may be needed.

Remember, improvement science is an organic, non-linear
process. A learning stance is critical to balance commitment
towards the Aim while being open to how to best reach it. If we
knew how to reach the Aim, we would have done it already.
Creating an Aim Statement
Step 1: Assemble a Team Based upon the problem being addressed, gather a team that is representative of
individuals who are closest to the problem and will be supported and empowered to solve the problem. Ensure the
team has defined shared norms and agreements for participation. Make sure everyone on the improvement team
has a common understanding of the critical terms and definitions the team will be using.

Process

Step 2: Ground in the Problem & Review Data Review the Problem Statement and outcome-level
baseline data to get a clear understanding of current performance. Examine and discuss example Aims and
non-example Aims (see page 2) in order to align the team’s thinking around the characteristics of a S.M.A.R.T.E. Aim
Statement. To get started, ground in equity by asking: Whose needs aren’t we serving as well as we could yet, and
why? From there, the team can begin to define the desired outcome. Crafting an Aim should be a learning process
and there should not be an expectation of “getting it right” the first time.

Step 3: Draft the Aim When beginning to draft an Aim Statement, return to the question What are we trying to
accomplish? Keep in mind that the Aim is not set in stone at this point and can be updated and refined as it is
stress-tested (Step 4). Consider using the following sentence frame to get started:
By June 15, 2023, (Name of Organization) will [increase / decrease] ____ (focus area for improvement
project) for targeted population related to general population) ____ from (baseline data) ____ to
(measurable outcome).

Step 5: Enroll the Community An Aim is the galvanizing force that brings people into the effort and
catalyzes change management. Communicate the Aim Statement and why it matters at various meetings (Staff PD,
Grade Level Meetings, PLCs, PTA Meetings, etc.) to enroll others into the effort. This is an opportunity for
stakeholders to fully embrace the complexity of the issue and buy into collective responsibility to accomplish the
Aim. A clear and compelling Aim Statement should motivate and inspire others to join the effort. It should be
concise enough for any staff member in the faculty room to articulate or for a parent to recall after a PTA meeting.
Inform stakeholders that Continuous Improvement is naturally iterative and agree to how those stakeholders will be
involved and when progress will be reported.

▸ What it is: A Driver Diagram is a visual representation of an organization's
working theory of improvement, providing a roadmap for the systemic changes

learning accumulates and changes are tested. Driver Diagrams focus improvement

the Aim in common language that can be understood by all who interact with it.

Components of a Driver Diagram

Overview

The Aim Statement is the north star guiding the
improvement effort. The Aim answers the first
essential question of improvement: What
specifically are we trying to accomplish?
Primary Drivers are the areas of focus that
represent the team’s hypotheses about what
needs to change to reach the Aim. They are often
phrased as “measurable states of the world that
need to be true to reach the Aim”.
●

Key components of Primary Drivers:
○
Usually between 3-5, similar in scope - they should be mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive.
○
High Leverage - they represent critical components the Improvement Team has elevated as the most
important conditions that need to be true in order to accomplish the Aim.

Secondary Drivers identify where a change in the system will create improvement in the Primary Driver, including
structures, norms, practices and processes (e.g. department meetings, learning lab, hiring processes, student
enrollment, family engagement, class placement, IEP meetings, coaching, grading practices, etc).
Change Ideas are the specific ideas the team will test to make changes to the Secondary Drivers. They should be
specific and target the way the work is completed. They tell how the improvement will happen to test the working
theory of improvement.

How to Read a Driver Diagram

Overview

The Driver Diagram is laid out in a way that tells the story of the team’s working theory of improvement. It begins
with an Aim Statement that is the central focus of the improvement effort. The Driver Diagram states what will
change, where it will happen, and how to reach that Aim. The sentence frame at the bottom of the diagram (below)
can be read as an “If/then statement” to show the relationship between the hypothesis of change (“If”) and the Aim
(“Then”).

Reading left to right, the Driver Diagram translates a high-level improvement Aim, pointing to key drivers (leverage
points) for reaching the Aim, which lead to specific changes that can be made. Reading the Driver Diagram from left
to right tells the team HOW to make the change.
Example 1: If, by June 2023, we want to increase the
average GPA of our Black and Latinx students with
disabilities experiencing poverty by 50% while
maintaining the GPA for all students (Aim), then we
need to target high quality tiered (academic, behavior,
social-emotional) instruction (Primary Driver) by
ensuring educators are conducting regular 1:1
check-ins on student goals (Secondary Driver), and
one way to do this is to provide educators with an
implementation guide (Change Idea).

Example 2: If we want to decrease chronic absenteeism (missing more
than 18 days per year) of Black and Latinx students with disabilities
experiencing poverty from 25% to 10% while maintaining attendance
rates for all students, by June 15, 2023 (Aim), then we need to target
creating trusting relationships within the school community and
support a sense of belonging in safe learning environments (Primary
Driver) by having all adults actively working to develop positive
relationships with these students (Secondary Driver) and one way to
do this is by providing culturally relevant lessons that appeal to student
interests (Change Idea).

Reading right to left, the Driver Diagram communicates hypothesized relationships between specific changes and
the Aim the team plans to test. Reading from right to left tells the team WHY they are making the change.
Example 1: If educators are provided an
implementation guide (Change Idea), then they will be
able to conduct regular 1:1 check-ins on student goals
(Secondary Driver), which will support high quality
tiered (academic, behavior, social-emotional) instruction
(Primary Driver), that will lead to an increased average
GPA of our Black and Latinx students with disabilities
experiencing poverty by 50% while maintaining the GPA
for all students by June 2023 (Aim).

Example 2: If educators provide culturally relevant lessons that appeal
to student interests (Change Idea), then all adults will be actively
developing positive relationships with students (Secondary Driver)
which will support students having trusting relationships and having a
sense of belonging in safe learning environments (Primary Driver) that
will lead to a decrease in chronic absenteeism (missing more than 18
days per year) of our Black and Latinx students with disabilities
experiencing poverty from 25% to 10% while maintaining attendance
rates for all students, by June 15, 2023 (Aim).

Measures Within a Driver Diagram

Measures

Driver Diagrams require a set of measures to understand how those areas of focus are performing and provide
feedback to the team for learning and decision-making. These measures illuminate what is working, for whom, under
what conditions, as well as inform whether the work being done is improving the outcomes or experiences of the focus
population or area targeted by the improvement process. There are different types of measures collected to inform
performance of each level of the Driver Diagram and analyze progress throughout the whole system.
●
Outcome Measures are used to measure the Aim with lagging measures reported infrequently and
after-the-fact and leading measures more frequently to show progress along the way.
●

Driver Measures help teams analyze performance of specific parts of the system to monitor progress and
influence future changes, usually collected more frequently (e.g. monthly, quarterly).

●

Process Measures are collected with greater frequency (e.g weekly) to understand if the implemented changes
are happening, how they are working, and if they are leading to improvements.

Example:
Aim: By June 2023, we will increase the average GPA of our Black and Latinx students with disabilities
experiencing poverty by 50% while maintaining the GPA for all students.
Outcome Measure (lagging): GPA at the end of the year for all students, disaggregated
Outcome Measure (leading): student grades, interim assessments

▸ What it is: Change Ideas are the hypothesized actions, practices, and innovations
generated by a team to reach their Aim. They tell how the improvement will happen by
being specific about what will be done differently within the team’s Driver Diagram. They
are rigorously tested through Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) cycles in order to determine if the
idea does in fact improve the outcomes and experiences for the target group. Change
Ideas can be:
●
Implemented at multiple levels of a system (e.g. classroom, school, network)
●
Different in size and scope
●
Entirely new changes to a system: new programs or structures
●
Adaptations of what currently exists within a system: small tweaks to something
that is already working
▸ Why it matters: While not all changes lead to improvement, all improvement requires
change. Testing Change Ideas is critical because they provide learning centered around
what is working, why, for whom and under what conditions. Teams must orient to learning
and consistently ask, “what might this change help us learn about how to solve the
problem?” Often in education reform, responses to large-scale and complex changes are
implemented without knowing if they will actually work, thus reproducing inequitable
outcomes. This leads to initiative fatigue and resistance to future change. Implementing
Change Ideas at smaller scales equips teams to learn fast, fail fast, and improve quickly
before network or school wide implementation.

▸ In This Guide: Process, Examples, Checklist, Tools, and References

▸ What it is: A System of Measures for Improvement is inclusive of the full set of data
points used by the Improvement Team to test their Driver Diagram, chart progress
toward the Aim, and determine when a change is an improvement. A System of
Measures is linked to a Driver Diagram to understand how well those key processes,
structures and the changes being tested are working.
The data from the measures provides feedback for the team on both long-term
outcomes and short-term processes. Measurement for Improvement flips the typical
measurement paradigm of “what data do we have and what can we learn from it?” to
“what do we need to learn and what data will we need to learn it?”
▸ Why it matters: Without measures in place, it is hard to know what is working for
whom, and under what conditions. A System of Measures provides a comprehensive
understanding of how the improvement effort is progressing toward achieving the
overarching Aim. The factors contributing to problems and solutions in education are
diverse. Thus, a comprehensive System of Measures must help teams to learn more
about processes, norms, learning environments, perceptions, and inputs.
To achieve reliable quality at scale, teams must understand, address, and learn from
variation, specifically to highlight inequities across groups. The system of measures is
only as good as the faith that individuals within the school system have in the
measures themselves. So, a System of Measures is important because it proactively
and transparently defines success, progress, and improvement.

Variation is the problem we study to understand what works, for whom, and under what conditions.
In Continuous Improvement, variation is the core problem to solve because the goal is quality results for all
students in a target population. A detailed examination of variation helps teams to gain a deeper understanding
of the systemic problems to be solved as well as what can be learned from those already experiencing a degree
of success.

Variation

For example, a principal noticed that student course passage data showed that Students with Disabilities (SWD)
are the subgroup struggling the most to pass their courses. The team identified a dimension (SWD) leading to
variation against course passage rates, which for the most part, defines success within a secondary school
system.
Next, the team studied the variation within the SWD population across other measures, and across various
subgroups. In this case, survey results showed SWDs reported a high feeling of safety at school, but exhibited an
especially low growth mindset. If so, some additional variation has been identified across groups: SWDs are
passing fewer classes, as a group, than other student groups, and they are also reporting the lowest growth
mindset across all other subgroups. Thus, the team began to adapt their Change Ideas to ensure that growth
mindset was explicitly addressed to support SWD success.
Additionally, the team looked to see if there are any courses in which SWDs were succeeding at the same rates
as all other students, and in which all students are generally successful at higher rates than the network average.
They found a few teachers and learned that they were more consistently and explicitly planning instruction for
students’ accommodations, a practice they were then able to spread to others. The team analyzed variation to
find "positive deviants" - people, programs, and schools to learn from; these are places in which it appears that
the school system's larger problem has been "solved." Here, variation helped not only add nuance and
understanding to the problems to solve, but to also begin to understand how people within the school had
already (at least partially) solved the problem.
Variation is inherent in all systems. Schools should work for everyone so it’s important to deeply understand the
experience and outcomes of those positioned furthest from opportunity so that teams can intentionally improve
the system to reliably, consistently, and equitably meet the needs of all young people, at scale.

Step 1: Outcome Measures
Outcome Measures indicate how the overall system is performing and track overall movement towards an Aim.
●
Identify how the Aim will be measured, at what frequency, and with what instrument or data. This
measure should be tied to student outcomes and experiences. Outcome Measures include both:
○
lagging measures usually reported infrequently (e.g. annually) and usually after-the-fact.
○
leading measures collected more frequently (e.g. semesterly) and show progress along the way,

grading tool.
Outcome Measure (leading): student grades, semesterly, reported through the report card grading tool

Process

Step 2: Driver Measures
Driver Measures are early indicators (intermediaries between outcomes and process) of whether or not progress is
being made on the intermediate outcomes usually connected to the Primary Drivers of the Aim.
●
Identify measures that show the performance of specific parts of the system to monitor progress toward
the Aim and influence future changes. These are usually reported more frequently than Outcome Measures
(e.g. quarterly, monthly).

Primary Driver: SWD are engaged in high-quality tiered (academic, behavior, social-emotional) instruction.
Driver Measure (leading): Course assessment scores collected after each unit (approximately every 6 weeks)

Step 3: Process Measures
Process Measures indicate if the process, norms, and structures of the system are performing with quality and
reliability. They answer three questions: (1) Are changes happening reliably? (2) Are changes resulting in
improvement? (3) What do people think of the changes?
●
Identify measures that indicate how and where the system (e.g. advisory class, policy implementation,
school) is changing. These measures will attach to Secondary Drivers and Change Ideas and should
determine if the changes are working reliably with quality and consistently. These actionable measures are
reported frequently and are embedded in daily work.

Secondary Driver: Educators are conducting regular 1:1 check-ins to support students in goal setting.
Process Measure: Students number of goals set, number of goals on track and number of goals completed
are collected by an adult advisor each week after student 1:1 check ins.

▸ What it is: The Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) cycle is a process for testing a change
and determining whether that change leads to the desired improvement. It is rooted in
the scientific method, and enables educators to quickly and rigorously conduct action
research. The PDSA cycle is both a specific set of procedural steps (a form to track
learning) and a conceptual methodology (a way of thinking and enacting change).
▸ Why it matters: The PDSA cycle lays the foundation for continuous improvement.
This process helps to build new knowledge faster informing future scale-up efforts by:
Compartmentalizing risk by learning rigorously, incrementally, and quickly
●
●

PDSAs serve as feedback loops for learning, providing the evidence of
reliability and quality required to responsibly scale a practice, program, or
process within a school system.
The rapid nature of PDSA cycles allows for low risk/high reward opportunities to
quickly and rigorously learn what does and does not work.

Bridging research and practice
●

Leading research informs the changes and practices chosen. The PDSA cycle
enables theory to be applied effectively in targeted contexts, where data helps
to understand what works, for whom, under what conditions.

Exposing gaps in knowledge about the system
●

PDSA cycles test the best thinking about how to improve a system or process
“on the ground”. This testing can expose unknown or unrecognized gaps in the
system. Learning about the system is a primary goal of PDSA testing.

Empowering stakeholders
●

PDSAs can empower stakeholders by allowing them test changes they believe
will lead to improvement in a bounded and disciplined way.

Do. This step includes collecting the data and noting observations and insights along the way.

The Networked Improvement Community (NIC) for Students
with Disabilities is a pilot community of 10 Charter Management
Organizations (CMOs) working towards the Aim of achieving
dramatic gains for Black and Latinx students with disabilities
experiencing poverty. Collectively, these CMOs serve more than
75,000 students.

With support from technical assistance providers Marshall Street,
SWIFT Education Center, and research organizations NIRN, RTI
International, and SRI International, our goal is to systematically
improve the way we serve students with disabilities and bring these
solutions back to school systems everywhere.

policies of the foundation.
