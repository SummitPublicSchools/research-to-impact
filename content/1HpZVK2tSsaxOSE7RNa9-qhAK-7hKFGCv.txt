Practice Overview
What is the Practice?
Coaches intentionally train co-teachers to use
the highest-impact models of co-teaching by:
• incrementally introducing co-teachers to
different co-teaching approaches,
• supporting co-teachers to use student data
to drive instructional decisions, and
• developing co-teachers’ skill and
confidence with each new model before
introducing more.

Why is this Practice Important?
The use of high-impact co-teaching models benefits students with disabilities by:
PROMOTING TRUE INCLUSION
A truly inclusive environment exists when all students are together, in the classroom,
getting the support they need to excel.1
ALLOWING FOR TAILORED INSTRUCTIONAL OPPORTUNITIES
Higher-impact models enable teachers to use differentiated instructional methods and targeted
interventions to meet a wider variety of students’ needs.2
DECREASING STUDENT-TO-TEACHER RATIOS
Research shows that lower student-to-teacher ratios can have a significant impact on students’
experiences and outcomes in school, particularly for students in our priority population.3 All highimpact co-teaching models lower the student-to-teacher ratio dramatically.
AFFORDING STUDENTS CONSISTENT ACCESS TO BOTH OF THEIR TEACHERS
Effective use of varied co-teaching models gives all students the opportunity to work with each
teacher, naturally providing them access to both teachers’ expertise and minimizing stigma.
Using an incremental method to coach on high-impact co-teaching models benefits teachers by:
PROVIDING EXPLICIT SUPPORT FOR AN APPROACH THAT IS NOT INTUITIVE
Lower leverage co-teaching models — like One Teach/One Assist — are often familiar, making
them easy for co-teachers to default into without explicit modeling and coaching. Dedicated
coaching acknowledges that sharing instructional responsibility in nontraditional ways is new and
requires direct support.4
TRULY VALUING EACH EDUCATOR
When both co-teachers’ roles are optimized
in the classroom, higher job satisfaction
follows.5 This is particularly relevant in the
current landscape in which special education
teachers are in tremendous demand and
their retention is a top priority for the field.

The Continuum of Co-Teaching Models
Not all co-teaching models are equal in terms of their impact on students’ learning. Marilyn Friend, one
of the foremost co-teaching researchers and developers, explains that models in which both people
have active teaching roles should be designated for “high use” based on their outsized impact on
student outcomes.6

SIX APPROACHES TO CO-TEACHING

Source: Teaching Tolerance Seamless Teaching Toolkit

Often, co-teachers receive professional development on new co-teaching models through
workshops or resources presenting and explaining all six models at once. Unfortunately, this kind of
professional development typically does not explain how student needs should drive the use of each
model. Within the Networked Improvement Community (NIC), leaders found that this professional
development approach was ineffective. Teachers were overwhelmed by new information and lacked
support for planning for the logistics and instruction associated with each model.
By incrementally building teachers’ confidence with more effective co-teaching models, teachers’
openness to incorporating a variety of models grew tremendously. At both Collegiate Academies and
Green Dot Public Schools, where an incremental approach was used by coaches, the prevalence
of One Teach/One Assist decreased significantly in favor of higher-impact, lower student-to-teacher
ratio models.

Bringing this Practice to Life
OPTIMIZING CO-TEACHING AT GREEN DOT
PUBLIC SCHOOLS CALIFORNIA
At Green Dot Public Schools, a coach facilitates the introduction of new co-teaching models to co-teaching
teams. The process begins with a student work analysis, followed by a brief “lesson” about a high-impact
co-teaching model that is appropriate for addressing the identified needs. This approach — as opposed
to professional development that presents every model at the same time — provides co-teachers with a
measured, accessible, and applicable introduction to each new model as it is needed in practice.
The following case study presents an example of this process with 9th grade ELA co-teachers Quentin
(general education) and Jesse (special education), who have been co-teaching for nearly a year. Glynis is a
special education instructional leader at Green Dot who facilitates their conversation, utilizing adult learning
techniques to push the teachers’ practice in a way that is engaging.
In the introduction described below, Quentin and Jesse have just finished their student work analysis and
have determined that students need support on personification. Quentin wants to provide more overt
examples of it so that students are clear on the purpose of the technique and when and how to use it.

Strategically Selecting a New Model
After the group completed the student work analysis, Glynis posed a question: what co-teaching
model would best meet their newly uncovered student needs? Quentin suggested a whole group
approach, because all students need to understand how to apply personification in their own
writing. Jesse called attention to the small group of students who did not yet fully understand the
concept of personification and suggested that perhaps he could work with them during part of an
upcoming lesson on this foundational content.

Glynis listened to the teacher’s analysis and built upon their insights, highlighting that they had an
opportunity to optimize both teachers through a specific co-teaching model: Alternative Teaching
in small groups. To support her suggestion, she shared stories from other Green Dot schools where
the incorporation of groups had led to dramatic improvements in students’ grades.
Jesse and Quentin were enthused by the prospect of trying it out, but also shared that they
generally relied on One Teach/One Assist or Teaming, so the new model would be outside of their
comfort zone. Wanting to ensure that they would be able to gradually incorporate the model, Glynis
recommended that they begin by focusing on just one of their co-taught periods, and by planning
for Jesse to work with their focal group of students — those who would benefit from foundational
content — for 10 minutes. Upon hearing this measured approach, Jesse and Quentin agreed that
they were ready to dig in and learn more.

Glynis opted to prioritize the second need — culture — during their time together in this session.
She coached the duo on how they could talk to their students about small groupings. She shared
effective approaches used by other co-teachers to destigmatize the new structure, including
the language previously used by a different co-teaching pair at another Green Dot high school:
“We’re trying this new thing because we really want to make sure that you’re successful on your
finals. We know there are a lot of kids in our class, and that can be distracting! Small groups help
everyone get the time and attention that they need.” Glynis reassured Jesse and Quentin that,
overwhelmingly, fellow Green Dot teachers found that students were comfortable if they knew
alternative small groups were helping everyone.

Shifting to Logistics
As the meeting neared its end, the group turned their attention to Jesse and Quentin’s other
coaching need — logistics — as well as next steps. To support the team’s practice and
development, Glynis shared a resource focused specifically on effective structures for running small
groups in co-taught spaces. She encouraged Jesse and Quentin to review the resource together
and to note questions or additional needs they found. The following week, Glynis would make sure
that the team felt prepared for their first attempt at Alternative Teaching, as well as meet with them
after their lesson to reflect on the result for students and their own learnings.
This incremental approach to professional development provided Jesse and Quentin with a useful,
applied introduction to a new co-teaching model in a way that was accessible and tied to their
students’ current needs.

Pairing with Purpose

The Role of Trust

Certain models, such as Parallel Teaching, require that
both teachers are able to effectively lead lessons for
the entire class, so it’s beneficial for special education
co-teachers to be focused on a content area that aligns
with their expertise or interests.7 If the special education
teacher lacks sufficient content expertise, they are less
likely to be able to teach a full lesson independently.
At Green Dot’s Ánimo Watts College Preparatory
Academy, co-teachers were initially paired without
prioritizing content expertise. A special education
teacher with years of math experience was working
in an ELA class, while a colleague with a degree in
English was co-teaching math. Both educators spent the
year lacking the content-based confidence to venture
into higher-impact co-teaching methods. When these
teachers’ assignments were flipped, they were able
to implement stronger co-teaching models, increasing
their satisfaction and improving the learning experience
for all students. See Co-Planning for Differentiated
Instruction to learn how strong co-planning addresses
content alignment between co-teachers.

Co-teaching requires a
deep level of trust between
educators, particularly when
using models that require
teachers to relinquish some
control and share instructional
responsibility with their
colleague (e.g., through Parallel

How to Implement this Practice
Create Structures for Gradual Release
of High-Impact Co-Teaching
Step 1: Allocate Time for Coaching and Co-Planning
In order for co-teachers to advance their co-teaching practice and effectively utilize new, higherleverage co-teaching models, structures and routines need to be in place to support learning,
application, and reflection specific to the practice of co-teaching and the mindsets and skills for
collaborative teaming between educators.
These structures should include time for coaches:
• to train teachers to build knowledge of co-teaching models, along with when and how to use
them in response to formative and summative student data,
• to observe or collaboratively plan and prepare with teachers (taking a gradual release
approach), and
• to debrief and guide reflection.
In addition to regular coaching sessions for co-teaching pairs, co-teachers should be allocated time for
routine co-planning between coaching cycles to work as a team to apply their skills and knowledge with
greater independence and confidence. Regular co-planning lets teachers plan for upcoming lessons,
reflect on students’ needs, remove barriers to access, and incorporate new high-impact models as the
year progresses.
To adequately support this practice, time should be reserved
for the following:
• For coaches, at least 90 minutes per month per
co-teaching pair in each coach’s schedule, to provide
at least 45 minutes for coaching sessions and 45
minutes for preparation and aligned observation.
• For co-teachers, at least one 45-minute coaching
session per month, and at least 45 minutes of
co-planning time per week.

SETTING CLEAR GOALS ON CO-TEACHING MEASURES
Goals should encourage the effective incorporation of higher-impact models. (See The Continuum of
Co-Teaching Models, above.) Set goals related to the following:
• Increased use of high-impact co-teaching models. For example:
– After two weeks of coaching, Team A will decrease their use of One Teach/One Assist during the
introduction to new material from five lessons (of five total) to four lessons per week, replacing it
with Alternative Teaching for students scoring below 50% on the previous week’s lessons.
– After four weeks, decrease further to three lessons; after six weeks, two lessons.
• Improved student outcomes. For example:
– After six weeks of using Alternative Teaching, exit ticket averages for students with disabilities
will increase from 30% to (at least) 50% and for the class overall from 50% to (at least) 65%.
• Co-teachers’ roles during instruction. For example:
– After four weeks, the general educator will lead the alternative group during introduction to new
material for at least one-third of lessons.

Step 3: Begin Coaching Session with Student Data
Different models of co-teaching (and the groupings used within them) serve different instructional
needs. By beginning a coaching session with an analysis of students’ most recent data, coaches can
help to ensure that teachers are building their capacity with a new method that will effectively address
the needs presented. This analysis could be either a student work analysis or a review of other
recent quantitative and qualitative data.

Step 6: Implement Short-Term Observation and Coaching Cycles
It is imperative that coaches observe and debrief with co-teaching teams as they try new co-teaching
models, swiftly addressing barriers, gathering data, and celebrating successes. Quick and targeted
feedback allows teams to tweak the implementation of models and gradually build their skill and
knowledge based on what has previously been tried.
Coaches should also help co-teachers progress monitor as they work through and try each new model:
1. Regularly review student academic outcomes data to assess whether changes to the coteaching model are positively impacting student learning. This ideally happens in weekly coplanning meetings between teachers for more rapid review and response to student data, rather
than monthly coaching cycles.
2. Gather students’ feedback on their learning experiences, inquiring about how the use of
higher-impact co-teaching practices is impacting their student experience (e.g., by administering a
brief, biweekly student survey).
By ensuring confidence and efficacy with one model before moving on to the next, each new structure
becomes an established tool for co-teachers to use. Coaches can help their co-teachers determine
if they are ready to progress to another model, using resources to help assess mastery, such as
Collegiate Academies’ co-teaching models scope and sequence.

Ongoing: Build Teachers’ Cross-Department Knowledge and Skill
For co-teachers to effectively implement co-teaching models, they need to share instructional
responsibility during lessons and communicate rapidly with one another throughout instruction. Further,
both teachers need deep knowledge of course content and accessibility strategies that allows them
to tailor instruction to individual student needs. Special education teachers should receive targeted
professional development that builds their content knowledge and fluency with the course curriculum.
General education teachers should similarly receive development focused on techniques for effectively
supporting students with disabilities. It is important that school leaders and coaches ensure they are
providing co-teachers with this essential training in an ongoing fashion.

Summary
High-impact co-teaching models — specifically Parallel, Alternative, and Station Teaching — allow
co-teachers to leverage their doubled capacity to meet the needs of all students, especially those
with disabilities. These models lower student-to-teacher ratios, allow for more universally designed
instruction, and create opportunities for each educator to have an active, strategic role with students,
thereby ensuring their learning and engagement.
While higher-impact co-teaching models yield stronger outcomes, teachers often experience a variety
of barriers to their implementation: knowledge of their different purposes and components, sufficient
content expertise, time necessary for effective co-planning, and support that helps them develop in all
of the above.
In order to implement these models well, co-teachers need to plan for different types of lesson delivery
as well as more complicated logistics. When coaches gradually introduce co-teachers to new models
and ensure their success with each approach before introducing another, they are significantly more
likely to see confident pairs who optimize their shared presence in the classroom and, in turn, see
students grow.
