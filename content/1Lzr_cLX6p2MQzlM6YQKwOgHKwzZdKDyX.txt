The Challenge
How can schools best protect and use co-planning time?
Glynis, the improvement lead and instructional coach at Green Dot Public Schools in Los Angeles, CA,
knew that when Green Dot’s co-teaching teams were able to regularly step away from the classroom
and strategically co-plan instruction, learning outcomes improved for their students with disabilities.
Despite this fact, and even though a weekly co-planning period for co-teaching teams was scheduled,
it was increasingly challenging for the co-teaching teams to use the planning periods to actually plan
their instruction. This was especially true during COVID-19, when new demands regularly took over
planning periods and teachers no longer had the flexibility to check in informally throughout their days.
Additionally, Glynis observed that when coteaching teams could carve out a planning
period — whether a weekly planning block or
quarterly planning day — the teams did not
consistently use data to inform the design of
instruction, leading to less effective co-teaching
in the classroom.
She needed a new solution.
Glynis needed to find a way to ensure that
regular planning time for co-teachers was held
sacred, that co-teachers felt supported by
the school in their skill development, and that
co-teachers were deepening their data-driven
instructional planning and co-teaching practices
through bite-sized coaching.

The Practice
Quarterly co-planning days give teachers
much-needed time to co-plan.
Green Dot’s quarterly co-planning days were designed as a pilot to gather teachers for protected time
for development and instructional planning. Now, after several years of implementation and revision, the
practice follows a specific and targeted structure for the four components of quarterly co-planning that
Glynis and the Green Dot team learned — from experience, observation, and teacher feedback — were
most critical for co-teacher success:
1. Completion of a student work analysis protocol. The co-teaching pair starts the day together
with a student work analysis facilitated by their coach (either in-person or virtually), analyzing
student data to inform needs for instruction. The coach guides the teaching pair to select either the
most effective model of co-teaching to meet the needs that appear in the student work analysis

How Green Dot Implemented
the Practice
Creating a Schedule
To begin the pilot, Glynis first tackled scheduling. She collaborated with school leaders to confirm that
co-teachers had common planning days in the school calendar. To ensure co-teaching teams had the
coverage needed to consistently attend quarterly, full-day co-planning, school leadership worked closely
with Green Dot network staff to build a large substitute teacher pool.
Later, with learning from the pilot, Glynis created a systemized process for scheduling co-planning days
that has become part of the infrastructure for the practice. The process requires close collaboration with
school leadership and co-teachers to schedule the days well in advance. It has five basic steps:

Green Dot’s Scheduling of Co-Planning Days
Step 1: Coach sends scheduling options to school site administrators.
Between August and September, Glynis sends site administrators a list of options for co-planning days for
each quarter. The options are based on coach availability, school vacations, other school and network events,
and school culture (for example, Mondays are avoided in order to keep a consistent schedule for students).
Step 2: Site administrators confirm dates.
The site administrators confirm whether the list of optional dates will work for their sites in two waves: by late
August for the first quarter and by mid-September for quarters 2–4.
Step 3: Teachers sign up for co-planning days.
Teachers receive the list of options for co-planning days and select their preferred dates via a Signup
Genius. If the available dates do not work for a teaching pair, they can email the coach directly, who will
coordinate a separate date with the co-teachers and their administrator.
Step 4: Teachers request substitute teacher coverage.
After selecting their preferred dates, co-teachers email their school’s office manager to inform them of the
upcoming co-planning professional development days. The office manager then coordinates with substitute
teaching agencies to secure coverage.
Step 5: Teachers craft coverage plans.
Finally, co-teachers craft coverage plans for any classes they will be missing and reserve a private space in
which to work together during their planning day.

Refining the Co-Planning
Day Structure
In their third year using the practice, and with the new scheduling
system in place, Glynis turned her attention to fine-tuning
structures to help co-teachers build their data-driven instructional
planning practices. The Green Dot team had previously
identified a bright spot in student grades at Ánimo Watts College
Preparatory Academy, which, once investigated, appeared to be
connected to the development and integration of a student work
analysis protocol with school-level teams. Glynis adapted the
pilot quarterly co-planning day structure and coaching agenda to
integrate the student work analysis protocol so that co-teachers
could drive instructional planning from that data. Glynis also
added a mini-lesson component to her morning sessions with
co-teachers that allowed teams to choose to learn more about
an instructional practice they wanted to build. These changes
meant that the professional development provided by co-planning
days aligned to school-level tools and routines, reinforcing their
standard use across teams, and remained highly informed by
and tailored to the unique needs of each co-teaching pair. Coteaching teams then worked to design lessons using the new
practice during their co-planning time later in the day.

How the Student Work Analysis Protocol is
Embedded in the Co-Planning Day
• Step 1: Teachers select recent student work representative of all student subgroups, such as
recent exit tickets, quizzes, or assessments.
• Step 2: Teachers describe the work sample, the context behind the work, and the standards they
aimed to address in their instruction.
• Step 3: Teacher teams review the work samples and descriptions to identify what they “notice” and
“wonder” about the student work.
• Step 4: Teacher teams select a high-leverage co-teaching model that addresses the needs
identified in the student work analysis, and the coach delivers a targeted, on-demand minilesson on the selected model to deepen co-teachers’ understanding and to help ensure effective
implementation.
• Step 5: Utilizing the selected co-teaching model, teacher teams plan instruction and/or
interventions to address the identified student needs.
• Step 6 [Optional]: Teacher teams create an artifact that represents their instructional plans, which
they can use for reflection with their coach later in the day.
For more information on the practice of student work analysis, see A Collaborative Approach to
Student Work Analysis.

Evaluating the Practice for Continuous Improvement
Throughout Green Dot’s implementation of quarterly co-planning days — from the pilot to the present
— Glynis and the team have gathered feedback and data to monitor effectiveness and improve the
development of the practice. Feedback collected from co-teaching pairs focuses on three key areas:
Use of Time, Impact of Time, and Ideas for Improvement.
To collect this feedback, Glynis provides teams with an exit ticket survey at the end of each co-planning
day. The survey contains a rating scale (Strongly Agree, Agree, Disagree, and Strongly Disagree) and
open-ended questions such as the following:

The Impact: Spreading Within and
Beyond Green Dot California
The practice of quarterly co-planning days, with embedded student work analysis and customized
professional development, has been a huge success. Since its implementation, class passage rates
of a C+ or higher nearly tripled for students with disabilities.
Further, recent survey results show teachers value the practice. One hundred percent of the
participating co-teachers stated that the day was a constructive use of their time. Additionally,
100% of the co-teachers noted that the co-planning day improved their relationship with their
co-teacher.
Glynis has continued to facilitate quarterly co-planning days to a growing positive reception. One
participant described co-planning days’ many benefits:

“We are able to be much more intentional with our planning. [It’s] really helpful to have
actual sit-down time to strategize and reflect on student learning. With dealing with
new curriculum and new grading practices and policies, it helps to have a thought
partner in how to approach student learning, what to cut, what to include, and how to
include reteach opportunities in the class.”
In addition, quarterly co-planning days have positively influenced the culture across the Green Dot
network. At the end of the 2022–23 school year, staff, administrators, and teachers shared, via survey
results, that co-planning days are considered to be highly valuable and that co-planning is a positive
practice.
This may be because, as Glynis and the co-teachers have observed, the quarterly co-planning days
provide a different type of professional development than what teachers had previously received from
their coaches and administrators, all of whom have different expertise, passions, and needs. Because
co-planning during the week can often be cut short by the length of prep periods or competing priorities,
the quarterly co-planning days provide the teachers with deep, focused practice and support for using
the student work analysis protocol that they may not otherwise
receive. Teachers also appreciated the extent to which their needs
and preferences drove the focus of the professional development
provided within their co-planning days, rather than it being directed
solely by Glynis.
Based on this success, Green Dot will continue to build the culture
of co-planning across the network. In the 2023–24 school year,
Green Dot will spread the practice of co-planning with student
work analysis to two more campuses, increasing the number of
co-teaching pairs from 10 to 18. Additional co-teachers will try
using student work analysis protocols on a more frequent, biweekly
basis. Finally, Glynis will work to determine the most aligned
measurement of efficacy between co-planning days and student
learning outcomes.
