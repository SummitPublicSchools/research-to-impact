Launching the Practice for High-Quality Implementation

What Makes a Data Cycle High-Quality?
Uses student-level, actionable data aligned to a school-wide learning goal
Follows a consistent routine (weekly/biweekly) embedded in staff planning time
Relies on a teacher-facing dashboard—easy to use, disaggregated by subgroup, and refreshed regularly
Involves clear roles and protocols for how teams analyze data and plan interventions
Prioritizes proactive intervention over reactive reteaching
Tracks and celebrates student progress, especially for students with disabilities
Emphasize that high-quality cycles feel doable and meaningful to teachers—"not another thing," but the way they plan better instruction

Prepare Needed Structures

1
If an existing SIS does not provide educators with access to disaggregated data aligned to a school’s goals, school leadership will need to develop a data dashboard.

Check out How-to - Design and Prototype a Data Dashboard
Determine school-level goal for student success
Data-driven instruction systems can fail to be adopted by teams when the systems are not deeply aligned to a school’s mission and current student needs. Base goals on existing data and past trends so that the goals are realistic and, more importantly, based on specific needs for priority subgroups. Use historical data to create a baseline for the goals prior to beginning interventions in order to measure the effectiveness of the practice. Goals need to be measured regularly and with fidelity over time.

Set up data dashboard
Using school-wide goals, determine which classroom-level data points best measure the goals to be used in the dashboard. These measures need to be relatively easy for teachers to collect with fidelity and consistency. Examples of data include: engagement measures, formative assessments, summative assessments.
Once the dashboard is established, test it for usability by teachers. Will teachers be able to easily disaggregate data at many different population levels, including class periods and subgroups? Can they easily refresh data or upload new data? These factors will help set the dashboard up for successful use.

Design protocols
A protocol provides directions for each step in a process, as well as the order in which steps should be completed, so that different users of the protocol are all doing the same thing to complete the activity. When using data for instructional support, it is important to use a protocol to help guide educators through the data review, data analysis, and instructional planning process in a systematic way that is standardized across teams.
Design the protocol to encourage teachers not only to look at their overall student trends, but also
to focus on individual students or small groups of students (e.g., students with disabilities), paying particular attention to students who have not met learning expectations across several periods of time.
The protocol should then lead teachers to devise interventions to support students based on needs identified in the data.

Design routines and norms for using data
Data entry and use of tool - it's important to systematize data collection and calibrate the quality of data collected in order to establish expectations and create accountability for aligned, ongoing use of data for this purpose.
Implementation and protocol use - School leadership should also attend to the norms for how teams will use the protocol for analyzing data and designing interventions.

Design and integrate staff data routines and norms
Socialize the purpose of routine data cycles
Train teachers on the school’s goals, data collection and quality norms, use of the data dashboard tools, and use of the protocols to analyze data and design interventions
Training should ensure teachers know how to complete technical steps: downloading data into dashboard, using dashboard’s functions effectively, and following the protocol for viewing, analyzing, and acting upon data

Implement Routine Data Cycles
3
Once staff are trained, launch the protocol to implement data cycles on a routine cadence — ideally every two weeks, if not weekly. Each iteration of the cycle consists of three key steps: (1) analyze data, (2) design interventions, and (3) implement interventions.

Analyze Data to Identify Student Needs
Educators, individually and then as a team, look for trends in student data and identify topics or standards that groups of students struggled to master

Key Steps:
Review student data to identify who is struggling, what they’re struggling with, and why
Look for patterns by student group, assignment, or time period

Explore the Example Meeting Protocol and Data Analysis Template from Green Dot
The purpose of analysis is to identify groups of students with common needs, or individual students with specific needs, that can be addressed through interventions in future classes. In this step, teachers look for commonalities among students, assignments, and event dates to better understand why some students mastered content and others did not.

Plan and Design Instruction or Interventions
Based on the needs identified during data analysis, teachers determine how to re-teach content or skills that students missed in prior assignments

Key Steps:
Plan specific interventions aligned to focus area of the root cause
Interventions can take many forms, but in every case should be designed based on what was learned through the data analysis
Set short-term SMART goals for specific students as needed
Once teachers have students grouped by needs based on their analysis of the data, they plan interventions aligned to the different focus areas. Planning by focus area ensures that students learn missing skills, correct misconceptions from prior lessons, or master content to help them better access the next lesson. Interventions can take many forms, but in every case should be designed based on what was learned through the data analysis.

Deliver Interventions
Educators use student groupings identified by the data and implement the interventions they determined would best support those students

Key Steps:
Implement the planned interventions (most important part of the routine data cycle)
Leverage collaborative planning to support in the design and implementation of interventions
Partner with coaches to strengthen practice and identify new resources for improved planning and implementation
The most important part of the routine data cycle is implementation of the planned interventions for students. Structures like collaborative planning can help teachers design and implement interventions with teaching teams. Teachers can also work with coaches to find new resources and improve intervention planning and implementation across data cycles.

The following actions and considerations may be helpful for the successful implementation of instructional interventions:
• Teachers develop classroom norms and routines to make implementing interventions possible.
• Teachers plan a new lesson format or activity to re-teach content to reach a group of students.
• Coaches conduct observations to provide feedback to teachers to support their growth and development implementing data-driven interventions.
• Teachers complete peer observations to access models and examples of interventions in practice.
• Coaches work with teachers to determine areas of growth in implementing interventions.
• Coaches schedule short periods of time with teachers when interventions will happen so that coaches can maximize how many teachers they observe in a day.

Once the interventions are implemented, a data cycle is complete. Teachers will begin the next iteration of the cycle by analyzing data to determine the effectiveness of their prior data-based decisions and subsequent implementation of interventions.

Creating the Conditions that Enable Successful Routine Data Cycles

What are Enabling Conditions?
For routine data cycles, this includes:
Mindsets
Infrastructure (e.g., student information system, scheduling, protocols)
Assessment materials and data dashboards
Staff commitment and time
Ongoing support
Enabling conditions are the minimum conditions needed for successful implementation of a practice
Enabling Conditions describes the minimum conditions needed for successful implementation, including resources, mindsets, staffing, and time allocations.

19
Enabling Conditions: Mindsets
All staff believe that students with disabilities can achieve at grade level when supported with timely, responsive instruction and intervention.
Educators believe they must meet students where they are—even if that means re-teaching concepts, changing lesson structures, or differentiating expectations and supports.
School culture embraces data as a tool for proactive support rather than evaluation.
Teachers and leaders see themselves as collectively responsible for closing gaps, especially for students furthest from opportunity.

20
Enabling Conditions: Infrastructure
Educators must have access to a student information system (SIS) or custom dashboard that:
Provides access to student-level data aligned to school goals
Enables disaggregation by subgroup (e.g., SWD, multilingual learners)
Supports real-time analysis and refreshes predictably (e.g., weekly grade updates)

If an existing SIS does not provide educators with access to disaggregated data, leadership will need to develop a dashboard.

See RDC Teacher Facing Data Dashboard Prototype and How-To: Design and Prototype a Data Dashboard
A student information system provides all staff access to data related to student success, such as student grades, mastery of standards, attendance, or assessment scores. Data can be disaggregated by student subgroups (e.g., multilingual learners, students in intervention, students with disabilities, students of color).

Enabling Conditions: Recommended Materials
Educators also benefit from the following guidance:
Assessment and progress monitoring measures of student performance
Examples: exit tickets, formative and summative assessments, work completion rates
Intervention strategies and associated instructional resources
Routine data protocol for analysis and planning

Various team members must be give time to complete their key tasks

Recommended: 1 hour weekly or biweekly

A Field Example: STEM Prep

Studying RDC in Practice: STEM Prep
STEM Prep’s model of routine data cycles uses a Gradebook Distribution Tool and Gradebook Distribution Protocol. The site has a school-wide strategy and goal for impact, and all staff use the protocol across the campus.

The practice has several key activities that sustain its impact:
All-staff training on the data tool and protocol in Summer PD
Guided use of the protocol in staff meeting sessions
Embedded use of the practice in collaboration structures
Coaching and feedback in biweekly instructional coaching sessions

All-staff professional development sessions review the WHAT, WHY, WHEN and HOW of the tool and protocol.

Appendix A. Improving Enabling Conditions

Driving High-Impact Implementation
When the right mindset, tools, time, and support are in place, routine data cycles become a powerful driver of equitable, high-quality instruction—not just another meeting or task

This section will explore:
How to build shared belief that data is for student support—not compliance—and that all students can succeed with the right instruction
How to create a teacher-facing dashboard with the right data, designed to support timely instructional decisions
How meeting time is essential to making data analysis and planning routine, not reactive
How coaching and PD help teachers move from looking at data to designing effective, targeted interventions
Effective implementation doesn’t just happen—it’s the result of intentionally designing the right conditions for teachers to succeed.

Routine Data Cycles only succeed when teachers believe that data is a tool for helping students—not judging them. A shared belief that all students can succeed is foundational.

Key Strategies:
Connect data use to a clear student success goal
Reinforce that data helps change outcomes—not monitor failure
Model vulnerability—leaders participate in the process
Celebrate small wins to build buy-in and momentum
Use simple metaphors: “The dashboard is the thermostat, not just a thermometer”

Review pgs. 3.4 and 3.10 in the practice chapter
Building a Culture Where Data Drives Support

Leadership actions are about setting vision, ensuring accountability, coaching, and iterating. Structures are the concrete systems, tools, schedules, and routines that make the practice sustainable.

Building a Culture Where Data Drives Support
Leadership Actions:
Set clear goals: Establish school-level goals aligned to mission and student needs.
Communicate purpose and build buy-in: Explain why data cycles matter and connect them to broader school goals.
Model norms and expectations: Reinforce consistent data entry, use of protocols, and timely analysis.
Provide coaching and feedback: Embed regular coaching sessions, grade-level meetings, and co-planning to support teachers.
Use continuous improvement mindset: Collect teacher feedback, adjust tools/protocols, and revise goals as needed.
Celebrate progress and hold accountability: Recognize improvements in student outcomes and ensure fidelity of implementation.
Allocate resources and time: Protect PD time, schedule team meetings, and set aside time for teachers to analyze data.
Structures that Sustain:
Data dashboard: A usable, disaggregated, regularly updated dashboard aligned to school goals.
Protocols: Step-by-step guides for reviewing, analyzing, and acting on data to ensure consistency.
Routines and norms: Expectations for data entry (e.g., weekly grade updates), meeting frequency, and collaborative use of dashboards.
Professional development calendar: Scheduled training sessions to teach use of dashboards, protocols, and intervention planning.
Team structures: Grade-level or content teams, data leads, and designated facilitators for cycles.
Coaching structures: Biweekly coaching cycles, co-planning time, peer observations.
Revision processes: Built-in reviews of goals, dashboard functionality, and protocols on a quarterly/annual basis.

If the dashboard is hard to use, misaligned, or designed for leadership—not teachers—data routines fall apart. Teachers can’t act on what they can’t easily see.

Key Strategies:
Build teacher-facing dashboard focused on instructional decisions
Use real-time, student-level data disaggregated by student groups
Start simple—track key metrics (e.g., assignment completion, assessment mastery)
Refresh data regularly based on grading schedules
Provide hands-on training on both dashboard use and data interpretation

Review pg. 3.12 in the practice chapter
Designing Tools Teachers Actually Use

Without protected time, data analysis and intervention planning won’t happen consistently. Time is the difference between reflective action and surface-level compliance.

Key Strategies:
Embed 45–60 minutes weekly or biweekly into team meetings or PD sessions
Make time sacred: no interruptions or deprioritization from other meetings or duties
Connect data time to existing planning or intervention routines
Use creative scheduling models built around student needs
Include time for both data analysis and designing next steps

Making Time for What Matters

The most common breakdown isn’t in reading data—it’s in translating it into precise, effective instructional moves. Coaching and PD are what turn data into action.

Key Moves for Coaches:
Coach for analysis and instructional action
Facilitate use of the data protocol and guide teams through structured routines
Build an intervention bank to help teachers choose effective strategies
Differentiate your coaching:
New teachers: Focus on dashboard fluency, reading patterns, and following the protocol
Experienced teachers: Push for deeper root-cause analysis and sharper, more targeted intervention design
Support data fluency over time. Use recurring coaching cycles to deepen teachers’ comfort with both the dashboard and the analysis-to-action process
Coaching Data into Action
Coach for analysis and action. Support teachers not just in identifying patterns, but in determining what instructional moves will address them.

Facilitate use of the data protocol. Guide teams through structured routines that move from trend identification to SMART goals and intervention plans.

Model and scaffold intervention design. Offer example moves for common gaps (e.g., reteaching key concepts, using scaffolds, small groups).

Build and reference an intervention bank. Help teachers choose from ready-to-use strategies aligned to student needs.

Differentiate your coaching.

New teachers: Focus on dashboard fluency, reading patterns, and following the protocol.

Experienced teachers: Push for deeper root-cause analysis and sharper, more targeted intervention design.

Support data fluency over time. Use recurring coaching cycles to deepen teachers’ comfort with both the dashboard and the analysis-to-action process.
