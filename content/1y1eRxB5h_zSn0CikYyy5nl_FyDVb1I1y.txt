Introduction
When existing data tools do not meet current needs and a school or network chooses to design a
custom data tool, it is important to invest the necessary time and effort to ensure the tool is useful,
usable, and utilized. Using Excel, Google Sheets, Tableau, or another data visualization tool, teams
can customize a dashboard to import and manipulate data to suit the measurement effort. To do this
successfully, school systems typically need an experienced data team with the knowledge and capacity
to implement these technical needs.
The iterative process necessary to develop an effective data tool can be time-intensive. The following
steps may be helpful when planning for this process.

Design and Develop
First, during the design stage, identify a clear purpose for the data dashboard and define core priorities.
The purpose and priorities should connect to student outcomes (e.g., achievement, GPA, attendance,
or social-emotional skills) aligned with the school’s mission.
The following questions are important to answer
during this phase:
WHO ARE THE PRIMARY USERS? (e.g., teachers,
grade-level teams, school leaders, or central office)
AT WHAT LEVEL SHOULD EDUCATORS ANALYZE
DATA? (e.g., individual students, subgroups, class
periods, courses, teacher, or school)
WHAT WILL EDUCATORS NEED TO BE ABLE TO
DO WITH THIS DATA? (e.g., reflect on a lesson,
reflect on a unit, or identify gaps between subgroups)
HOW MUCH TECHNICAL ABILITY DO
EDUCATORS NEED TO DEVELOP TO USE THE
TOOL EFFECTIVELY?
HOW WILL DATA BE INPUT OR DOWNLOADED
INTO THE TOOL, AND WHO WILL MANAGE THAT
PROCESS?
HOW MUCH TIME WILL EDUCATORS NEED TO
DEVOTE TO THE TOOL OUTSIDE OF MEETINGS?
HOW OFTEN WILL EDUCATORS ACCESS THE
DASHBOARD? (e.g., daily, weekly, biweekly, or
monthly)

Building a Dashboard
at Collegiate
Collegiate Academies found,
early on, that their general
student information system (SIS)
did not allow teachers to view
student mastery of standards by
the types of filters they needed
(e.g., grade levels, class periods,
or subgroups) to evaluate and
respond appropriately to the
data in order to reteach skills
students hadn’t mastered. For
that reason, Collegiate opted to
create their own tool for looking at
students’ progress on standards
and subsequently developed a
spreadsheet dashboard that let
teachers easily see how students
were progressing on each
standard and devise plans for
how to teach and reteach skills.

These questions will help determine how to design the initial prototype of the dashboard. Key stakeholders
may also have valuable input into what the dashboard should look like and how it should function.

Set Up Data Dashboard
Build a prototype of the data dashboard
based on the defined purpose, priorities, and
requirements gathered in the design phase.
Using school-wide goals, determine which
classroom-level data points best measure
the goals to be used in the dashboard.
These measures need to be relatively
easy for teachers to collect with fidelity and
consistency. Examples of data include:
• engagement measures, such as
attendance or assignment completion;
• formative assessments, like exit tickets,
checkpoints, or independent practice; or
• summative assessments, including
project grades, course grades, or unit
tests.
Once the dashboard is established, test it for
usability by teachers.

Building Tools
for Every Level
Schools can choose between multiple,
discrete dashboards that each serve a
primary purpose, or nested dashboards that
provide a one-stop-shop data experience
for an entire school system. For ease of
access, a single, nested dashboard allows
teachers and district leaders alike to view the
same data at different levels of aggregation,
each using the dashboard to meet their
individual objectives. For example, a chief
academic officer can be monitoring school-byschool and student-group-by-student-group
progress, while a school leader can track that
data as well as classroom-by-classroom or
grade-by-grade student progress. Teachers
have access to this same data, but can
focus their efforts on understanding their
own students on a more granular level or
collaborate across their grade-level team.

Test and Improve
Test the prototype with teachers, who can give input on the tool’s utility and ease of use, using a small
set of representative data. Will users be able to easily disaggregate data at many different population
levels, including class periods and subgroups? Can they easily refresh data or upload new data?
Considering these factors will help set the dashboard up for successful use.
The initial testers should be key individuals whose buy-in will be important to garner. By engaging these
individuals early, you provide transparency into the process and can integrate their perspectives, so
they can champion the prototype’s use later. These individuals will likely be:
• lead teachers or department heads,
• coaches,
• school leaders, or
• other educators or leaders whose voices are important to new initiatives.
The first prototype of the dashboard will not be perfect, so it is essential to create something simple and
be prepared for feedback. Continue to iterate on the dashboard with users until it has the functionality
desired to meet the stated objectives.
Throughout the prototyping phase, give special attention to how users actually interact with the
dashboard. Anticipate challenges educators may have and training they may need to use the
dashboard effectively.

Routinely Assess Implementation
and Quality
After developing and refining the prototype,
consider establishing a team structure that allows
regular assessment of the implementation and
quality of the tool. A structured approach allows
teams to systematically evaluate how the tool
is being used and its effectiveness. Frequent
evaluation of sample data can provide insights
into the way individuals and teams are using
the tool, identify emerging trends, and pinpoint
barriers in implementation. NIC partners found a
quarterly review cadence — to review how the tool
is being used and to do a strategic set of empathy
interviews with users at the classroom, school
leadership, and district levels — allowed them to
make timely adjustments to the tool, integration
settings, and training plans for team members.
Backward-plan timelines for each of these steps
to occur based on the final target roll-out date. In
order to train leaders and educators on how to use
the tool before school starts in the fall, testing of
the tool should be complete by the end of the prior
school year.

Developing with the End
User is Essential
The successful development of any
new tool or protocol requires its own
cycles of improvement. Both STEM
Prep and Collegiate Academies
spent approximately a semester getting
feedback from users at regular intervals
through surveys and interviews, which
helped their development teams
troubleshoot issues and improve
inefficiencies. For example, Collegiate
Academies learned that many teachers
did not have enough knowledge on
how to use Google Sheets for them
to create filters on their own. As a
result, the team created filters ahead of
time for teachers and did not take for
granted that teachers could learn how
to use Google Sheets on their own.
In addition, they designed additional
training to increase current and future
staff capacity to use Google Sheets
independently.
