Enabling Conditions: Routine Data Cycles
A Guide for Leaders
This guide is a companion to the Routine Data Cycles practice in the Marshall
Research-to-Impact Practices, addressing common needs and barriers at a systems and
site-leader level.

Why School Leaders Matter
School leaders play a decisive role in whether practices take hold and endure at a school site.
Their responsibilities include:
● Setting clear priorities for routine data cycles and creating culture and mindsets
where data-driven reflection leads to improved outcomes for every student (aligned
vision and mindsets);
● providing time, tools, and protocols to help teachers analyze and act on data
(infrastructure);
● ensuring access to quality assessments and instructional materials so teachers
can act on timely, relevant data (assessment and instructional materials); and
● cultivating skills that make high-quality implementation possible with training and
coaching (staff commitment and ongoing support).
By modeling data-use as a learning practice, establishing the conditions that sustain the
practice, and reinforcing a culture of shared responsibility, leaders ensure that routine data
cycles become a consistent, scalable, and lasting part of a school’s work.

Enabling Conditions for Routine Data Cycles
Aligned Vision & Mindsets
● School leaders share a clear expectation for routine data cycles, including an explicit
definition of roles and responsibilities.
● All staff believe that students with disabilities can achieve at grade level when supported
with timely, responsive instruction and intervention
● School culture values data as a shared language for understanding student progress
and driving instructional decisions
● Teachers and leaders see themselves as collectively responsible for closing gaps for all
students through grade-level access and targeted support that meets students where
they are

Infrastructure (e.g., student information system, scheduling, protocols)
● Ensure (or design) a data dashboard or student information system (SIS) report template
that provides access to disaggregated student-level data aligned to school goals and
that supports real-time analysis, ideally at the classroom level. Start with a simple
teacher-facing tracker and build toward a schoolwide tool over time.
● Adopt a common data-to-action protocol for routine data analysis and action planning
(start with the RDC Template Protocol and adapt it for your site)
● Build a site-wide schedule that protects weekly or biweekly data cycles in teacher prep
or team collaboration spaces
● Build a site calendar that sets data entry due dates and aligns benchmark assessment
windows and staff training with data cycles

Assessment and instructional materials
● Assessment and progress-monitoring measures of student performance (e.g., exit
tickets, formative and summative assessments) with staff trained to administer and score
them consistently
● High-quality instructional materials, including curriculum and intervention strategies and
resources teachers can use when responding to student data

Staff commitment and ongoing support
● A data lead to create, iterate upon, and maintain the data tool
● Time for professional development (PD) on the use of the dashboard and how to design
appropriate interventions for students in response to data
● Coaching that helps educators translate data into targeted instructional responses
The resources provided below strengthen these enabling conditions and offer solutions to common
barriers.

Building a Culture Where Data Drives Support
Routine Data Cycles only succeed when teachers believe that data is a tool for monitoring the
effectiveness of instruction and for helping students make progress. A shared belief that all
students can succeed when provided with the needed supports is foundational.

Key Strategies:
● Connect data-use to a clear student success goal
● Reinforce that data helps change outcomes—not monitor failure
● Model vulnerability by participating in the practice alongside educators. Engage in
coaching with a set of educators to sit close to their work, or run your own data cycle on
schoolwide data with your leadership team and share what you’re learning and changing
● Celebrate small wins to build buy-in and momentum
● Use simple metaphors: “The dashboard is the thermostat, not just a thermometer.”

Infrastructure
Designing Tools Teachers Actually Use
If the dashboard is hard to use, misaligned, or designed for leadership rather than teachers,
data routines fall apart. Teachers can’t act on what they can’t easily see.

Key Strategies:
● Build a teacher-facing dashboard focused on instructional decisions (see How-To:
Design and Prototype a Data Dashboard); Refresh data based on grading schedules
● Use real-time, student-level data disaggregated by student groups (e.g., exit tickets)
● Start simple—track 1-2 key metrics (e.g., power standards, assignment completion (as a
leading indicator only) and assessment mastery)
● Establish step-by-step protocols for reviewing, analyzing, and acting on data to ensure
consistency. For example, a structured data-to-action protocol includes steps to analyze
trends, name focus areas, set SMART goals, and choose interventions (see the full
chapter for detailed guidance).

Making Time for What Matters
Without protected time, data analysis and intervention planning won’t happen consistently. Time
is the difference between reflective action and surface-level compliance.

Key Strategies:
● Embed 45–60 minutes for the full protocol weekly or biweekly into teacher prep blocks,
department or team meetings, or PD sessions when first starting out. Over time, 30-45
minutes will likely be sufficient.
● Make time sacred: take proactive action to ensure there are no interruptions or
deprioritization from other meetings or duties
● Connect data time to existing planning or intervention routines
● Use creative scheduling models built around student needs
● Include time for both data analysis and designing next steps

Assessment and Instructional Materials
High-Quality Assessment and Instructional Materials
Educators are supported with updated and relevant assessment data, clear progress-monitoring
measures, and instructional resources that enable effective data analysis and planning.

Key Strategies
● Assessment and progress-monitoring measures of student performance with staff
trained to administer, score, and enter them consistently.
○ Examples: exit tickets, formative and summative assessments
● Data tool for entering assessment and progress-monitoring results that allows for easy
disaggregation (see Infrastructure above)
● Intervention strategies and associated instructional resources

planning or

● Create and use an intervention strategy bank. Ready-to-use
strategies aligned to common needs reduce planning time and

● Use whole-class reteaching when most students miss the same

students need what, and choose a targeted strategy from the

● Use a structured data-to-action protocol with guiding questions to
take educators through how they analyze trends, name focus areas,
set SMART goals, and choose interventions. (See the full chapter

● Embed coaching in the data cycle. Coaches support teachers in
interpreting the data and designing precise instructional responses
(See the full chapter for additional guidance.)
● Review student work alongside the data. This helps clarify
misunderstandings and informs stronger intervention choices.
● Leverage existing curriculum resources. Teachers can use what
already exists in the curriculum—such as built-in reteach lessons,
practice activities, or extension tasks—to respond directly to what
the data shows

Staff Commitment and Ongoing Support
From Data to Action: Investing in Staff and Support Systems
Sustaining effective routine data cycles requires dedicated time, consistent coaching, and
ongoing professional learning that help educators translate data into actionable instructional
moves.

Tips for Implementation
● Regular time for educators to analyze data and plan interventions on their own or with
the guidance and support of their grade level or department team
● Include special education teachers and interventionists in data cycles, connecting
findings between course mastery and IEP goal progress
● A data lead to create, iterate upon, and maintain the data tool

○ Getting started: 1-3 hours to set up a simple teacher-facing tracker that each
educator can copy and use in their setting (see example template)
○ Building a schoolwide tool (e.g., STEM Prep): up to 40 hours for initial creation and
iteration, plus 3+ hours monthly for maintenance
● Time for professional development (PD) on the use of the dashboard and how to design
appropriate interventions for students in response to data
○ Provide hands-on training on both dashboard use and data interpretation. Follow up
with coaching or personalized support as needed.
○ 1 hour biweekly PD time for educators when first launching the practice
○ 1–2 hours prior to the school year for initial training for new and returning staff
○ 10–20 hours for development of training materials
See Making Time for What Matters under “Infrastructure”

Coaching Data into Action

instructional moves. Coaching and PD are what turn data into action.
