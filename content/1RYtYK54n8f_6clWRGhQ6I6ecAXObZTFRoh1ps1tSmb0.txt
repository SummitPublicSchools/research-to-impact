The Challenge
Students with disabilities often do not receive high-quality, differentiated instruction partly due to limited or ineffective use of data to drive instructional design.

This issue is compounded by educator lack of access to tools for making timely decisions.

As a result, the most disadvantaged students are at risk of falling behind.

Why is the Use of Data Important?
Routine data analysis can support high-quality instruction for all learners.
Data-driven instruction is foundational to effective MTSS.
Lack of knowledge and training around data-driven problem solving is a key barrier to implementing MTSS with fidelity.1
Disaggregating student data by priority populations (e.g., students with disabilities) helps educators make important and data-driven instructional decisions.
1 Drury et al, “Educational Leaders’ Perspectives”
DISAGGREGATED DATA ALLOWS FOCUS ON PRIORITY POPULATION.
Whole-class, or aggregate, data masks differences that may exist between groups or individual students. Therefore, it is important that data dashboards are able to disaggregate by group — particularly for the different subgroups within our priority population, students with disabilities who are Black and/or Latinx, and living in poverty — allowing teachers to unpack what is happening in their classroom across subgroups. With this detailed data, teachers can ensure they are responding to the students who most need additional support.

Evidence-Base for Effective Routine Data Cycles
Data can identify and mitigate bias in the system
Reviewing and comparing data across student subgroups can help teachers:
reflect on how instruction was planned and delivered
attend to how assignments were graded
With disaggregated data educators can identify trends and patterns of student performance on a variety of assessments to see discrepancies and reflect on how their systems or practices contribute to those discrepancies. 1
1 Kania and Kramer, “The Equity Imperative in Collective Impact”

Defining the Practice

What are Routine Data Cycles?
The regular analysis of student data to proactively design classroom-based interventions, typically using a customized data dashboard.

What a Routine Data Cycle Is and Is Not
IS
IS NOT
A proactive, classroom-embedded practice for instructional planning
A generic data team meeting or test score review
Rooted in real-time, actionable data (e.g., exit tickets, grades), using a teacher-facing data dashboard that can be disaggregated by student demographics
Based on infrequent or summative data (e.g., state tests only) via a schoolwide student information system that cannot disaggregate demographic data
Centered on teacher-driven inquiry and intervention
Driven only by admin or assessment teams
Alignment of interventions to learning standards and specific skill gaps
A re-teaching of everything missed, without clear focus
Implemented on a routine cycle (weekly or biweekly)
A one-time intervention plan or reactive scramble near report cards
Enables targeted small group or 1:1 supports
Always whole-class reteaching

Core Components of Routine Data Cycles
Analyze data to identify student needs. Educators, individually and then as a team, look for trends in student data and identify topics or standards that groups of students struggled to master.
Designs interventions for students. Based on the needs identified during data analysis, teachers determine how to re-teach content or skills that students missed in prior assignments.
Deliver interventions. Educators use student groupings identified by the data and implement the interventions they determined would best support those students.

A Field Example: STEM Prep

Case Study: STEM Prep’s Routine Data Cycles
Built the Gradebook Distribution Tool: a teacher-facing dashboard
Created the Gradebook Distribution Protocol: step-by-step process for reviewing data, setting goals, and planning interventions
Solidified Key Structures: Schoolwide goal + all staff required to use the protocol
At Crown Prep Academy, STEM Prep’s 5–8 school in Los Angeles, leaders noticed that teachers often scrambled at the end of grading periods to push students to submit missing assignments. This practice raised passing rates but did little to build mastery. To address this, the principal and staff hypothesized that a custom data dashboard could help teachers monitor student progress in real time and respond earlier to individual needs. They launched the work by developing a gradebook toolkit, training teachers to use it, and embedding data discussions into existing professional development.

What RDC Looked Like in Practice
Teachers downloaded grade reports → dashboard disaggregated data by learning goal, time period, student group.
Dashboard flagged students at risk.
Teachers set SMART goals and design interventions
Monthly PD meetings were used for scheduled data pulls and action planning.
Over time, teachers began doing it independently.
This is what Routine Data Cycles look like in action: teachers pull grade reports, see which students are struggling, set a SMART goal, and act immediately. And because data cycles happen on a schedule; it’s proactive, not reactive.
“This is what Routine Data Cycles look like in action: teachers pull grade reports, see which students are struggling, set a SMART goal, and act immediately. And because data cycles happen on a schedule, it’s proactive, not reactive.”

Supports That Sustained the Practice
Summer PD: All staff trained on schoolwide goals and the use of tools + protocol.
Staff Meetings: Protected time for guided data cycles.
Embedded Data Entry: Built time into bi-weekly PD for data work.
Coaching: Biweekly sessions to move from analysis to action.
Culture: Leaders reframed data use (thermostat vs. thermometer) and celebrated small wins.
During early roll out, leaders adapted in response to barriers. They improved the tool, built buy-in with simple metaphors, and shifted plans from ‘the student needs to...’ to ‘I will...for the student.’ Coaching and repetition made it stick. As the model spread, leaders kept adjusting. They found teachers weren’t updating data outside PD—so they built time into PD itself. That culture of continuous improvement was essential.

Why it Worked
The model at STEM Prep worked because data was:

Aligned with schoolwide priorities.
Routine: built into PD, not optional.
Actionable: always tied to an intervention.
Disaggregated: surfaced needs of subgroups.
This wasn’t just a dashboard. It was a system - aligned, routine, actionable, and equitable. That’s what made it work.
