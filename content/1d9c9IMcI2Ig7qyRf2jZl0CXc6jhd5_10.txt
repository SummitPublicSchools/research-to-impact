WHAT IS THE PRACTICE?
Educators develop strategic relationships with students, families, and local organizations
that provide postsecondary resources to students. Educators can then support and
progress-monitor families’ submission and completion of applications to the organizations.

WHY IS IT IMPORTANT?
• Families need opportunities to learn more about the different supports and resources
available for their students after high school.
• Nationally, only about 14% of IEP meetings for students with disabilities who are 14
years and older include a vocational rehabilitation counselor. Another 17% of IEP
meetings include someone from a different outside organization.1
• Only eight percent of individuals with disabilities in the US who were 16–24 years
old applied for vocational rehabilitation services, even though an average of 56% of
applicants received services.2
• Many families experience significant barriers when applying for and accessing services.
By acting as intermediaries, educators can better ensure that students who need and
qualify for services receive those services.

The Challenge
Families need guidance to navigate student enrollment
in community-based organizations.
Many students with disabilities need transition services to help them reach their goals after high school.
Community-based organizations offer students with disabilities services, including educational day
programs, job training programs, and support for instructional accommodations for college students.
For many students, these services are essential for their successful transition out of high school.
Families do not always have the time to get all the information they need about community-based
organizations and make the connections themselves. Unfortunately, these organizations can also be
mired in bureaucracy and difficult to navigate. Families can struggle with different steps required for
enrollment in a program:
• Understanding program eligibility criteria (e.g., income level, disability type, or service needs)
• Completing initial applications
• Submitting required documentation (e.g., income verification, documentation of disability, or IEP)
• Participating in intake interviews
• Attending meetings to develop a service plan for the individual student
For North Star Academy Lincoln Park High School (LPHS) in Newark, NJ, connecting students
with disabilities and their families with these organizations is key to helping students reach their
postsecondary goals. Uncommon Schools has always worked to connect students and families with
community-based organizations that provide services to students after high school, but they did
not have a clear process and set of resources to support case managers and college and career
counselors in making these connections.
Tonya Ballard, in her roles as special education instructional leader and part-time career and college
readiness teacher, worked with a diverse set of stakeholders from the network: Joe Frick, Uncommon’s
career and technical education manager;
Alex Walker, college counselor; Roydon
Pellew, alumni success counselor;
Meghan Fitzgerald, cross-regional
manager of special education; and
other members of the Uncommon
team. Together, the group brainstormed
solutions and identified a clear starting
point — enrollment in the Division of
Vocational and Rehabilitation Services
(DVRS) — to develop a process that
could be replicated across schools and
programs in the network to strategically
use resources to meet the needs of
Uncommon’s Continuum of Postsecondary Readiness Supports for

The Practice
Working Directly with Community-Based Organizations
To Support Families
After bringing the team together to unpack the challenge and consider solutions, Tonya set out to create
a process that could be the bridge between community organizations, like DVRS, and the students
they aimed to serve. Tonya’s first step was to research local organizations in order to connect students
and their families with services that could help them long after they graduate. Being the link between
families and external organizations meant that Tonya, as the students’ case manager and college and
career counselor, was responsible for making these connections and functioned as the families’ point
person at school.
To better support students and families with the application process, Tonya knew she would need to
fully understand DVRS and the steps to apply. She reviewed their website, reached out to Joe and
other colleagues who had experience working with DVRS or similar organizations, and made a list of
questions she could not easily answer through research. Next, Tonya established contact with a point
person at DVRS, from whom she was able to gather information about the program and a host of
services they provide, including:

Tonya’s contact directed her to DVRS’s online
enrollment information. She then sent the
application to all her students’ families with a
message emphasizing the importance of applying
while students are still in high school. By applying,
students could find out more about what DVRS
provides, learn how to fully enroll in the program,
and even begin receiving services.
At least one student’s family began applying to
DVRS in response to Tonya’s email. When the
family hit a roadblock in understanding how to
complete their application process, they reached out
to Tonya for help. Together, Tonya and the family
finished the application, which allowed Tonya the
opportunity to develop stronger bonds with the
family and learn the process deeply.

As soon as the student and their family started having meetings with staff from DVRS, Tonya asked to
join. As a result of Tonya’s strong relationship with the student and their family, everyone was eager to
have Tonya join as an important member of the team. By joining the student’s meetings with DVRS,
Tonya strengthened her connection with the DVRS support provider and deepened her understanding
of what families experience when they enroll in DVRS programming.
Inspired by her success with the first family, Tonya asked to be included in meetings with all of the other
students from her school to whom she had recommended DVRS, so that she could similarly support
their transitions from high school to life after high school. Fortunately, the application for vocational
rehabilitation services in New Jersey provides a location for the name and contact information
of the applicant’s special education teacher. Providing this information gave DVRS authorization
to communicate with students’ special educators and removed the need to have families sign an
additional form to consent to this contact.
Tonya also had families add her as a contact in all their email communications with DVRS. This allowed
Tonya to reach out to the assigned DVRS counselors, give them background on her role, and ask for
any paperwork she could support students to complete. In this way, Tonya could track the documents
and steps each student and their family needed to complete and help make sure all the materials were
submitted. Acting in this role further fostered Tonya’s trusted relationships, allowing her to continue
supporting families. The importance of Uncommon’s investment in Tonya’s role as a career and college
counselor and point of contact for her students’ families cannot be understated.

How Uncommon Implemented
the Practice
Over time, Tonya devised a set of steps to use throughout the year to increase the impact of the practice.

Step 1: Develop Relationships with Families
• Better understand families by inviting them to school events that allow for personal interactions.
• Start building trust with families as their point person by providing them with information through workshops.
Step 2: Connect with Community-Based Organizations
•
•
•
•

Research community-based organizations, both public and private.
Use existing relationships to build new connections with organizations and individuals at the organizations.
Determine whether there are opportunities for students to receive community-based supports on campus.
Confer with established internal supports about community-based organizations.

Step 3: Coordinate Materials
• Understand the steps and requirements that families will need to satisfy to complete applications.
• Identify barriers families may encounter when completing requirements.
• Ensure families provide consent for communication between the school and the organization.
Step 4: Support Students and Families with Requirements
• Assign the completion of applications to students as homework.
• Hold in-person workshops for families to get support completing applications.
• Create a way of tracking the completion of steps and applications (e.g., application complete, application
submitted, intake meeting held, services offered).
Step 5: Follow Up After Application
• Regularly follow up with families who do not submit materials.
• Connect with organizations to maintain relationships.
• Document successful strategies and useful materials.

At the same time Tonya was gaining knowledge about DVRS, she was also collaborating with the
special education and college and career readiness teams to create quarterly workshops for families.
These sessions let families learn about specific programs that support students with disabilities and
meet Uncommon staff members and outside organizations who are resources to them and their
students. These activities strengthened families’ trust in their students’ teacher, taught them new
information, and helped facilitate future interactions with key personnel.

A Sample of Quarterly Family Workshops
Tonya and the college and career readiness team developed a sequence of four
workshops that counselors or case managers can host throughout the school year.
QUARTER 1: SEPTEMBER
Topics include collaborating with my young adult in finalizing their transition
plan; an explanation of the curriculum/transition process for each quarter;
documentation that families should gather; and key online systems, including
PowerSchool, YouScience, Overgrad, Common App, and FAFSA.
QUARTER 2: NOVEMBER
Topics include completing the FAFSA; making early decisions for the college
process; finding a mentorship, internship, job, or portfolio, depending on the
transition plan; financing college; updating Overgrad.
QUARTER 3: MARCH
Topics include summer program applications, scholarships, preparing for the next
grade/retention conversations, spring break ideas, and DVRS sign-up.
QUARTER 4: MAY
Topics include preparing for end-of-year activities (e.g., graduation, field trips,
ceremonies, meetings) and scholarships.

The Impact
Learning and Codifying Lessons to Expand this Practice
As Tonya has continued to work to connect families to community-based organizations, she has
discovered strategies to increase the number of students applying to programs. One strategy she
has employed is making applications into homework assignments for students to complete. She
uploads the applications to a Google Classroom that students and families can access. She has
also found that it is very important to continue to follow up with families who do not at first complete
applications. By keeping track of these families and connecting with them repeatedly, Tonya is able
to build a more trusting relationship with them as they see how reliable and supportive she is. Her
persistent follow-up also helps her discover the barriers that arise for families, which she can work to
mitigate in future years.
Tonya has been able to use her knowledge and experience with this practice to create a guide for
special educators and college and career counselors throughout the Uncommon Schools network.
In the guide, educators can access information, structures, and materials from the work that Tonya,
the special education team, and the college and career readiness team developed and expanded
upon during the team’s engagement in the NIC, so all Uncommon families of students with disabilities
can be connected with organizations that provide important resources for their students. As a result,
Uncommon has been able to increase the capacity of special educators and college and career
readiness counselors and build partnerships with local organizations and guest speakers to broaden
the array of career opportunities students can access.

Uncommon Schools serves approximately 19,000 students in grades K–12 across New York City, Rochester, NY, Camden,
NJ, Newark, NJ, and Roxbury, MA. Focal schools participating in the Networked Improvement Community included
Uncommon Charter High School in Brooklyn, NY, and North Star Academy Lincoln Park High School (LPHS) in Newark, NJ.
Uncommon Schools is a part of the Marshall Street Networked Improvement Community, a three-year intentional
community of 10 public charter school networks nationwide aiming to make dramatic gains for Black and Latinx students with
disabilities experiencing poverty. Collectively, the 10 networks identified over 20 best practices that improve learning outcomes
and school culture for students positioned furthest from opportunity. Learn more here.
