Practice Overview
What is the Practice?
The collaborative analysis of student
work by an interdisciplinary teaching
team, specifically a general and a special
educator, to collect and review data on
student strengths and needs in order to
plan instructional next steps.

4.3

Student Work Analysis
Across Any Team
Within the NIC, CMOs featured in this
chapter focused their energy on the
collaborative student work analysis done
between a general and a special education
teacher. However, it is important to
highlight that whenever multiple educators
come together and look at students’ work,
particularly when those educators possess
different areas of expertise, the findings
will be more nuanced than those coming
from individual or siloed review of the
same work. We encourage leaders to think
about other teams within their schools,
such as grade-level or department-level
teams, that can benefit from a student
work analysis routine to drive data-based
decision-making together.

Why is Student Work Analysis Important?
Black and Latinx students with disabilities experiencing poverty have been underserved for decades.
To reverse the effects of learning and opportunity gaps, students must receive the learning support
they need, when they need it. When teachers engage in rigorous student work analysis, they are
enabled to meet students’ exact needs in a rapid manner.1

Why it Works
Collaborative student work analysis using an instructionally focused protocol meets the needs of Black
and Latinx students with disabilities experiencing poverty by:
BUILDING EDUCATORS’ CAPACITY TO UNDERSTAND ROOT CAUSES
OF MISUNDERSTANDINGS
Reviewing student work with an explicit focus on students’ “misconceptions, errors and learning
difficulties” allows teachers to identify the best instructional practices to address said needs. Doing this
review with a colleague who has a different area of expertise allows a teacher to see a wider and more
nuanced set of root causes.2 This was demonstrated by collaborating teachers in the NIC, who, after
multiple rounds of collectively engaging in a student work analysis protocol, began to “take on” each
other’s lenses. This allowed them to expedite students’ growth because they were gradually learning
from one another and “thinking” like their colleagues.
ENSURING CRITICAL ALIGNMENT BETWEEN TEACHERS
Collaborative work analysis helps teachers gain a shared understanding of grade-level rigor so that
both teachers can teach toward it. This makes instruction from each teacher and toward IEP and
course goals more efficient and more impactful.
EXPLICITLY ACKNOWLEDGING THE IMPACT OF STUDENTS’ LEARNING NEEDS
ON THEIR WORK
By including specific knowledge of students’ learner profiles and IEPs (including their disabilities and
accommodations) in the analysis of student work, educators reduce blind spots around the “why” of
students’ approach to content and learning activities.
LEVERAGING MULTIPLE FORMS OF DATA
Focusing regular analysis on students’ work in addition to quantitative data allows teachers to
supplement numbers with rich, illustrative examples of students’ work and processing.3 For many
teachers, this can reinvigorate their investment in data-informed instruction as it is highly studentcentric.4

Bringing this Practice to Life
INTEGRATING STUDENT WORK ANALYSIS INTO ROUTINES
AT GREEN DOT PUBLIC SCHOOLS CALIFORNIA
In 2020–21, the principal at Green Dot Public Schools’ Ánimo Watts College Preparatory Academy (“Watts”)
came into her role with a deep investment in engaging teachers in student work analysis in service of
proactively addressing student needs. As she was iterating on that practice, co-teachers were simultaneously
beginning to participate in quarterly co-planning days.
A year later, though survey data showed that teachers loved the co-planning days, student outcomes data
continued to display large achievement gaps. The improvement team hypothesized that, perhaps, teachers
weren’t making substantial changes to their instruction based on student work. They grappled with how
to address this discrepancy without overwhelming teachers with a “new” initiative and completed a larger
stepback to look at their performance data. This led the team to identify a bright spot in grade improvements
at Ánimo Watts. Upon investigation, the team learned that the site had adopted a new student work analysis
practice at the school that worked well with the schoolwide collaboration and coaching structures. Based on
data and stakeholder interviews, the team decided to adopt Watts’s student work analysis protocol (SWA) for
further iteration and spread across programs.

In the fall of 2022, Green Dot made the SWA the central focus of the professional development
hour that launches each co-planning day. The focus on student work analysis has three core goals:
• Ground teachers’ collaboration time in students’ learning
• Provide a direct link to the subsequent co-planning time
• Enable teachers to intentionally plan to address the newly uncovered needs

After a semester of use, promising data began cropping up throughout Watts’s classrooms. Of their
eight co-taught classrooms, seven of them saw students’ grades increase such that more than 84%
of students were passing their courses.
The example below describes how two teachers, Heather, an 11th grade general education math
teacher, and Justin, her special education counterpart, used the shortened SWA protocol to review
student exit tickets from the previous day in one of their biweekly co-planning meetings.

Sorting Student Work and Discussing the Lesson’s Context
For this meeting, Heather and Justin decided to review a set of work for the full class. Heather
brought the exit tickets she had scored against their standards-aligned rubric in advance of the
meeting. The teachers confirmed their shared understanding of exemplary completion of the
exit ticket, which asked students to (1) interpret a word problem, (2) create an aligned one-step
equation, (3) solve that equation, and (4) explain and justify their work in writing.
Next, they sorted their students’ work twice: first to gain a picture of overall performance, then to
identify any gaps between students with and without disabilities.

Their findings showed Heather and Justin that they needed to dedicate ample time to
understanding the exact needs of their students with disabilities. Understanding the root of
students’ misconceptions would allow them to provide the necessary support to close the gaps
between students with and without IEPs.
Before moving to the next part of their student work analysis — noticing and wondering about
students’ work — Heather and Justin talked through what they had done to instruct on this skill
prior to the exit ticket. In previous days, they had taught students the skill of using arrows to
connect components of a word problem to portions of the aligned equation. They had also taught
students the acronym “RAD” — that is, “Restate the question, Answer the question, Defend your
work and thinking.”

Noticing and Wondering
Next, Heather and Justin shifted into “noticings and wonderings,” focusing on work at each score
level one by one. The teachers reviewed the work of students who scored a 3 or 4 and noted
that these students were able to successfully interpret the word problem, accurately solve their
equation, and then provide a correct explanation of their work. Heather noticed that students had

Heather and Justin then moved on to discussing students who scored a 0 or 1. This group was

focus entirely on that subgroup. Justin noticed that two students, Jayme and Edwin, had struggled

who scored a 0-1, Kayla, had incorrectly transcribed numbers from the word problem and had
completed the equation in the wrong order. This caused her to produce a wrong answer which she

justification, yet incorrectly. Across their analysis of this group, Heather and Justin were able to
group these and other students into categories depending on the needs students had demonstrated
with the multi-step problem-solving process.
Finally, the conversation shifted to looking at the work of students who scored a 2. For the entire
class as well as the subset of students with disabilities, Heather noticed a clear trend: students
had correctly interpreted the word problem and accurately solved the ensuing equation, yet they
had struggled to defend their work in writing. The teachers focused their attention on students with
IEPs, noticing that their written responses were highly literal. So, rather than explaining how they
had arrived at their answer to the equation, these students had detailed the steps of their work (for
example, “First, I wrote 250, then, I wrote a division sign...”). Justin again wondered if his sentence
starter accommodations had confused students who received them. Whereas for students scoring
a 3 or 4 he had realized that those accommodations were in fact helpful, for the students scoring a
2 he wondered if his sentence starters were too long and should have, instead, been more concise
(“Restate, Answer, Defend”).

Clarifying Root Causes
Having extensively reviewed students’ work, Heather and Justin worked to uncover the root causes
of students’ misunderstandings. They began by discussing students who had scored a 3 or 4.
For these students, they realized that most of the students who had struggled with concision had
arrived to class late, thereby missing the mini-lesson that Heather and Justin had team-taught at
the beginning of the lesson focused on being targeted and specific in written responses.
Next, they focused on students who scored a 2. With her increased knowledge of students’
accommodations and needs based on multiple rounds of student work analysis, Heather built on
Justin’s wondering about the sentence stems and realized that students scoring at this level were
getting lost in the complicated explanations that they had provided. Justin posited that “less could
be more” for this group of students.

Defining Focus and Brainstorming Aligned Supports
Finally, Heather and Justin moved to action. They began by articulating their focus and goals for
the entire class. Based on what they had seen in their students’ work, they decided to focus on the
skill of explaining mathematical processes and thinking in writing. They agreed that this skill applies
broadly to the application of multiple content standards within their mathematics course, and it
helps students with other courses and content, namely English Language Arts.
They set the goal that within three weeks, 85% of all students will score a 3 or higher on their
written explanations of the day’s math content. They did not set a separate goal for students
with IEPs, but instead planned to focus their instructional efforts to offer re-teaching and
accommodations to ensure these students could demonstrate proficiency at the same target levels
as their peers. They agreed that regardless of the math content for the next three weeks, they
would add in opportunities for students to practice written defenses of their thinking.
Finally, they discussed aligned instructional strategies. Justin noted specific considerations for
students based on their learning profiles. For example, Edwin has a speech-to-text accommodation
he is often hesitant to use that could assist in his written justification of his response, while
Jayme benefits from examples of what is expected in order to reference them as models for his
own attempts. Justin decided that a focus on shared writing for daily written defenses was an
instructional approach that could meet both of these students’ needs. He decided to work with the
two students together in a small group to model aloud exactly how a strong written response could
sound and to practice multiple examples verbally, then in written form, so they could gradually
demonstrate the skill with greater independence. Justin decided that after a week of this practice,
he would gradually shift the task of articulating a response to Edwin and Jayme so that they could
gain practice with it on their own. Turning their attention to Kayla, Justin and Heather discussed

How to Implement this Practice
Before School Begins
Step 1: Determine Analysis Cadence
Regular student work analysis dramatically improves the quality of student interventions when paired
with collaborating teachers’ co-planning time. As school leaders are working to carve out time for
multiple collaborative practices, they may consider creative ways to structure in student work analysis
time, such as:
• using a portion of regular professional development time for collaborating teachers to meet;
• supporting teachers to use 5–10 minutes of longer instructional blocks for daily work analysis; or
• having collaborating teachers use an alternating schedule for their weekly common prep,
focusing on student work analysis in week A and co-planning in week B.
Note: This structure does not work for co-teachers who need to co-plan weekly.

Step 2: Select a Student Work Analysis Protocol
Having a standardized protocol is essential to help teachers understand how to analyze student work.
School leaders can choose to adopt a strong, existing protocol, such as Green Dot’s, or they might
modify or create their own protocol. Successful protocols include the following elements:

Celebrating successes
Reviewing data together

Of paramount importance is that school leaders and instructional coaches are clear on and fluent in the
SWA protocol prior to introducing it to teachers.

Step 3: Launch the Protocol with the Entire Staff and Schedule
in Spiraled Sessions
Prior to having collaborating teachers initiate their student work analysis practice, leaders launch
the protocol.
Commit 45–60 minutes to initial professional development that:
• shares rationale for the collaborative analysis of students’ work, focusing on the unique lenses that
general and special educators bring to this practice, and
• introduces staff to the student work analysis and response protocol.

Step 4: Establish
Coaching Support
The impact of coaching on the efficacy of
student work analyses is highly beneficial. When
a supportive third party joins the discussion,
collaborating teachers can be pushed to amplify
their individual expertise in reviewing students’
work and to think more creatively about how to
address the root causes of students’ needs.
Before launching this practice with collaborating
teachers, school leaders should determine
when coaches will support work analysis
meetings. Coaches’ presence is crucial to
the initial success of the collaborative student
work analysis and to the increasing efficacy of
analysis by pairs who need additional support.
Ideally, coaches join every student work
analysis meeting:
• as the protocol is launching at their site, or
• if either collaborating teacher is very new to
their content or to teaching.

Coaches join meetings monthly or quarterly:
• if coach availability and portfolio do not
allow for weekly attendance, or
• once teachers are effectively going through the protocol with minimal facilitation.
When coaching collaborative teaching teams in the use of student work analysis, there are two common
needs for coaches to keep in mind to ensure teams leverage their individual and collective expertise
and knowledge of students:
• Push teachers to “notice and wonder” deeply. A coach at Green Dot noticed that, when
learning how to use the SWA protocol, collaborating teachers often have the inclination to move
quickly to instructional next steps. By encouraging collaborating teachers to linger on the notice
and wonder step and go deeper in the analysis of students’ work — especially through each
teacher’s unique lens and expertise — the collaborating teachers have more time to bounce ideas
off of one another. This back-and-forth builds rapport and understanding and gets to the most
accurate and pressing needs for students’ learning.
• Inquire about accommodations. Coaches should ensure that students have received their
appropriate accommodations before the analysis begins. This inquiry creates a space for
necessary dialogue to occur if accommodations are not being provided, and also ensures that
teachers are looking at the most accurate representation of students’ learning.

Before the Meeting
Step 1: Collect Student Work
In order to analyze student work, teachers gather a representative sample of their class’s most recent
scored work (for example, an exit slip, quiz, or test).

During the Meeting
The goal of collaborative, interdisciplinary
student work analysis and response is to
carefully review representative students’ work
for progress toward rigorous, grade-level
content standards and for manifestations of
disabilities that may be inhibiting students from
accessing that content. The findings from the
student work analysis process inform upcoming
lessons so that barriers to access are removed
before instruction occurs, increasing the
ability for all students — particularly those with
disabilities — to successfully master content the
first time.
During the meeting, teachers follow their
school’s student work analysis protocol. Most
protocols include the four steps described on
the following page.

The Value of Recent Data
After the first year of implementing SWA
efforts, Green Dot’s improvement lead
realized that when teachers focused their
student work analysis on the previous
day’s exit ticket or writing sample, they
were able to spend their time addressing
their students’ most immediate, pressing
needs. Conversely, when teachers
brought a “bigger” unit assessment from
three weeks prior, the findings were often
outdated or too broad to feel immediately
actionable.

When analyzing students’ work,
it is imperative that teachers are
getting an accurate assessment
of what students know. For students who
have designated accommodations, it is
crucial that those accommodations are
provided. If not, collaborating teachers
will have an incorrect understanding of a
students’ knowledge and skill. For example,
a student who is entitled to having text read
aloud before responding to comprehension
questions, but who does not receive that
accommodation, may present as struggling
with inferential questions when, in fact, they
were struggling with decoding the text.

Collaborative Student Work Analysis Protocol
STEP 1: CELEBRATE SUCCESSES
Collaborating teachers reflect on ways in which their collaborative student
work analysis and planning has positively impacted their practice and their
students’ outcomes. Teachers ground themselves in the work’s criteria for
success and revisit the instructional support that was provided prior to and
during the work sample or assessment, including accommodations.
STEP 2: REVIEW DATA
Collaborating teachers review each representative set of students’ work
and elevate what is seen in the work itself without planning solutions. Both
teachers are taking an investigative stance, seeking to note objectively what
they see, and an inquisitive stance, seeking to determine why and how
these results came about. They use guiding questions to push themselves to
elaborate on noticings and wonderings, identifying causes of strengths that
can be replicated and using their unique expertise to get to the root causes of
misconceptions or challenges.
STEP 3: PLAN INTERVENTIONS
Collaborating teachers identify specific next steps for each group of students
reflected in the representative samples, ensuring that there is an explicit focus
on the needs of students with disabilities. Teachers articulate what they want
to see next in students’ work.
Collaborating teachers discuss what they need to address next, based on the
root cause reflections and aligned to their articulated foci, as well as how they
can best proceed in ways that are accessible to students.
STEP 4: ASSIGN NEXT STEPS
Collaborating teachers explicitly connect their proposed intervention
responses to their next steps for planning and instruction, confirming what role
and specific responsibilities each will take to enact their plans.
For a more detailed description and guidance, see Green Dot’s Student Work Analysis Protocol
(2022–23) in the appendix. Alternative versions available for reference include the Green Dot
Biweekly Co-Planning Agenda with embedded student work analysis (2022–23) and the

ACHIEVING ALIGNMENT FOR GRADE-LEVEL RIGOR AT EDNOVATE
Collaborative student work analysis has benefits beyond highlighting students’ most pressing needs. The
practice requires reviewing students’ work together against the expectations of student performance for that
activity. At Ednovate, collaborating teachers Katie (general education, English) and Jerry (special education)
found that by collaboratively analyzing students’ work, they grew in their shared understanding of what
should be expected of their students, regardless of IEP status.

In the spring of 2023, Katie and Jerry began
working with a coach to help them optimize
their collaborative partnership. Prior to
that time, Katie was inconsistently scoring
work and inputting the information into
Gradebook, their grades platform, but Jerry
neither helped with evaluating work nor
had access to the submitted results. In an
effort to increase alignment as a teaching
team, the teachers, with the support of
their coach, began engaging in daily data
tracking, collecting, scoring, and analyzing
against a standards-aligned rubric.
Through this process, Katie and Jerry
realized that they were not, in fact, in
alignment about what rigorous student work
looked like at the end of the lesson. Through
their discussions, the teachers realized
that Jerry was holding a higher bar for
student responses than Katie. v realization
pushed them to collectively raise the bar
for what they expected when evaluating
students’ work and to amplify the rigor of the
questions that they were asking of students
during lessons and in students’ work. At the
close of the quarter in which they shifted to
this type of student work analysis, Katie and
Jerry’s class saw an increase in their course
pass rate from 79% to 97%.

A COLLABORATIVE APPROACH TO STUDENT WORK ANALYSIS

Summary
The practice of analyzing student work to inform future instruction is relatively common in schools.
However, when general and special teachers collectively analyze students’ work through their unique
lenses, they are able to build a rich and detailed understanding of strengths and needs in a way that a
single educator cannot. In order to best serve the needs of all students, particularly Black and Latinx
students with disabilities, instruction needs to be highly tailored and targeted to individual student
needs. When students’ work and learning are evaluated simultaneously by content experts (general
education teachers) and accessibility experts (special education teachers), students’ needs are
identified more precisely, and aligned supports are crafted with both high rigor and access in mind.
All teachers are then able to more effectively respond to the needs of individual students as well
as the whole class. This response might look like implementing high-impact small groups based on
academic data, rather than simply grouping students based on their ability designations. Alternatively,
this collaborative student work analysis could lead educators to identify important moments for wholeclass instruction. Analyzing student work as a team ensures that all teachers in a classroom are deeply
knowledgeable about student needs and the gaps of the class as a whole. This leads to a greater
feeling of shared responsibility for all learners.

PRACTICE TOOL

Green Dot’s Student Work Analysis Protocol (2022–23)
Note: Questions that are likely to be within the unique purview of special educators are denoted with “SPED.” Ideally, however,
as this process occurs more regularly, many of these questions can be increasingly answered by either educator.

INTERDISCIPLINARY, COLLABORATIVE STUDENT WORK ANALYSIS
Necessary
Resources

Bring a representative sample of the most recent scored work (exit slip, quiz, test, etc.):
2 students who demonstrated mastery and above (earned an 80+% or a 3–4)
2 students who are approaching mastery (i.e., earned 60–79% or a 2)
2 students who are not yet approaching mastery (i.e., earned <60% or a 0–1)
1–2 students with disabilities
1–2 students who are emergent multilingual learners

Celebrating
Successes
3 minutes

Collaborating teachers reflect on ways in which their collaborative student work analysis
and planning has positively impacted their practice and their students’ outcomes. For
example:
• What is one way in which you are using student work that is making an impact on
your students’ learning, experiences and/or outcomes, specifically for students with
disabilities?
• What is one way you are currently using student work that has made an impact on
your teaching practice, particularly when supporting students with disabilities?

Articulating
the Context
for the Work
Sample
5 minutes

Collaborating teachers ground their analysis in a reminder of the instructional support that
was provided prior to the work sample or assessment. For example:
• What type of methods were used to introduce new material? For practice?
• What small group instruction was provided? Who participated? What was the focus?
• What are students’ accommodations (per their IEP or 504)? Were those
accommodations provided when students were producing this work? (SPED)
• What specially designed instruction was provided leading up to this work? (SPED)
• If relevant, what co-teaching models were used to support this content?
Teachers also ground themselves in the criteria for success for the work by:
• Reviewing the standard and stating, in their own words, what it demands of learners.
• Reviewing the rubric used to assess any written work.

Teachers review each representative set of students’ work and elevate what is seen in the
