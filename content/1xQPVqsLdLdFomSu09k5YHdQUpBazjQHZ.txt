Executive Summary
Repeated Reading: Building Oral Reading Fluency in Secondary Readers
Repeated reading is a high-leverage evidence-based literacy practice that directly builds oral
reading fluency—accuracy, rate, and prosody—skills that unlock access to grade-level content.
This executive summary distills key guidance from the Repeated Reading: Building Oral
Reading Fluency in Secondary Readers chapter into a streamlined reference tool for school and
system leaders. It provides a clear overview of the why, what, and how of implementing
repeated reading.

Why Repeated Reading is Important
Fluent reading frees up cognitive resources for comprehension, supporting students’ ability to
make meaning from complex texts. Students with disabilities are especially at risk for fluency
gaps, which contribute to barriers in accessing grade-level ELA standards and widen
achievement gaps.
Decades of research affirm that repeated reading is one of the most effective interventions for
increasing fluency, rate, and comprehension—even at the secondary level. Repeated reading
builds student confidence and fluency skills, and peer-to-peer models cultivate socially
supportive learning environments. Repeated reading also builds the capacity of educators to
address core literacy needs at secondary grade levels.

What Effective Repeated Reading Looks Like
High-quality implementation requires systematic protocols, motivational supports, and progress
monitoring. Without structure and fidelity, fluency practice risks being inconsistent or superficial.

Elements of effective repeated reading:
● Structured and Frequent: The practice is clear, routinized, and frequent — 3+ times
per week in short 5–10 minute sessions.
● Data-Driven: Pre-assessment informs the model, and progress monitoring is embedded
via cold/hot reads and data meetings.

● Motivating and Goal-Oriented:

How to Implement and Sustain Repeated Reading
Clear systems—from identifying students and selecting texts to coaching staff and tracking
progress—ease implementation and sustain the practice over time. Seven key actions are
described in the table below. Further explanation is provided Repeated Reading chapter:

Define Vision

1. Assess
Students

● Use universal screeners and diagnostics to identify students with
sufficient decoding but weak fluency (rate <120 CWPM, accuracy
<95%).
● Confirm comprehension needs and prioritize for intervention.

2. Design the
Model

● Define the model: peer-partner or teacher-led; whole class, small
group, or 1:1.
● Determine setting (e.g., co-taught, intervention, or pull-out) and
frequency (3x/week, 5–10 minutes).
● Weigh tradeoffs between peer and teacher-led models.

3. Build the
Infrastructure

● Select texts:choose 200–350-word grade-level texts from core content;
convert to Curriculum-Based Measurements (CBMs) with word counts.
● Prepare progress monitoring tools (timers, graphs, trackers).
● Establish routines and scheduling to protect intervention time.

4. Train Staff

● Provide training on the purpose, routines, and data collection.
● Model how to record errors, calculate rate and accuracy, and give
corrective feedback.
● Offer practice opportunities and coaching.

5. Roll Out &
Train Students

● Introduce the purpose and routine using growth-mindset framing
(“fluency makes reading easier and more enjoyable”).
● Model the process, teach feedback stems, and practice timing,
recording, and graphing with students.

6. Implement
the Practice

● Begin intervention sessions.
● Conduct cold and hot reads 3–5 times per passage, 3x/week.
● Track and graph student progress each session.
