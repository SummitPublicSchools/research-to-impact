Preface
In Anchoring Emotions: One school’s skill-based practices for supporting students with emotional-based
disabilities, we share a set of best practices for improving learning outcomes in secondary students —
particularly Black and Latinx students with disabilities experiencing poverty — through an emotional
support program for students in special education with emotional or behavioral disabilities or other
health impairments integrated within neighborhood schools. The Networked Improvement Community
for Students with Disabilities (NIC), a group of 10 public charter school networks serving more than
75,000 students nationwide, identified the best practices through their work between 2019 and 2023.
Led by Marshall Street at Summit Public Schools, in partnership with technical assistance providers
NIRN, RTI International, SRI International, and SWIFT Education Center, the purpose of the three-year
NIC was to systematically improve the way our education system serves Black and Latinx students
with disabilities experiencing poverty and share these solutions with school systems everywhere. This
report is funded by the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation. The findings and conclusions contained within
are those of the authors and do not necessarily reflect positions or policies of the Bill & Melinda Gates
Foundation.
The NIC used continuous improvement to translate existing research for improving learning outcomes
into best practices and to iterate the practices to ensure they worked in a variety of school contexts.

In Anchoring Emotions: One school’s skill-based practices for supporting
students with emotional-based disabilities, we share the results of this vital work:
Two best practices that Mastery Schools used as part of their supplemental behavioral
support model while in the NIC to successfully improve outcomes for students who need
emotional support. Each best practice includes:

school cultures of continuous improvement as well.

About Marshall Street
Marshall Street is a division of Summit Public Schools based in Redwood City, California. Marshall
draws from more than 15 years of institutional knowledge and extensive experience in school
improvement. Through our programs, we increase the capacity of our school partners to carry their
work forward in the ways most meaningful to their communities.
Continuous Improvement is a set of principles and practices to help educators “get better at getting
better.” Marshall’s Continuous Improvement team uses these tools to tackle intricate, systems-level
problems in K–12 education. From 2019 to 2023, Marshall led a multi-year Networked Improvement
Community (NIC) to make dramatic gains in the learning experiences, environments, and outcomes for
Black and Latinx students with disabilities experiencing poverty.

About the Anchor Emotional Support Program
at Mastery Schools
The Anchor program at Mastery Schools — a comprehensive therapeutic support program for students
receiving supplemental emotional support — is a unique, one-of-a-kind program that was developed to
disrupt national and local outcome disparities related to students with emotional disabilities. By joining
the NIC, the Anchor team set out to explore how they could create school-wide programmatic supports
to significantly increase student attendance rates and decrease suspension rates for students with
emotional and behavioral disabilities, ensuring this group of students had equitable access to their
education. This project engaged a diverse array of leaders, educators, and practitioners at Mastery
Schools and Simon Gratz High School who were instrumental to its success.

Introduction
The Challenge
America’s public schools struggle to meet the
needs of students with disabilities.
Public schools across America struggle to meet the diverse
needs of students, particularly those furthest from opportunity.
When marginalization intersects with disability, students often
face low expectations, are systematically segregated, are
denied access to college and career curricula, and do not
receive sufficient support to obtain a high school diploma and
pursue their dreams. In the 2017–18 school year, 8th graders
with disabilities performed 39–41 points lower than their peers without disabilities in reading
and math,1 illustrating just how deeply schools are failing students with disabilities.

Students of color are over-identified as needing emotional support.
In the last few decades, there has been a growing concern regarding the disproportionate number
of students experiencing poverty, particularly students of color, found eligible for special education
services.2 Data continues to show that students of color and those living in poverty are also
disproportionately identified as having emotional disabilities or other health impairments.3 These same
students are also more likely to be placed in educational settings that are more restrictive learning
environments. Students who are inappropriately placed in more restrictive environments may not
receive the level of rigor or expectations academically or behaviorally that they would when educated
along with their peers without disabilities, putting them at greater risk of negative outcomes.

Suspension and expulsion are more likely for students with
emotional support needs.
Researchers have also found that students with emotional and behavioral disabilities and other health
impairments are also far more likely to be suspended or expelled than their peers with and without

disabilities.4 This is particularly striking because it may be ineffective to use suspension as a form
of punishment for students with behavioral or emotional disabilities5 or students with aggressive,
hyperactive, and social skills concerns.6 Students with these disabilities often also have academic

Exclusionary discipline contributes to a school-to-prison pipeline.
Research also shows the way in which suspension of students increases their likelihood of entering

are warranted because there is a clear overrepresentation of students with disabilities in the juvenile
justice system compared to their peers without disabilities.9

Mastery Schools’ Solution
Mastery Schools developed a program to address these important realities for their students in special
education with emotional support needs and to help improve their future outcomes. Mastery’s program
focuses on using interventions and best practices rooted in research, such as positive behavioral
support, differentiated instruction, developmental therapy, and life space crisis intervention, for
addressing challenging behaviors. The program’s intent is to help students in special education receive
learning within Mastery’s network and to provide them the opportunity to access the general education
curriculum in the least restrictive environment possible. By addressing problem behaviors and educating
students in the most rigorous academic setting possible, Mastery is able to reduce the likelihood that
their students with emotional support needs will require more restrictive levels of support.

Itinerant Emotional Support
Students who receive itinerant emotional support are educated within the general education setting
between 80% and 99% of the day. The student appropriate for this tier of support has a diagnosed
school-based emotional or behavioral disability that requires mild therapeutic and behavioral
interventions to meet academic and behavioral goals. The core components of the itinerant emotional
supports include a functional behavior assessment and a positive behavior support plan, as well as
aligned behavioral interventions and case management.
FUNCTIONAL BEHAVIOR ASSESSMENTS AND POSITIVE BEHAVIOR SUPPORT PLANS
A trained individual assesses each student’s unique behavioral and emotional needs to determine the
most prevalent negative behaviors the student displays, to establish the function of those behaviors,
and to identify an aligned replacement behavior. Within the positive behavior support plan, the IEP
team decides how the student will receive interventions to learn the discrete skills and replacement
behaviors and what reinforcement will help the student generalize new skills across all settings.
WEEKLY 1:1 COUNSELING AND/OR SOCIAL SKILLS IN A SMALL GROUP
Students gain awareness of their negative behaviors and practice appropriate replacement behaviors
through counseling and discrete skill instruction. Mastery meets each student’s needs by employing
social workers for each school at a 300:1 ratio and using an evidence-based counseling model or a
research-based, CASEL standards-aligned social-emotional learning curriculum. Social workers are
also responsible for many of Mastery’s multi-tiered system of supports’ Tier 2 interventions, as well as
crisis response and targeted social work case management.
CASE MANAGEMENT
Each student receiving itinerant emotional support is assigned a special education case manager
whose role includes managing the positive behavior support plan across all settings, ensuring that
interventions are in place, and providing more intensive reinforcements than are provided as part of
Tier 1 systems. Additionally, case managers are responsible for evaluating the effectiveness of plans,
sharing progress with families, and supporting the IEP team in determining if the plan and associated
interventions should be reduced, maintained, or intensified.

Full-Time Emotional Support
Students receiving full-time emotional support are not educated within general education. Students with
this level of need require significant therapeutic and behavioral interventions to meet their academic
and behavioral goals. Mastery contracts with a variety of providers for full-time emotional support,
including a private school that aligns with Mastery’s suite of curriculum, instruction, and assessments.
Students at this level access a curriculum that is modified to provide a higher level of daily therapeutic
programming. The benefit of this private school partnership is that students can return to a less
restrictive environment with less interruption to learning.

Approved Private School
Some students do not respond to placement with the full-time emotional support providers and
subsequently are placed by the IEP team in approved private schools that offer more intensive
therapeutic environments.

Elements of Anchor’s core components were initially developed in partnership with Greentree School,
an approved private school providing full-time emotional support programming in a private setting.
Greentree developed their model based on the Student Achievement Model (SAM), the Boys Town
Education Model, and Life Space Crisis Intervention. Core components of the Anchor model are
described in depth below and in the collection’s chapters.

Schedule & Staffing
To ensure appropriate, proactive, and responsive adult support is available for students based on
need, an increased staff-to-student ratio exists in Anchor compared to similar programs. For every 12
students within three consecutive grade bands, Anchor has:
• 1 full-time emotional support teacher who is also a case manager,
• .5 clinician (20 or 29 hours per week), and
• 2 classroom counselors.
This staffing ratio makes it possible for students to spend between 20% and 60% of their day receiving
emotional support in either a general education or a special education environment. Students’ levels of
support are determined by their current performance. Staff schedules regularly adapt to meet changing
student needs.

Behavioral feedback is the intervention used to reinforce and generalize students’ use of SAM skills
across all settings. Behavioral feedback is a form of behavioral teaching, which, to be effective, requires
timely and specific delivery of feedback targeting the correct behaviors. “Positive Praise” scripts are
based on research to support increasing student motivation, and “Learning Point” scripts provide a
systematic method to address inappropriate behavior and teach replacement skills.

Daily Skills Class
Daily Skills Class is the time and space where students in Anchor are provided explicit instruction and
practice opportunities on discrete skills to promote the learning and generalization of skills across all
settings. Daily Skills Class is required five days per week for a minimum of 25 minutes per day. The
course is taught by a special education teacher or clinician and uses evidence-based curricula that
have shown success as Tier 3 interventions.

Enabling Conditions
Each of the aforementioned Anchor components contributed to improved experiences and behavioral
outcomes for Black and Latinx students with disabilities experiencing poverty within the NIC. The
effective implementation of these practices was made possible because of three core enabling
conditions that were in place in Mastery Schools’ emotional support programming: a program designed
for each individual student’s needs, effective data systems, and network-level support. Without these
three conditions, the impact of the practices is dramatically reduced.

Program Designed for Students
Mastery developed Anchor as a supplemental emotional support program that individualizes its services
to meet each student's needs rather than expecting students to fit within the program as designed.
Mastery's full range of emotional and behavioral support programs, and the Anchor program within it,
embodies the true intention of providing a full continuum of services for students. Anchor is successful
because it attempts only to support students who need the level of support that the program was
designed for. Students requiring less support are served with itinerant services, and those benefiting
from more support are served in Mastery’s specialized school setting. Anchor itself was designed to be
flexible, adapting to each student’s individual needs as they progress in the level system. By analyzing
student data every two weeks — as opposed to waiting for a school suspension or manifestation of
determination like many emotional support programs — Anchor staff ensure that students always
receive a level of support that corresponds to their current needs.
Mastery wanted to ensure that the mindsets held by Anchor staff were shared by all school staff as they
interact with students with emotional and behavioral disabilities, so they created their Modified Anchor
Response Plan (MARP) as an adaptation to their existing culture plan. All students at Mastery are held
to behavioral expectations, but for students in the Anchor program, school staff might need to respond
differently to behaviors, which MARP helps define. To help all staff understand MARP and feel confident

Effective Data Systems
Many of Anchor’s systems rely on constant data collection from school administrators, special education
teachers, classroom counselors, and other school and network staff. Mastery has been able to include
the data necessary for Anchor’s success in their existing school and network-wide student information
systems. When adding Anchor-specific data to these systems, a concerted, network-wide data
integration effort ensured that existing data and new, Anchor-specific data could work together.
To successfully align the existing student information systems to Anchor program data, Mastery Schools
spent considerable time and effort developing a network culture that understands emotional and
behavioral disabilities. A key way in which this was accomplished was by making it possible for network
staff to analyze the network’s key performance indicators for the subset of students in Anchor. This
analysis not only allows the network to better respond to trends related to students’ emotional support
needs, but it also allows network and building leaders to focus on academic growth and proficiency for
these students. In addition, Mastery has been able to build a system that allows Anchor staff to collect
granular, real-time data around behavioral feedback. All stakeholders, from network leaders to students
and families, can view data in real time to see how students are demonstrating their prosocial behaviors.
These additions to Mastery’s data infrastructure have been invaluable to the continued success
of emotional support programming, making it possible for network staff to analyze Anchor data by
aggregating the data from multiple schools’ Anchor programs to determine areas of strength and
opportunities for growth within the Anchor program as a whole. They are also able to compare the
different schools’ Anchor programs to learn from programs that are seeing positive outcomes for their
students and to more intentionally support programs that have additional needs. Each school’s Anchor
program can similarly analyze data to identify needs within the different areas of their program or to
respond appropriately for individual students. This type of data support makes it possible for Anchor staff
to tailor their program to students' needs and ensure that each student is receiving what they need to
make progress academically as well as emotionally.

Network Support
Mastery Schools, as a network, has invested significant time and resources into building their full
continuum of emotional support programs to meet the diverse needs of students with emotional and
behavioral disabilities. The Anchor program has a team of three network-level staff members who
oversee and support each school’s Anchor program. These team members have successful schoolbased experience working with students with emotional and behavioral disabilities that lends them
expertise and credibility. The network team members provide direct coaching and support to each
school’s Anchor team, provide professional development throughout the school year, document the
core program components, develop implementation guides, and continue to research and modify the

Mastery’s Role in the NIC
The partnership between Mastery Schools and the NIC stemmed from a shared commitment to tackling
systemic inequities in the US education system, particularly for students with emotional and behavioral
disabilities. Mastery joined the NIC to refine and improve their Anchor program model in a way that
allowed them to spread it across their organization and, ultimately, to share it with schools nationwide.
The program was already well-established and showing promising gains for students at Mastery Gratz.
Yet, Mastery and the NIC recognized the program’s potential to impact the broader field of education by
sharing it with schools nationwide.
Participation in the NIC provided Mastery with dedicated improvement coaching, continuous
improvement tools, and data support to refine and improve their model; access to a community of
experts and practitioners with whom to learn and collaborate; and resources to help codify the Anchor
program to share and spread it broadly with schools nationwide. Engaging with the NIC spurred the
evolution of Mastery’s practices, which led to the piloting and adoption of initiatives such as behavioral
feedback and peer-to-peer mentorship. During this time, Mastery addressed barriers like staff capacity,
mindsets, and training, improving staff awareness of how best to serve students with emotional and
behavioral disabilities across the school. With the benefit of coaching, data assistance, and peer
networking, Mastery refined its model, increasing student access to general education settings,
boosting student attendance, and reducing suspensions, while also gearing up to expand the program’s
impact beyond its current schools.

behavioral disabilities on behalf of improving learning outcomes for all students and two case studies
on larger macro-level practices to support a full continuum of services for students with emotional or

Each of the practices detailed in this book is an independently strong and valuable practice. However,
each practice is most effective when built on a programmatic foundation that provides opportunities for
academic learning and disability-related skill development.

The audience for this collection is educators, schools, and districts who believe that all students are
capable of increasing their academic achievement, who fundamentally believe that it is the system’s
responsibility to ensure this is a reality for all students it serves, and who are looking for ideas on
how to initiate or refine their approach to this work. While a great many research papers, reports, and
technical assistance centers provide guidance on practices for students with emotional and behavioral
disabilities, this collection is unique:
• It ties together the multiple elements critical to addressing the needs of all students, specifically for
Black and Latinx students with disabilities experiencing poverty.
• It emphasizes the importance of using data at all levels of the school system to improve outcomes
for students.
• Each chapter is grounded in both the foundational and current research on the practice but
emphasizes (1) detailed, real-world examples of the practice as implemented at Mastery Schools
and (2) a step-by-step guide to implementation of the practice.

Chapter 1: Building Student Leaders: Peer-to-Peer Mentorship
for Secondary Students
Student mentorship is a peer support structure pairing two students together in a mentee/mentor
relationship for regular formal and informal check-ins to build positive peer relationships and increase
student sense of belonging. Mentees in Mastery’s peer mentorship program were able to develop
relationships with older students whom they could look to for guidance and rely on for additional
emotional support. This chapter offers the learning from Mastery’s implementation and the steps to
implement a peer mentorship program in any school context.

Chapter 2: Providing Timely Behavioral Feedback Across Classrooms
Educators follow a defined protocol to provide immediate feedback to students on their application
of taught behavioral skills, enabling them to effectively apply these skills in various contexts. To
successfully implement behavioral feedback, program leaders must also devote time to the training,
observation, and coaching of staff so that feedback is provided consistently and with fidelity. This
chapter is a guide to setting up the necessary conditions and implementing behavioral feedback.
When implemented with quality, behavioral feedback can have significant positive impacts on student
skill development and overall behavior and, subsequently, on academic learning.

Case Study: Maximizing Student Progress Through Data-Informed
Systems
This case study describes Mastery’s goal-setting, collection, analysis, decision-making, and reflection
of and based on data at essential programmatic points to ensure fidelity, quality, and outcomes at
all levels of the program. Mastery’s investment in data systems that support data specific to the
Anchor program ensures that the needs of students with emotional and behavioral disabilities are
not overlooked by school or network staff. The case study highlights the importance of creating
clear systems and structures that make it feasible for staff and students alike to make data-informed
decisions.

Case Study: Refining Program Implementation Via Quarterly
Collaborative Evaluation at Mastery Schools
This case study explains how Mastery’s network leaders use rubrics to evaluate and design
programmatic support for each school’s Anchor program on a quarterly basis to create accountability
and continuous improvement cycles for programs. Network- and school-level Anchor leaders

Practice Overview
What is the Practice?
Student mentorship is a peer support structure that pairs two students in a mentee/mentor relationship
for regular formal and informal check-ins to build positive peer relationships and increase students’
sense of belonging.

Why is Peer-to-Peer Mentorship Important?
Developing strong relationships with peers and staff at school is important because it can impact
students’ sense of belonging and connection to their community, factors essential to students’ success
for persistence to and through K–12 schooling.1 Strong relationships are especially important for
students with disabilities who are increasingly included in educational and community settings, yet
may not always feel a sense of belonging and inclusion socially and emotionally with peers. Students
with emotional and behavioral disabilities may particularly struggle to connect and relate to their peers.
This struggle may be related to their disability, the way peers react to their disability, and sometimes a
combination of related factors. Peer-to-peer mentorship provides opportunities for students to develop
social bonds, create community, and learn new ways of relating to other students.

Why it Works
TEACHING AND MODELING DEVELOPMENT OF SOCIAL SKILLS
Students participating in peer-to-peer mentorship have the opportunity to work on important prosocial
skills that many students need modeled and reinforced as they develop relationships with peers.
Peer mentorship focuses on building prosocial skills that all students need to learn in school and that
specialized programs for students with emotional and behavioral disabilities work to teach, such as:
• offering to help someone,
• considering the feelings and needs of others,
• sharing and kindness,
• volunteering time,
• self-efficacy, and
• self-awareness.
These skills can be taught through explicit instruction in the classroom, but it is essential that students
have opportunities to practice skills in a supportive environment that provides feedback and allows
for them to make mistakes. Implementing skills with supportive peers can help students, particularly
students with emotional and behavioral disabilities, generalize their learning to their interactions with
other students and enhance their application of prosocial skills in other contexts.
STUDENTS BUILD CONNECTIONS AND COMMUNITY AT SCHOOL
As students build these interpersonal skills in their peer-to-peer mentorship program, they also gain a
sense of belonging with students who were not part of their typical social groups at school.2 Developing
these relationships with other students with disabilities enables students to relate to peers with similar
experiences and can also help them gain a positive identity and pride related to their disability.3 The
feeling of understanding from peers and the sense of belonging within a group of students create
important connections between students and their school, helping them feel more engaged, respond
more positively to authority, and improve academic achievement.4
MENTORS GAIN CONFIDENCE AND LEADERSHIP
The positive messages students receive about their disability from peer mentorship can help combat
negative and exclusionary social messages students may have previously internalized.5 Mentors also
have the opportunity to build new leadership skills as they learn how to coach and support their peers
with a similar disability.6 As mentors see the positive effects of their leadership, their confidence grows,
helping them in and out of the classroom. This creates a virtuous cycle, in which mentors’ growing
confidence helps them to further build up and support their mentees.

Bringing this Practice to Life
FROM PILOT TO PROGRESS: THE SUCCESS OF PEER MENTORING
AT MASTERY SCHOOLS
Mastery Schools’ Simon Gratz High School in Philadelphia, PA, began piloting a peer-to-peer mentorship
program in the 2021–22 school year to cultivate a sense of belonging among students in their school
community. The program paired older students from their Anchor emotional support program with younger
students, aiming to foster authentic relationships between them. Each week, mentors and mentees meet
formally and informally to nurture their relationship and collaboratively address any barriers to learning
experienced by the mentees. Over the course of two years, the Gratz team has expanded and refined the
program based on insights gained from the pilot, enabling them to involve more students and better address
student needs through this initiative.

Starting Small and Seeing Big Problems
Before the COVID-19 pandemic, Gratz was separated into two campuses. Vernetta Newman, one
of Gratz’s emotional support counselors, ran a meet-and-greet day bringing together the upper- and
lower-level students in the Anchor program. The meet-and-greet day was a time for celebration,
socializing, playing games, and presentations by upperclassmen about the Anchor program on the
upper campus. Vernetta and the Anchor staff found that these one-day events were insufficient to
create a sense of belonging and community connection for their students. This need for community
was particularly acute for students in Anchor who came from alternative placements, and others
who experienced so many suspensions that they did not feel connected to school or their peers.

The team also reflected that students in Anchor were not attending school often enough and
thought giving students a non-academic reason for coming to school might help. The team needed
to find a strategy to help their students make connections at Gratz that would help them feel
connected and want to come to school more regularly.

Getting Peer-to-Peer Mentorship Started
Once students returned to campus for in-person learning during the 2021–22 school year, Vernetta
began building Anchor’s peer-to-peer mentorship program as a way to connect older students in the
program with younger ones. Vernetta recruited outgoing, assertive students with strong attendance
records and demonstrated success within the program to serve as mentors. In the first term, three
students became mentors, each with one mentee. In order to pair mentees with their mentors,
Vernetta spoke with Anchor staff and, in some cases, staff at the middle schools attended by the
students, to deeply understand students’ personalities and needs. Unfortunately, Gratz continued to
experience temporary school closures due to COVID-19, and the mentorship pairs could only meet
regularly for about a month. However, during this time, Vernetta was able to learn important lessons
about matching students, logistical needs, and structures for formal mentorship sessions that she
could apply to the next school year.

Mentorship as a Way to Build Connection
For the 2022–23 school year, Vernetta took a different approach to identifying mentors for the
program. She realized that less outgoing students could learn the skills for good mentoring —
like how to communicate with new people — and that the program itself could teach those skills.
Vernetta developed a mentor training curriculum and guidelines with explicit expectations for
mentors. Twelve students engaged in peer-to-peer mentorship in the second year, and formal
sessions were able to happen more regularly from the start of the school year. Along with formal
sessions, Vernetta worked to build informal texting into mentor-mentee relationships to create less
structured interactions between students. Reworked meeting protocols and solutions for logistical
barriers meant that mentors and mentees were able to benefit considerably in the second year of
the mentorship program.
By the end of this second year, the peer mentorship program was running more consistently. In
end-of-year surveys, students expressed its value in helping them build connections:
• “I’ve learned how to control my emotions.”
• “I can build a relationship with other people. Let’s say I’ve been through a struggle, and they
are going through it now; I can tell them how to get out of it cause I’ve been there, done that.
Like having a problem with a teacher. I told [my mentee] to sit there, do the work, and follow
the teacher’s directions. You’ll be out of that class in no time.”
• “I was mad one day, and [my mentor] talked to me, ‘Do you need anything? Do you need
space to calm down?’ Then we just started talking about what happened.”
• “I always used to get in trouble, and he told me how to act over there, that fighting isn’t worth
it. Ain’t gonna get you nowhere.”

Learning to Improve
As Vernetta became more skilled at mitigating some of the barriers that hindered students from
attending all their formal meetings, students voiced the value they saw in peer mentorship and
expressed a desire for more mentoring sessions.
First, through some trial and error, she worked out how to ensure that mentoring sessions were held
when students could all attend. This meant making sure that sessions were not during classes that
students could not or did not want to miss. Finding a schedule that worked helped increase student
attendance at sessions. Second, to increase attendance, Vernetta created permanent laminated
passes for students to keep for the day and time peer-to-peer mentorship would be held. This way,
students did not have to request permission from teachers for a pass each time, and access to
passes was not a barrier for mentor pairs. In the end, Vernetta also realized she needed a backup
plan for mentors or mentees who ended up without their partners due to an absence.
Students reported that they valued their connections and interactions with peers that resulted from
mentorship even though pairs were not always perfect. Vernetta started to develop a rubric for
determining which students could make good mentors. She realized that some of the mentees in
the program could eventually transition to become mentors instead because of how their behavior
had progressed in the Anchor program. After the first year of implementing explicit mentor training,
Vernetta had insight into the amount and types of training and support mentors needed to build and
maintain relationships with their peers.
Whether they felt they were gaining communication, self-regulation, or leadership skills, Gratz
students enjoyed and benefited from the Anchor program’s peer-to-peer mentorship. From
2019 to 2023, during which time Gratz implemented peer-to-peer mentorship, the percentage
of Anchor students attending school at least 90% of the time increased by 26 points.

Mastery Gratz significantly reduced the attendance gap between student groups between 2019 and 2023.

How to Implement this Practice
This section provides step-by-step guidance for implementing a peer-to-peer mentorship program,
along with accompanying resources to get started.

Prepare Needed Structures
Step 1: Identify a Peer Mentorship Lead
Identify a staff member who can lead the mentorship program. School leaders can support this
program lead by giving them the authority to make decisions in the best interest of the peer mentorship
program (e.g., giving students permanent hall passes or creating a schedule for formal mentorship
sessions). The peer mentorship lead will also be more effective when given a clear set of roles and
responsibilities for their role that empower them to oversee the effort. Some of the tasks they will be
responsible for include:
• coordinating program schedules,
• creating student pairings,
• raising awareness and promoting the program among teachers,
• relaying expectations to mentors and mentees,
• monitoring formal mentorship sessions, and
• managing ongoing communication with families and students.
Because this role requires a considerable amount of time, it is
important to consider other staff responsibilities when assigning the
role. If existing responsibilities cannot be reassigned, consider whether
a stipend can offset the additional tasks for the program leader.
Mastery Schools provides a $2,000 stipend for their mentorship lead.
It is helpful to choose a program lead who deeply understands
students’ backgrounds and needs so that they can properly match
mentors and mentees, address any emotional or behavioral needs
during sessions, and communicate easily with students and families.
A staff member who does not know students well will likely need to
spend additional time talking to other staff members to gain insight into
potential mentors and mentees.

Step 2: Design Tools, Protocols, and Norms
In order to plan and implement a successful mentorship program, it is important to create specific
materials and resources that give mentors and mentees an understanding of the structure and
expectations for their participation, such as the following:
• Mentorship agreement form to set expectations for participation in the program
• Mentorship training materials (e.g., presentation, worksheet, readings) to help students
understand the purpose of peer-to-peer mentorship, their role, and how it can benefit them
• Mentorship protocol that gives students a structure to follow during their sessions to ensure they
are communicating and collaborating with their mentor or mentee
• Attendance logs to track attendance at mentorship sessions to identify which students need help
mitigating barriers
Establishing and communicating norms for the program’s operations can build buy-in among school
staff and minimize conflicts related to students missing class for mentorship sessions and submitting
make-up work. Consider a regular communication strategy to remind teachers about sessions and
obtain information about students’ make-up assignments. With teachers’ active support for the program,
some absences from mentorship sessions can be avoided.
Because other absences are unavoidable, have concrete plans in place for mentor or mentee
absences from formal mentorship sessions. Such plans might include alternative activities students do
independently or an alternative meeting protocol for temporary pairings of students who are not in an
official mentorship relationship.

Step 3: Recruit Students
The ideal recruitment strategy will depend upon the size of the population from which mentors and
mentees will be identified. If students come from a large set of over 100 students or even an entire
school, it can be helpful to have teachers make announcements, make school-wide announcements,
and put posters up around school. For smaller populations, such as those within one program or
classroom, it is best to use existing adult and student relationships to talk directly to students about the
opportunity to participate in the peer-to-peer mentorship program. This strategy can be effective when
recruiting students for a larger mentorship program as well. Encourage staff members to consider all
students as potential mentors who could benefit from developing self-confidence and leadership skills,
not just those who may already demonstrate those abilities.
Consider offering incentives to students to recruit their participation and retain them in the program
long-term. Effective incentives may differ for mentors and mentees. For example, in the first year of
their mentorship program, Gratz gave mentors a $25 gift card each quarter if they regularly participated
in mentorship (i.e., they attended 75% of mentorship sessions) but chose not to give incentives
to mentees. Mentors could earn a bonus $25 gift card if they met certain criteria (see mentorship
competencies in Step 7). The Gratz team found that students enjoyed the program enough that these
incentives were unnecessary going forward.

Step 4: Get Permission from Families
Engaging with families from the start of the program can be helpful in building buy-in and ensuring
they can support the program implementation, particularly to encourage their students’ attendance at
sessions. At the start of any peer mentorship program, schools should provide detailed information
about the program to families, including its goals, structure, activities, and expected outcomes.
This information can be shared through written materials, presentations, or virtual meetings. This
communication also allows staff to highlight the potential benefits of participating in the program for
both mentors and mentees and address any concerns about how the program will be implemented
and monitored. Mastery Gratz required all mentees and mentors in their program to have parents or
caregivers sign a consent form in order to participate. Once the program begins, regular communication
with families can provide updates on activities, progress, and any changes to the program as needed.

Step 5: Create Student Pairs
Creating cohesive student pairings is an essential
part of having a successful mentorship program.
Consider the factors that most often connect
students at school, such as:
• neighborhood,
• cultural factors (e.g., religion, ethnicity,
traditions)
• interests (e.g., video games, the military, hiking),
• TV shows,
• music,
• behaviors exhibited, and
• student schedules (e.g., out-of-school
responsibilities, hobbies).

Using Data to Pair
Mentors and Mentees
Mastery Gratz initially used student
attendance and tardy data to pair
students so that mentors and mentees
would not be alone during sessions. For
the second year of implementing peerto-peer mentorship, Vernetta met with
Anchor staff to gain more anecdotal and
qualitative data to better understand
which mentors and mentees would be
best matched. They found this strategy to
be more effective for matching students
than solely relying on quantitative data.

When creating peer mentor pairs, it can be helpful
to survey participating students to gather specific
information about them to guide their pairing.
An alternative strategy could be to allow students to meet in an informal setting and observe which
students naturally gravitate toward one another. A more structured version of this approach would
involve a “speed dating” scenario where mentees engage in five-minute conversations with each
mentor, aided by prompts, to determine which pairs interact most effectively together.
Have a plan for how to respond if pairings are not working and need to be changed. Pairings may not
work for a variety of reasons; it is a good exercise for both students in an unsuccessful pairing to meet
together with the mentorship lead to discuss why the pairing is not working. In situations where there is
a sensitive personal issue, one-on-one conversations with the mentorship lead can also be very helpful.
Empower the students by asking what characteristics they now know they need in a mentor or mentee,
as well as whether there is another student they think may be a better match. It is not recommended
to change another successful pairing to rematch students. Instead, look for a student not currently
engaged in peer mentorship to create new pairings.

Step 6: Create a Mentorship Schedule
Schedule times for formal mentorship sessions that are regular and consistent. Use student schedules
to help determine the best day and time for these sessions so they are least likely to impact high-needs
academic courses (i.e., English and mathematics). It will be important to communicate this schedule
with teachers, particularly general educators, who will need to excuse students from class to attend
mentorship sessions.
Consider what time of the day mentorship sessions will be least disruptive to student learning while
also encouraging students to attend school. Gratz strategically chose to have the formal mentorship
sessions during the last period of the day to support students’ timely arrival and consistent attendance
at sessions. Holding sessions at the end of the day also prevents students from struggling to reregulate for a return to an academic setting after the relative downtime allowed by the mentorship
check-in (e.g., eating a snack together or playing a game).

Put Peer Mentorship into Practice
Step 7: Train Student Mentors
In order to act as an effective mentor to peers, students acting as mentors will need training to help
them build competencies that will allow them to facilitate conversations with their mentees. A helpful
first step before designing mentor training is to know what competencies students should have when
they finish the training. The specific competencies will depend on the mentorship program’s purpose
and goal. Competencies might include:
• leading a conversation with a peer,
• filling out protocols for sessions,
• making eye contact,
• requesting adult assistance,
• using technology for effective informal communication,
• creating SMART goals, or
• asking probing questions.
Plan to conduct mentor training before the first peer-to-peer mentoring session so mentors are ready
when formal mentoring begins. Some mentors might need continuing training or support after the
initial session(s). Consider having mentors identify areas of growth they hope to work on. Use these
areas to coach them throughout the semester or year. This type of self-reflection and goal setting
for mentors can help them build important leadership skills that will help them as mentors as well as
outside of the program.

Step 8: Hold Pre-meetings
Every mentee should have a pre-meeting with their mentor and a staff member before mentoring
begins. Use this pre-meeting to make sure students know that participation is voluntary, relay clear
expectations for the program, have students sign their mentorship agreement form, and set up a
communication plan. Encourage students to exchange contact information, and remind the mentor
to reach out to their mentee before the first formal session. Starting the informal communication
between mentor and mentee during pre-meetings can be very helpful to the success of mentorship. For
example, using informal communication (e.g., text messaging), mentors can make sure mentees are at
school for formal mentorship sessions in the future. This can immediately lessen potential attendance
issues during formal sessions.

Step 9: Hold Mentorship Sessions
Peer-to-peer mentoring can happen through formal and informal interactions. Both types of interactions
are important for the mentor and mentee to bond, transfer skills, and create routines.
FORMAL SESSIONS
Students can be required to meet for formal sessions on a weekly or biweekly basis, depending on
the schedule devised. Set an expectation that students will all arrive on time to maximize the session.
Formal sessions can be as short as 15 minutes or as long as a class period, depending on the protocol
developed and the ability of the mentor to facilitate the protocol. Initially, mentors may require more time
to navigate the protocol during the first sessions. Regular, informal interactions can also reduce the
need for lengthy formal sessions.
Make sure students have comfortable seating in private, non-classroom spaces to meet with one
another. Mentoring spaces should allow conversations to be confidential. Consider having snacks
available for students to help them feel more at ease while talking.
INFORMAL SESSIONS
Informal interactions between mentors and mentees
can be used to help mentees move forward with any
goals or action items that come out of formal check-ins.
Ideally, mentors are informally communicating with their
mentees at least once a week through text, in-person
check-ins, or emails.
It is helpful to have a document or structure for mentors
to communicate to the mentorship lead that an informal
check-in happened. The mentorship lead can then
easily follow up with mentors, remind them to check in
on their mentees, and address any barriers they are
having with informal check-ins.

Summary
Mentorship can be a powerful way for students with common backgrounds and experiences
to support one another. Peer-to-peer mentorship programs that pair students who have similar
disabilities give participants additional common ground. This type of program empowers older
students to be leaders to younger students, to help teach skills, and to provide support as
someone who understands the mentees’ disabilities. Younger students have the opportunity to
connect to older students whom they can look up to and use as part of their support system,
increasing their sense of belonging at school. Participating in mentorship provides another
chance for both mentor and mentee to gain new social, academic, and leadership skills that
will help them navigate personal and professional relationships in the future. Mastery’s peerto-peer mentorship program was correlated with a substantial increase in the number of
students attending school at least 90% of the time. Their success shows the potential for using
mentorship as an effective intervention for improving attendance for students with disabilities,
especially students with emotional and behavioral disabilities.

Practice Overview
What is the Practice?
An intervention in which educators follow a
defined protocol to provide immediate feedback
to students on their application of taught
prosocial behavioral skills, enabling them to
effectively apply these skills in various contexts.

3.3

What is a Prosocial
Behavioral Skill?
A prosocial behavioral skill is a chain of
individual, discrete actions or cognitive
operations performed in specific situations
that demonstrate helping, sharing,
or caring for others.1 The teaching of
prosocial skills must be explicit and
comprehensive, and it relies on control
of the environment, reinforcement, and
generalization of skills across all settings.

Why is Behavioral Feedback Important?
When school teams provide direct instruction on the prosocial behavioral skills students need,
partner that instruction with discrete practice, and offer generalized feedback on those skills across
settings, schools can reduce the number of disciplinary consequences students experience. That
reduction can have a significant impact on students’ short- and long-term outcomes. Students
with emotional and behavioral disabilities receive, on average, seven disciplinary consequences
(suspension or expulsion) per year. Over 60% of students with emotional or behavioral disabilities
receive at least one disciplinary consequence per year. And almost three quarters (73%) of students
with emotional or behavioral disabilities are suspended or expelled at least once during their school
careers.2 Disciplinary consequences can increase negative stereotypes about students with emotional
and behavioral disabilities and reduce their opportunities for learning by removing them from their
classroom settings. Furthermore, it is unlikely that these types of consequences help change
behavioral outcomes for students with emotional or behavioral disabilities.3 Instead, exclusionary
practices likely contribute to the school-to-prison pipeline — that is, the way in which disciplinary
systems in K–12 schools increase the likelihood that students will enter the criminal justice system.4

Why it Works
INTERVENTIONS IN INCLUSIVE SETTINGS HELP GENERALIZE LEARNING.
Direct instruction on prosocial skills and how to generalize behavioral strategies, as well as feedback
on the use of strategies, can be more effective when students learn as often as possible in inclusive
settings, where they can encounter authentic opportunities to apply the prosocial behaviors they have
been taught. When students spend more time in inclusive settings, they interact with a more diverse
set of students. As they encounter a wider range of behaviors from these peers, they also learn and
have the opportunity to apply new strategies for engaging with these diverse behaviors.5 In addition,
by receiving behavioral feedback in an inclusive setting, students with emotional and behavioral
disabilities can experience education that is academically rigorous while also improving behaviors
long-term.
REGULAR FEEDBACK POSITIVELY REINFORCES PROSOCIAL BEHAVIORS.
Positive reinforcements (e.g., Positive Points, Kickboard Points, Dojo Points, WOW Points!) and realtime feedback (e.g., “Nice job walking into class on time,” “I appreciate you being in your seat”) for
even small steps of a behavior are effective interventions when working with students with emotional
and behavioral disabilities.6 Recognizing small gains helps maintain students’ motivation to continue
to improve toward the target behavior. Behavioral feedback has also been found to increase student
engagement in learning, while decreasing the number of times teachers attempt to correct behaviors.7
As these students spend more time focused on learning, and their teachers spend less time
addressing behavior, all students benefit from additional learning opportunities.

STAFF TRAINING LEADS TO BETTER FEEDBACK.
When schools implement deliberate staff training on how to deliver high-quality, timely, and targeted
behavioral feedback, staff are better equipped to respond to students in ways that reinforce prosocial
behaviors. With regular, high-quality feedback over time, students can develop skills to address areas
of need that result from their disability and can better use those skills in a variety of environments.
Increasing prosocial behaviors through equitably implemented feedback can lead to fewer of the
disciplinary consequences that can put students with disabilities into the school-to-prison pipeline.
Behavioral feedback instead increases the amount of time students spend in the classroom to learn
academic skills for future success.

How to Implement this Practice
This section provides step-by-step guidance for implementation of a behavioral feedback program
specific to a school and community context, along with accompanying resources to get started.

Lay the Groundwork
Step 1: Train Program Staff
Program staff will benefit from training on the following:
• The skills that they need to teach students (e.g., SAM skills, Collaborative Problem Solving)
• How to recognize opportunities for students to use their skills in different types of classroom
settings (e.g., specialized program classes, interventions classes, core general education classes,
electives)
• How to provide learning and positive feedback with the use of a script
• How to enter data into a shared system
• How to use data with students
When training staff in the practice of behavioral feedback, it is essential to identify priority behavioral
skills for the start of the year and focus training on how staff can provide targeted feedback to students
on those skills. Staff training also needs to cover the skills necessary for staff to implement the program.
For example, staff may need to know how to enter data into data systems as soon as school begins but
can learn how to use data with students later in the school year. Throughout the year, training can build

the program staff who implement the behavioral feedback practice.

Step 6: Staff Follow Rounds Schedule to Observe
and Support Students
Program staff follow the predetermined schedule to join students in their classrooms throughout the
day. Staff enter classrooms to observe specific students during parts of the school day when the
students are likely to have opportunities to demonstrate the skills they are working on. If necessary,
staff can also create opportunities for students to demonstrate skills if they do not naturally happen. For
example, staff can ask a student to work actively on an assignment until finished or to get the teacher’s
attention appropriately if they have a question. When the student correctly demonstrates their skill (e.g.,
remaining on task), behavioral feedback using positive praise language helps reinforce the skill. The
staff member gives this praise using predefined steps and an example script, such as the following:
Steps

Example Script

1. Descriptive praise

John, you are remaining on task by sitting in your seat, having still
hands, and working quietly on your workbook pages.

2. Reason

This is important so you are not disrupting others and you are able
to focus and complete your work.

3. Request acknowledgement

Does this make sense?

4. Positive consequence/reinforcer

You earned 2 points for treating others with respect.

If the student has the opportunity to demonstrate the skill but is unsuccessful (e.g., does not remain on
task), the staff member provides behavioral feedback using “learning language” to redirect the student,
explain the appropriate behavior, and prompt the student again to practice the skill. Learning language
includes the following steps:
Steps

Example Script

1. Initial praise/empathy

Hey John, thanks for listening to me.

2. Description of inappropriate behavior

I see you are not remaining on task. Ms. Johnson asked you to
quietly complete your workbook pages, but you are out of your seat
talking to your friend.

3. Negative consequence

You earned two learning points for not remaining on task.

4. Description of appropriate behavior

Make sure you are remaining on task by sitting in your seat and
working quietly on the workbook pages assigned by Ms. Johnson.

5. Request acknowledgement

Can you get back to work for me?

For progress to occur, students need multiple opportunities each day to practice skills with the support
of behavioral feedback. Ideally, the established daily rounds schedule accommodates multiple
opportunities for practice and behavioral feedback from staff.

Step 7: Students Conference and Reflect with Staff
Students’ daily reflection on their behavior and data is important for learning prosocial behavioral skills
and for changing how they respond to antecedents in the future. At the end of the school day, conduct
end-of-day conferences with students to facilitate reflections on:
• points and feedback they received,
• the reasons why they received them,
• what they should have done differently, and
• what they plan to do the next day.
After conferencing, students also have the
opportunity to add up all of their positive points
and subtract the learning points they earned
that day. With the net points calculated, students

Step 8: Staff Reviews Data and Makes Changes as Needed
Behavioral feedback data can be used to help an emotional support program continuously adapt and
improve for students. Program leads, network leaders, or teams can analyze measures like these on a

• Total number of feedback opportunities
• Ratio of positive to learning feedback
• Percent of feedback that is positive
• Percent of feedback that is learning
• Total number of opportunities for each student to practice skill(s) across settings
• Consistency of feedback throughout day (i.e., a similar amount of feedback given each
class period)

Summary
In order to implement a full continuum of services for students with more intensive behavioral needs
and to ensure these students have access to general education classrooms, schools must design and
implement high-quality behavioral interventions that are adaptable across environments. Behavioral
feedback uses the underpinnings of research on positive reinforcement and token economies to help
students recognize when they have used the skills they are working on or missed an opportunity to
respond differently. Behavioral feedback also helps students learn how to generalize their skills to a
variety of classrooms and groups of students in general and special education settings. Students also
work with staff on daily reflections of their behavior and planning for the next day, setting themselves up
for more successful days.
By investing in their school staff through initial and continuous training, schools can implement
behavioral feedback with fidelity and quality across learning environments. With these supports in
place, students can internalize their areas of growth, reflect on their actions, and see positive change
in their behaviors over time. Mastery School’s reduction in the percentage of Anchor program students
who experience a suspension demonstrates the potential that effective implementation of behavioral
feedback can have for students with disabilities in inclusive settings.

WHAT IS THE PRACTICE?
The regular collection and analysis of data for goal-setting, decision-making, and reflection
to ensure fidelity, quality, and outcomes at all levels of any program.

WHY IS IT IMPORTANT?
• The purpose of data-based decision-making is to set goals, adapt instruction, evaluate
the effectiveness of programs and practices, improve policy, and reallocate resources
to improve student outcomes.1
• Data-based decision-making is particularly important when supporting students with
disabilities, to ensure that interventions, instructional strategies, and accommodations
have their desired effects.
• It is best not to use just one type of data to provide the necessary information to make
decisions but to include various types of data.2
• In the case of Mastery’s Anchor program for students with emotional and behavioral
disabilities, as with all special education supports and services, it is important to know
whether students are in the least restrictive environment possible for them to make
appropriate academic progress. Data needs to be collected and analyzed regularly to
ensure that students are making progress and are receiving the appropriate level of
support to do so.

The Challenge
Ensuring Students Are Receiving the Appropriate Level
of Support and Services
Data-driven decision-making has always been a key component of special education when students are
initially assessed and re-evaluated every three years to determine their eligibility for services. Between
evaluations, schools do not always make frequent use of data to ensure that they are providing
students with an education in the least restrictive environment possible for the students to make
educational progress. As a result, schools often keep students in programs or provide interventions and
services that are overly restrictive and limit the amount students are educated with their peers without
disabilities.3 When educators do not collect and review data to take action for their students in a timely
manner, they often overlook students’ real, current needs.
At Mastery Schools, using data to make decisions about students’ supports is an essential component
of their Anchor program. In order for their team to meet the needs of students with emotional and
behavioral disabilities in their highly individualized program, they need to look at data daily and weekly
to responsively adjust their programmatic supports for students. The Anchor team also reviews data
monthly and quarterly to look at the bigger picture of their program in order to monitor its effectiveness
and identify longer-term improvements they can make.
At Mastery’s Simon Gratz High School, Specialized Services Program Director Veronica Koff needs
to ensure that students are continuously receiving the most appropriate level of support within the
program. The only way Veronica can be confident that students are getting what they need is to know
with certainty that each component of the program, as well as the overall program, is working. Data is
critical for her as she makes decisions about what students and the program need.

The Practice
Using a Variety of Data Sources to Make Decisions about
Students, Practices, and Programs
Mastery Schools created a network-wide data infrastructure that makes it possible for each school’s
Anchor program to make data-driven decisions for students. Veronica and her Anchor team at Gratz
collect, analyze, and respond to data on a regular basis to help students make progress. But their use
of data doesn’t stop there. The team regularly aggregates data for analysis at the program and network
level and uses structured meetings to look at the results and address the needs that arise.
Veronica and her team collect multiple types of data to make decisions about what students, staff,
and the program need to be successful. Three particular systems exemplify their use of data to make
decisions at different levels of their program:

Anchor staff use Kickboard, an app for collecting data on student behavior throughout each school

data from behavioral feedback to make important decisions about students, staff, and the program.
During short daily huddles, the Anchor team meets to discuss the specific and daily needs of students:
• They share information they each know about students’ specific situations that can impact the
students during the day (e.g., Marquis left his phone in the car; Jenna is absent due to a death in
the family).
• They ask questions about upcoming schedule changes and related needs and suggest students
who may need check-ins during that day.
• They reflect on previous student-teacher interactions, behaviors, and any changes that can be
made to improve interactions in the future.

Program Evaluation: Improving a Program
Using Multiple Sources of Data
Once each quarter, each campus Anchor program director meets with the network-level director of
specialized services, Ann McKetta. Together, they use Anchor’s program evaluation rubric to look
for specific programmatic elements — their “Must Haves” — within the campus’s Anchor program.
The rubric is broken into two phases of progress: Phase 1, in which the program meets the minimum
requirements of each criterion, and Phase 2, in which the program exceeds the minimum criteria.
A major purpose of these evaluations is to create consistency across Anchor program student
experiences so that students receive high-quality intervention and support regardless of the school they
attend. The practice ensures campus and network teams partner together to identify bright spots within
the program implementation they can celebrate, as well as to consider opportunities for growth at the
campus level and the network level support needed to progress in priority areas.
At Mastery Gratz, Veronica and Ann walk through Anchor spaces to check for specific program
elements in the rubric before sitting down to review all the data Veronica and her team have collected.
They look at Gratz’s data to look for specific areas to celebrate or elements for growth that Veronica
can work on with her staff or students. While this time gives Veronica a chance to zoom out and look at
her program’s progress and areas for improvement, Ann is able to observe Gratz as a data point from
the network’s point of view, as just one of Mastery’s Anchor programs. Currently, Gratz is scoring mostly
at Phase 2 on all the rubric criteria, so Ann can learn from Gratz’s implementation of the program and
practices to consider how other campuses can learn from the areas in which Gratz excels.

WHAT IS THE PRACTICE?
On a quarterly basis, network leadership and campus leaders collaboratively conduct
observations and review data against a multi-phase rubric to evaluate the effectiveness of
program implementation and develop prioritized goals for improvement.

WHY IS IT IMPORTANT?
• Networks with multiple schools need systems and processes to ensure fidelity and
quality of program implementation, particularly for programs like Anchor.
• Across a network, outcomes can vary for students based on the way programs are
implemented. This variation in outcomes may be even greater for students with
disabilities and those in emotional support programs.
• Networks need ways to identify positive examples of programs and determine how
to allocate resources to ensure students are well served. Networks can measure
programs against these exemplars and use them to devise action plans for program
improvement.
• Importantly, program improvement using a variety of data sources on an ongoing basis
helps improve the academic, social-emotional, and behavioral learning that students
experience.
• Networks can use data from program evaluation to inform network-level support,
coaching, and professional development.

The Challenge
Ensuring High-Quality Programming
Across the Mastery Network
Mastery Schools created the Anchor program to provide an educational environment where students
with emotional and behavioral disabilities can explicitly learn skills that meet their IEP goals. Mastery
created Anchor to be a supportive setting that can flexibly increase the amount of time students
receive education in general education classrooms. To accomplish this, Anchor has multiple levels of
programmatic support and intervention, ranging from students receiving direct support for 20% of their
day to others receiving support for more than half the day when needed. Mastery now offers Anchor
programming at each of their secondary campuses.
School-level program directors need to ensure that the skills students are working to master align with
their IEP goals, that staffing is adequate to provide various levels of support and education for those
skills, and that students have the necessary reinforcement and opportunities to master them. Mastery’s
network-level director for Anchor needs to oversee program implementation fidelity and student
outcomes across campuses so that all students in Anchor programs receive a high-quality program. To
make this possible, Mastery needed a valid and reliable way to monitor program effectiveness, provide
feedback on strengths and growth areas, and provide targeted support for each school’s Anchor
program.

Conclusion

Black and Latinx students with disabilities who are experiencing poverty sit at the intersection of
three of the most pervasive systemic barriers to a high-quality education in the United States. Add
this reality to the fact that students of color are disproportionately identified as having emotional
disabilities1 that lead to their placement in restrictive learning environments away from their peers,
it is clear that this specific population of students faces significant obstacles before even entering
the school building. Furthermore, the school-to-prison pipeline is well documented by the ACLU
and others and disproportionately affects students with emotional support needs based on their
over-suspension and expulsion.2 It is clear that, while there is no one-stop solution to this problem,
schools need to be taking steps to ask themselves, “How can we best serve our students?” and
perhaps more importantly, “Which of our students are we failing to serve at all?”
Mastery Schools recognized these problems and created the Anchor program — a comprehensive
therapeutic support program for students who receive supplemental emotional support. Students in
the Anchor program receive an individualized functional behavior assessment and positive behavior
support plan as well as supports that may include, but are not limited to, an emotional support
classroom, access to a clinician and classroom counselor, individual therapy, group therapy, and
communication with the school social work team and outside mental health service providers.3 When
Mastery joined the Marshall Street
Networked Improvement Community
for Students with Disabilities (NIC),
they set out to refine the program
and expand it to more schools within
their own network, and to disseminate
it broadly to further the reach of its
impact. This collection highlights the
work that Mastery’s Anchor program
did within the NIC and the promise
Mastery and Marshall observed in
pilot efforts between 2019 and 2023.

The improvement team at Mastery’s Simon Gratz High School, one of the 25 schools in the Mastery
network, pushed themselves to consider how students in the Anchor program were situated within
the larger school community. By joining the NIC, the Anchor team set out to explore how they could
create school-wide programmatic supports to significantly increase student attendance rates and
decrease suspension rates for students with emotional and behavioral disabilities, ensuring this group
of students had equitable access to their education. This collection identifies two best practices that
support students with emotional and behavioral disabilities in the general education setting: Building
Student Leaders: Peer-to-Peer Mentorship for Secondary Students and Providing Timely
Behavioral Feedback Across Classrooms. There are also two case studies, Maximizing Student
Progress Through Data-Informed Systems and Refining Program Implementation via Quarterly
Collaborative Evaluation at Mastery Schools.
The improvement team at Gratz focused on practices that would increase attendance and decrease
multi-day suspensions for the students in their program. They hypothesized that they could achieve
these outcomes by increasing students’ sense of belonging to the school community, working with
staff to unpack biases, adjusting school policies that deal with how suspensions are approved, and
explicitly teaching coping skills and strategies to students with academic and behavioral needs. In
2019, prior to participating in the NIC, only 37% of students in Anchor had 90% attendance rates,
compared to 71% of general education students. By June of 2023, after careful implementation
and adjustment of these strategies, that number had increased from 37% to 63%. Over the same
time period, the students in Anchor who were suspended one or more days decreased from 76% to
40%, and the average suspension duration decreased from 3.8 days to 1.5 days. Mastery’s impact
continues to trend positively, showing further improvements in the first semester of the 2023–24
school year in attendance rates and ongoing reduction in days of suspensions for students in Anchor.
Though the Anchor program is comprehensive, the practices that Mastery has implemented are
accessible to schools and networks of all sizes and could be put into place individually rather than
all at once. When done well, each of these practices can lead to positive outcomes for students
with disabilities and for the overall student population. Considering the key lever of ensuring that all
students feel a sense of belonging and that this target population spends at least some time away
from the general education setting, implementing peer-to-peer mentorship is a way to build important
connections, foster a sense of community, and support leadership growth for mentors. Additionally,
explicitly teaching behavioral skills throughout the year can lead to life-long learnings that are
transferable across groups and settings. As one student in the Anchor program shared, “Here I can
be motivated to do the right thing... When my non-Anchor peers get in trouble, I tell them to use the
things [our counselor] tells us, like calm down and get water or something... I think I will still use what
Anchor is teaching me throughout my life.”

Key Takeaways
IT IS POSSIBLE TO SERVE STUDENTS WITH EMOTIONAL AND BEHAVIORAL
DISABILITIES IN NEIGHBORHOOD SCHOOLS.
Too often, schools push out students with emotional and behavioral disabilities to more restrictive
settings. Yet Anchor demonstrates that many of these students can thrive in traditional schools
alongside their peers. When schools rethink their policies and interventions to maximize inclusion – and
educators embrace aligned mindsets, habits, and beliefs – it is possible to create an environment that
sets up students with emotional and behavioral disabilities for success.
DATA IS A KEY LEVER IN SUCCESSFULLY SUPPORTING STUDENTS.
The use of timely, relevant data is crucial in ensuring that all students are receiving the support that they
need. Too often, schools fall into the trap of infrequent monitoring and static support, leaving students
stuck at a support level that no longer meets their needs. By using shorter data cycles and fluid resource
allocation, schools can ensure that their model fits their students, instead of trying to force all students
into a pre-existing model. As students’ quantitative and qualitative data demonstrate progress, they can
be gradually integrated into less restrictive environments within their neighborhood school setting.
CHANGING THE CULTURE CAN DISRUPT THE STATUS QUO.
The school-to-prison pipeline is pervasive and disproportionately affects students with emotional and
behavioral disabilities. Only by shifting the culture of how we see and treat students can we begin
to disrupt the status quo. Programs that focus on acknowledging and eliminating bias in classroom
discipline, policies that favor additional support rather than longer suspensions, and mentorship
programs that encourage students to attend school more frequently are all ways that schools can begin
to change this culture.

The practices laid out in this collection are examples of how
a school has dedicated time, personnel, and resources to
addressing inequity within its community.
Not all schools will be able to make all of these shifts, especially considering the multitude
of challenges that each individual school faces. However, it is crucial that schools push
themselves to ensure that each of their students is being supported and that students’
needs are being addressed regardless of what has been done in the past. Perhaps that
looks like using data more frequently in evaluating the level of support students receive.
Perhaps it involves mentorship. Or perhaps this collection provides inspiration for a new
program. Start by asking, “Which of our students are we failing to serve?” and then make
a plan for how to start.
