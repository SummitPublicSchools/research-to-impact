Snapshot
WHAT IS THE PRACTICE?
Practitioner-focused professional development and aligned coaching that builds
the knowledge and skills of secondary teachers to effectively provide evidence-based
interventions (EBIs) aligned with the science of reading to adolescents with disabilities.

Key Terms for this Practice
What do we mean by practitioner-focused professional development?
For the purposes of this practice, practitioner-focused professional development refers to
professional development sessions that provide educators with structured opportunities to:
1. build knowledge of evidence-based instructional practices aligned with the science
of adolescent reading,
2. apply learnings through in-session reflection and practice,
3. engage in collaborative learning to build collective knowledge and establish mutual
accountability, and
4. adapt practices for the specific needs of the populations served by the participants
with a focus on culturally and linguistically diverse students with disabilities.
What do we mean by aligned coaching?
For the purposes of this practice, coaching refers to individualized teacher coaching that is:
1. content-specific, with the intended outcome of increasing teachers’ capacity to
implement evidence-based practices specific to improving reading outcomes for
adolescent readers with disabilities, and
2. job-embedded, meaning that coaching experiences occur during the teachers’
contracted school days and are directly aligned with their instructional responsibilities.

WHY IS IT IMPORTANT?
National assessments of reading consistently indicate that an alarmingly high number of
secondary students lack the foundational reading skills necessary to meet grade-level
reading benchmarks.1 These low levels of reading proficiency are particularly pronounced
for Black and Latinx students as well as students with disabilities. Low reading levels
present significant barriers to students accessing, making meaning from, and finding joy
in rich, complex, grade-level texts and ultimately limit students’ options for education and
employment.

Snapshot
CONTINUED

To improve literacy outcomes in students — particularly Black and Latinx students with
disabilities experiencing poverty — educators must be well-versed in how to teach
reading. Elementary teachers typically receive training in teaching reading as part of their
credentialing, but secondary teachers typically do not. Though a comprehensive body of
research points to practices that are most likely to close the gaps for adolescent readers
with disabilities, these practices are not consistently implemented in secondary schools
and classrooms, and secondary teachers consistently report that they do not feel equipped
to teach foundational reading skills.
Encouragingly, research shows that sustained, intensive professional development
that focuses on specific academic content and is connected to practice is associated
with student achievement gains.2 Providing professional development that meets these
evidence-based criteria has significant potential to improve reading outcomes for students.

WHY IT WORKS
Practitioner-focused professional development and aligned coaching bridge theory
and practice, supporting the consistent implementation of evidence-based interventions
in classrooms and schools. The professional development practices described in this
case study help secondary educators understand and apply the science of reading,
while equipping them with the tools and coaching to teach reading skills as part of their
core instruction.
The guidance contained in these stories shows
how schools can build the capacity of instructional
coaches and district staff to craft effective and
efficient professional development with the goal
of consistent implementation of evidence-based
interventions aligned with the science of reading.
These best practices emerged from Professional
Learning Communities (PLCs) on evidence-based
literacy interventions as part of the Networked
Improvement Community for Students with
Disabilities (NIC). Together with corresponding
recommended resources, the best practices
provide a blueprint for schools across the country
to build educator capacity for teaching reading.

The Challenge
How can schools support secondary literacy teachers to
implement evidenced-based reading interventions?
The NIC’s literacy-focused PLC was initially
established to build the capacity of systemslevel leaders to design and deliver literacyfocused professional development within their
own networks. After the first year, the Marshall
team found that the content was not consistently
transferring to classroom-based educators.
Further, participants consistently reported a
lack of teacher knowledge related to adolescent
literacy instruction as a key barrier within
their improvement efforts. This is aligned with
research on the topic, which finds that while
the science of reading provides a great deal
of guidance regarding how individuals learn to
read, why some struggle, and the most effective
instructional approaches for teaching reading,
evidence-based practices are not consistently
implemented in classrooms and schools,3 and
this disconnect between theory and practice
disproportionately impacts students from low
socioeconomic backgrounds and students
learning English.4

What is the science
of reading?
The Reading League proposes this as a
common definition for the term:
“The science of reading is a vast,
interdisciplinary body of scientificallybased research about reading and issues
related to reading and writing.
This research has been conducted over
the last five decades across the world,
and it is derived from thousands of studies
conducted in multiple languages. The
science of reading has culminated in a
preponderance of evidence to inform how
proficient reading and writing develop;
why some have difficulty; and how we
can most effectively assess and teach
and, therefore, improve student outcomes
through prevention of and intervention for
reading difficulties.”6

One contributor to this research-to-practice
gap may be a lack of knowledge of evidencebased practices on the part of secondary
teachers. This group consistently reports that both teacher preparation programs and job-embedded
professional development opportunities fail to equip them with the knowledge and skills necessary to
address reading deficits for adolescent readers.5 To build confidence in their capacity to implement
evidence-based reading interventions and to ultimately improve reading outcomes, secondary teachers
need access to ongoing professional learning opportunities that focus on student learning, are contentspecific, and are connected to instructional practice.
Along with the Marshall team, Green Dot Public Schools, Noble Schools, and Uncommon Schools,
the three participating partner schools within the NIC’s literacy PLC, needed to find a way to close
these gaps and ensure that their adolescent students were receiving the evidenced-based reading
interventions that they needed.

The Practice
Year-Long Practitioner-Focused Professional Learning
Community to Build Educator Capacity
The Marshall team, in partnership with educators from the three networks and NIC content expert
Genevieve Thomas, decided to shift the focus of the PLC to be practitioner-focused and to include
classroom-based educators and the network-level staff from the participating networks. The intention
behind this shift was to build capacity and alignment across levels within the systems. The PLC
was redesigned to increase educator and staff capacity to implement evidence-based instructional
practices for adolescent readers with disabilities. A subset of PLC members participated in regular,
job-embedded coaching with a literacy content expert with the goal of increasing the transfer of
research to practice within their classrooms.
Because the intended outcome of the PLC was to bridge the research-to-practice gap by increasing
participants’ capacity to implement evidence-based reading practices, the PLC used a review of
the literature on effective reading practices for adolescent readers with disabilities — “EvidenceBased Strategies for Reading Instruction of Older Students with Learning Disabilities,” by Roberts
et al.7 — as the foundation for their scope and sequence of topics. This research summary outlines
five instructional priorities for adolescent readers with disabilities: word study, fluency, vocabulary,
comprehension, and motivation. The scope and sequence of the PLC was designed to build capacity
within each of these instructional areas.

Intended Outcomes
The goals of the PLC were to support participants to:
• connect with other educators from across the NIC to build community, increase capacity to
implement evidence-based instructional practices, collaboratively seek solutions to common
barriers, and share and celebrate successes;
• describe the evidence-based strategies for reading instruction of adolescents with disabilities,
including summarizing the essential research; and
• modify instructional approaches and materials to more effectively include evidence-based
practices for supporting reading development in secondary readers.

Professional Learning Community Structure
The PLC was designed and facilitated by Genevieve Thomas, literacy content expert, in partnership
with members of the Marshall Improvement team. PLC participants included teachers as well as
school- and network-level leaders from the participating networks. The PLC met monthly for 90-minute
virtual sessions, which included one hour of knowledge building and application around a specific
element of reading, followed by 30 minutes of opt-in small-group or one-on-one coaching with the
content area expert.

Digging into EvidenceBased Practices for
Vocabulary Development
One of the instructional priorities for
adolescents with reading disabilities
is vocabulary, and the PLC covered
evidence-based practices for developing

• Vocabulary knowledge is
highly correlated with reading
comprehension.

instructional sequence following the model

and hearing feedback from a partner,

student vocabulary.

Coaching Structure
PLC members also had access to individual and small group coaching between PLC meetings. The
intended outcome of coaching experiences was to provide individual support to teachers as they worked
to apply the learnings from the content-focused PLC sessions to their local contexts and support them in
accelerating their improvement efforts for students with disabilities. Coaching activities included:
• co-creating instructional materials;
• analyzing student data in order to draw conclusions and inform planning;
• modeling, practicing, and receiving feedback on specific instructional routines; and
• designing instructional protocols.

Putting Word Study in Action at Uncommon Schools
In an early coaching conversation, Val and Genevieve worked together to co-analyze the results of
an oral reading fluency curriculum-based measurement that Val had administered to the students in
her reading intervention class. Val and Genevieve identified that one root cause of low fluency was
difficulty reading multisyllabic words.
As a participant in the literacy PLC, Val learned about effective word study instructional practices,
including teaching syllabication and building morphological awareness. In her coaching conversations
with Genevieve, she developed a hypothesis that explicitly teaching these strategies would improve
students’ skill in reading multisyllabic words, thus improving their reading fluency and ultimately
reading comprehension. Val and Genevieve collaborated over the course of several coaching
conversations to plan instructional strategies, debrief their effectiveness, and make modifications to
instructional plans based on learnings. After several coaching cycles, the instructional protocol for this
reading intervention class now consistently includes explicit word study instruction using evidencebased practices.

How to Implement this Practice
Step 1: Plan Professional Development Opportunities that
Build Teacher Capacity to Implement Evidence-Based
Reading Practices for Adolescents with Disabilities
The process for planning the professional development series is nonlinear. The order of the steps that
follow will vary based on each school or network’s local context, and some steps may not be needed.

Identify a content area expert.
Identify a content area expert who will lead the design of the professional development series and the
individual sessions. This should be an individual with expertise in the science of reading as it relates to
adolescents with disabilities, as well as experience designing professional development for adult learners.
If no internal team members have this expertise, source, vet, and contract with an external consultant.

Articulate a purpose for the professional development series.
Articulate a purpose that clearly connects to improved reading outcomes and increased access to
grade-level texts and content for adolescents with disabilities. Explicitly grounding the purpose of the
professional learning experiences within the school’s or network’s priorities for students with disabilities,
with a focus on increased access to grade-level text and content, ensures that equitable access and
outcomes for all learners remain the north star.

The following questions may serve as helpful catalysts for articulating the purpose of the professional
development series:
• What are the current, related school and/or network priorities, and how might this professional
development serve to further progress toward these priorities?
• What does the data currently tell us about reading achievement and progress for students with
disabilities? How does this compare to the general student population?
• What are teachers saying about their readiness to implement evidence-based reading practices
for students with disabilities? What do performance evaluations and/or instructional observations
suggest about the knowledge and skills teachers need to develop?
• How will building teacher knowledge and skill around evidence-based reading practices for students
with disabilities move the school or network closer to established goals and priorities?

Identify participants.
Start by determining the scale of the implementation. If practitioner-focused professional development
related to evidence-based reading practices for students with disabilities is new for the school or
network, consider testing the structure with a smaller group of users before spreading and scaling. When
starting small, select participants who can commit to participating in the experiences, express excitement
about the content, will work to implement the practices in their planning and instruction, and are willing to
transparently share their learnings and feedback in the interest of improving the structure before scaling.
To create the strongest possible experience, leaders can participate alongside practitioners. This adds
both support and accountability.
At scale, participants in the professional learning experiences should include not only the educators
who are responsible for planning and implementing reading instruction for adolescents with disabilities,
but also those who coach them. If students with disabilities are educated primarily in general education
settings, both special and general educators should be equally involved. Determine participation
expectations for educators, including who is required to participate and who can voluntarily participate.

Design the scope and sequence.
When designing the professional development scope and sequence of topics, consider both the intended
outcomes of the series and the current, relevant research related to those outcomes. For example, if an
intended outcome is for participants to implement valid assessments and analyze the results to identify
specific student reading needs, review the literature on this topic, and extract key findings to anchor
the scope and sequence of topics. See Further Learning for suggested articles summarizing the most
important science of reading concepts for secondary educators of students with disabilities.
Generate a list of learning needs for the target participants in relation to these outcomes. For example,
if the participants have a strong foundation in best practices for vocabulary instruction following a
previous school-wide focus on vocabulary, but little depth of knowledge related to word study and fluency
instruction, consider a focus on word study and fluency that explicitly builds on participants’ knowledge
about effective vocabulary instruction.
Determine the number of professional development sessions, aiming to convene at least monthly, and
map out the topic for each session.

Create a plan for measuring effectiveness.
Based on the intended outcomes for the professional development series, consider the metrics you will
use to measure impact and effectiveness. Consider how you might measure the following:
• Participant perceptions of the professional learning experiences
• Participant learnings (i.e., what participants know and can do as a result of the professional
learning experiences)
• Application to practice (the extent to which participants apply learnings in their planning and
instruction)
• Student outcomes on school or district measures of reading performance and growth
See the How to Measure section of this chapter for more guidance on metrics and measurement.
Define the team of individuals that will meet to review the metrics of success as well as the cadence
for those meetings. This might include the content developer, professional learning facilitator, coaches,
school leaders, and network or district leadership. Convening on a quarterly basis will allow the team to
modify the plan in response to participant feedback and needs.

Professional Learning Sessions
Determine the intended outcomes for each session.
Begin planning with the end in mind, focusing on the series-level outcomes for professional learning.
Consider, “As a result of participating in this professional development series, what do we want

• Participants will know and be able to define the literacy instructional focus areas for adolescents

• Participants will believe that explicit and systematic instruction aligned with identified student

• Participants will increase their sense of self-efficacy related to designing and implementing

will support participants in achieving the outcomes.

connect and collaborate with others. Plan ways for
participants to actively capture learnings and make
meaning across the session.

Reimagining Reading Comprehension Instruction
One session of the practitioner-focused literacy PLC implemented within the NIC was focused on
evidence-based practices for supporting reading comprehension instruction. The purpose of the
session was articulated as “reimagining reading comprehension instruction as an opportunity to
explicitly develop the specific subskills that will support secondary students, particularly secondary
students with reading and language disabilities, in accessing and making meaning from complex,
grade-level texts in order to ensure equitable access to core content for our target population.”
After clarifying the purpose of the session, Genevieve designed activities to support participants in
achieving this outcome. These included:
• a reflection activity in which participants shared in partners their current understandings of reading
comprehension;
• a summary of the most relevant and current research related to reading comprehension instruction
for adolescents with disabilities, followed by an opportunity for participants to process and capture
learnings;
• breakout conversations where participants could choose one aspect of reading comprehension
instruction to dig into more deeply with peers; and
• time for participants to consider applications to their current instructional planning practices and to
commit to action-oriented next steps.

Step 3: Deliver the Professional Development Sessions
Facilitate professional development sessions.
It is important that sessions are led by strong facilitators who possess cultural humility and an
awareness of cultural and identity-sustaining pedagogy. This is particularly important for our target
population. Effective facilitators acknowledge the ways in which dynamics of race, class, and ability
impact reading achievement for the target population while simultaneously communicating high
expectations for both students and educators.8

Use feedback to assess and improve.
Gather feedback after each session, and use it to assess impact and inform the design of subsequent
sessions. While articulating a scope and sequence for the professional development series will aid in
planning individual sessions, it is also important to view the scope and sequence as a flexible roadmap

that will be adapted based on participant needs and feedback. Collecting feedback at the end of each
professional learning experience helps to know both the extent to which session objectives were met
and what participants may need for future sessions and coaching experiences. Feedback can be
gathered using a variety of methods, including surveys, pre- and post-assessments, or frameworks
such as “3-2-1,” in which participants share three key learnings from the experience, two immediate,
action-oriented next steps, and one lingering question or idea they intend to explore further.

Adjust the scope and sequence as needed.
A team should regularly revisit and revise as necessary the professional development scope and
sequence. The team should review feedback, collaboratively synthesize trends, identify pressing
participant needs, and brainstorm responses. After feedback has been reviewed and analyzed, plan
to communicate back to participants what was learned from their feedback and how the professional
learning approach is being modified to address their needs.

Step 4: Provide Content Area Coaching Between
Professional Development Sessions
Plan for coaching.
Determine who will lead the coaching experiences between professional development sessions.
This might be the individual that designed and facilitated the professional development, or it could
be another team member. First and foremost, the coach should have a strong foundation in teacher
coaching practices. The coach should also be well-versed in evidence-based reading practices for
adolescents with disabilities, which implies that they should be attending sessions alongside the
teachers they coach.
Align the coaching approach with any relevant school or network coaching models or processes.
For example, if the district has a specific protocol that is followed in other instructional coaching
experiences, apply that same framework for these literacy-focused coaching sessions.
Determine the frequency of coaching sessions, ideally biweekly, but with at least one coaching
session occurring between each professional learning session. Schedule coaching sessions with
individual teachers early in the process of planning professional learning to support consistency and
accountability, but practice flexibility and allow for adjustments to the schedule as needed.

Facilitate coaching experiences.
Coaches should establish a student-centered outcome for each coaching touchpoint in collaboration
with the teacher, and outcomes should clearly align with the reading instructional knowledge and skills
developed in the professional development sessions. Coaching experiences should include active
practice of instructional planning and/or literacy practices, along with feedback whenever possible.
Both the coach and the teacher are responsible for documenting key decisions and next steps, and for
following through on their respective next steps between coaching touchpoints.

Practice Snapshot
SPREADING PROFESSIONAL LEARNING AT NOBLE SCHOOLS
Several special education teachers at Noble Schools have been implementing a structured protocol for
developing reading fluency since the 2021–22 school year. After seeing consistent implementation of this
practice result in improved reading fluency for students, the special education leadership team decided to
provide a professional learning experience to other educators within the network in an effort to spread the
promising practice.

The team first set out to identify content area experts to design and deliver the professional
development session. They solicited an internal co-teaching team, special education teacher
Megan Murphy and general education World History teacher Derek DeHaan, who had been
successfully implementing a reading fluency protocol in their co-taught World History class for
several months, to lead the work.
Megan and Derek designed a professional development session and key training materials in
alignment with the recommended practices described within this chapter. First, they explicitly
shared the “why” by summarizing important research that clearly establishes the role of reading
fluency in the ability to make meaning from text. They thoughtfully connected the specific topic of
fluency to their overall network goals, sharing that “Literacy is the key to healthy, choice-filled lives
for our students.”
Within the session, Megan and Derek clearly communicated the evidence-based practices
for developing reading fluency by sharing a specific instructional protocol, showing a video of
their class engaged in the protocol, and reviewing the resources they developed to support
implementation of the practice. They built participant buy-in by sharing data that clearly suggested
an improvement in student reading outcomes as a result of participating in the reading fluency
protocol, as well as testimonials from students. The session concluded with opportunities for
participants to choose from several optional activities designed to support them in applying learning
to their own instructional contexts.
Megan and Derek facilitated the session twice during Noble professional development days,
in which teachers could choose sessions aligned with their instructional priorities. Across the
two offerings over 50 Noble teachers opted to attend. Feedback collected following the session
indicated that a significant number of attendees planned to incorporate reading fluency practices
into their instructional planning after attending the session.
See Repeated Reading: Building Oral Reading Fluency in Secondary Readers for
comprehensive guidance on how to implement instruction designed to improve students’ oral
reading fluency.

The Impact
Empowering Educators to Put Knowledge in Action
Upon conclusion of the year-long practitioner-focused PLC, 100% of participants reported that the PLC:
• enhanced their knowledge related to adolescent literacy,
• provided them with practical tools and resources that were applicable to their work, and
• was instrumental in addressing challenges faced within their roles and local contexts.
Feedback from participants collected over the course of the PLC suggests that structured professional
development sessions and aligned coaching were successful in achieving the intended outcomes and
enhancing NIC members’ continuous improvement efforts. The shift to a practitioner focus for the 2022–
23 school year more effectively met the needs of both systems-level and classroom-level participants.
For example, Glynis Shulters, a member of the Green Dot improvement team working at the network
level, reflected on the ways in which participating in the PLC supported her team’s efforts to enact both
tiered literacy assessments and evidence-based practices for supporting reading fluency: “The Literacy
PLC has helped me deepen my knowledge base of the science of reading, which has helped me be
able to support our site team at Watts to create a multiple gating approach and structures for fluency
intervention to begin.” Val Dutta, the literacy innovation specialist and teacher of reading intervention
classes within the Uncommon network, reported, “I had not taught word study to high school students
before, and this PLC has given me materials that I can directly use with teachers and students. The
students are very engaged in this sequence of materials and building real skill, thanks to this PLC.”
The applied learning achieved through the PLC also increased educators’ capacity to implement
a variety of evidence-based instructional practices for adolescent readers with disabilities across
NIC partners:
• At Green Dot California, explicit professional development has been designed to build the
knowledge and skill sets of educators to implement oral reading fluency interventions as they
spread and scale efforts. This includes an initial team training with application activities, guidance
documents grounded in the research and practical knowledge from the PLC members, and a
gradual release coaching model. Additionally, PLC participation inspired conversations about
research on motivation for struggling readers, which led one school to implement an incentive
program that increased software engagement and Lexile growth.
• At Uncommon Schools, Val Dutta continues to apply the knowledge acquired from the PLC in
Academic Prep and other reading intervention spaces, focusing on building reading skills within the
context of grade-level instruction. Uncommon has spread their instructional framework and literacy
coaching practice to additional schools, with the intent to scale it further in the upcoming school year.
• At Noble Schools, PLC members are integrating the research and practice they gathered within
the PLC to construct and adopt district-wide professional development and related resources for
educators on building reading skills. They have trained classroom teachers and support staff in
the science of reading and the implementation of a structured repeated-reading protocol to apply
evidence-based interventions in core classes. The protocol helped students build reading
fluency while learning course content.
