Snapshot
WHAT IS THE PRACTICE?
The regular collection and analysis of data for goal-setting, decision-making, and reflection
to ensure fidelity, quality, and outcomes at all levels of any program.

WHY IS IT IMPORTANT?
• The purpose of data-based decision-making is to set goals, adapt instruction, evaluate
the effectiveness of programs and practices, improve policy, and reallocate resources
to improve student outcomes.1
• Data-based decision-making is particularly important when supporting students with
disabilities, to ensure that interventions, instructional strategies, and accommodations
have their desired effects.
• It is best not to use just one type of data to provide the necessary information to make
decisions but to include various types of data.2
• In the case of Mastery’s Anchor program for students with emotional and behavioral
disabilities, as with all special education supports and services, it is important to know
whether students are in the least restrictive environment possible for them to make
appropriate academic progress. Data needs to be collected and analyzed regularly to
ensure that students are making progress and are receiving the appropriate level of
support to do so.

The Challenge
Ensuring Students Are Receiving the Appropriate Level
of Support and Services
Data-driven decision-making has always been a key component of special education when students are
initially assessed and re-evaluated every three years to determine their eligibility for services. Between
evaluations, schools do not always make frequent use of data to ensure that they are providing
students with an education in the least restrictive environment possible for the students to make
educational progress. As a result, schools often keep students in programs or provide interventions and
services that are overly restrictive and limit the amount students are educated with their peers without
disabilities.3 When educators do not collect and review data to take action for their students in a timely
manner, they often overlook students’ real, current needs.
At Mastery Schools, using data to make decisions about students’ supports is an essential component
of their Anchor program. In order for their team to meet the needs of students with emotional and
behavioral disabilities in their highly individualized program, they need to look at data daily and weekly
to responsively adjust their programmatic supports for students. The Anchor team also reviews data
monthly and quarterly to look at the bigger picture of their program in order to monitor its effectiveness
and identify longer-term improvements they can make.
At Mastery’s Simon Gratz High School, Specialized Services Program Director Veronica Koff needs
to ensure that students are continuously receiving the most appropriate level of support within the
program. The only way Veronica can be confident that students are getting what they need is to know
with certainty that each component of the program, as well as the overall program, is working. Data is
critical for her as she makes decisions about what students and the program need.

The Practice
Using a Variety of Data Sources to Make Decisions about
Students, Practices, and Programs
Mastery Schools created a network-wide data infrastructure that makes it possible for each school’s
Anchor program to make data-driven decisions for students. Veronica and her Anchor team at Gratz
collect, analyze, and respond to data on a regular basis to help students make progress. But their use
of data doesn’t stop there. The team regularly aggregates data for analysis at the program and network
level and uses structured meetings to look at the results and address the needs that arise.
Veronica and her team collect multiple types of data to make decisions about what students, staff,
and the program need to be successful. Three particular systems exemplify their use of data to make
decisions at different levels of their program:

Behavioral Feedback: Using Daily Data
for Immediate Action
Anchor staff use Kickboard, an app for collecting data on student behavior throughout each school
day, as they observe, support, and coach students on the SAM skills identified in their IEPs. Students
and staff can use the app to see data from feedback given to students in real time. Counselors enter
information on tablets, which are also available in Anchor classrooms for students to use throughout
the day to look at their feedback and at the end of the day to cash out points they earned from their
behavior. In daily conferences with a staff member, students reflect on their behavior, progress, and
goals for the next day.
In the same way that students use the data from Kickboard to reflect and plan, Anchor staff use the
data from behavioral feedback to make important decisions about students, staff, and the program.
During short daily huddles, the Anchor team meets to discuss the specific and daily needs of students:
• They share information they each know about students’ specific situations that can impact the
students during the day (e.g., Marquis left his phone in the car; Jenna is absent due to a death in
the family).
• They ask questions about upcoming schedule changes and related needs and suggest students
who may need check-ins during that day.
• They reflect on previous student-teacher interactions, behaviors, and any changes that can be
made to improve interactions in the future.

Using Daily Behavioral
Feedback Data to Make
Programmatic Decisions
The Anchor team repeatedly saw data showing
that students were struggling behaviorally in
math classes. Notes recorded in Kickboard
clearly indicated that a number of students
in Anchor needed more support with their
foundational math skills, making math classes
particularly challenging because academic
frustration was often the antecedent to students
being redirected by counselors. In response
to the data, Anchor added a math skills class
to their academic offerings to help students
increase their foundational math skills and feel
more successful in their math classes.

4.6

Modifying How Staff Respond
to Students Based on Their
Disability and Needs
All students at Mastery Schools are held to behavioral
expectations. For students in the Anchor program, school
staff need different ways to respond to schoolwide “culture”
infractions to support students’ therapeutic needs. To that
end, Mastery created the Modified Anchor Response
Plan (MARP) as an adaptation to their existing culture plan.
The MARP provides questions and protocols to guide staff
responses, which vary based on the level of infraction:
• Level I Infraction: A behavior that disrupts the learning
environment or that is insubordination
• Level II Infraction: A behavior that impacts the
school culture and community of safety, respect, and
cooperation that is fostered at Mastery
• Level III Infraction: Serious violations of the Mastery
Disciplinary Code of Conduct and/or behaviors that are
criminal violations
The level and frequency of a student’s culture infractions
impact the student’s Anchor program level.

Program Evaluation: Improving a Program
Using Multiple Sources of Data
Once each quarter, each campus Anchor program director meets with the network-level director of
specialized services, Ann McKetta. Together, they use Anchor’s program evaluation rubric to look
for specific programmatic elements — their “Must Haves” — within the campus’s Anchor program.
The rubric is broken into two phases of progress: Phase 1, in which the program meets the minimum
requirements of each criterion, and Phase 2, in which the program exceeds the minimum criteria.
A major purpose of these evaluations is to create consistency across Anchor program student
experiences so that students receive high-quality intervention and support regardless of the school they
attend. The practice ensures campus and network teams partner together to identify bright spots within
the program implementation they can celebrate, as well as to consider opportunities for growth at the
campus level and the network level support needed to progress in priority areas.
At Mastery Gratz, Veronica and Ann walk through Anchor spaces to check for specific program
elements in the rubric before sitting down to review all the data Veronica and her team have collected.
They look at Gratz’s data to look for specific areas to celebrate or elements for growth that Veronica
can work on with her staff or students. While this time gives Veronica a chance to zoom out and look at
her program’s progress and areas for improvement, Ann is able to observe Gratz as a data point from
the network’s point of view, as just one of Mastery’s Anchor programs. Currently, Gratz is scoring mostly
at Phase 2 on all the rubric criteria, so Ann can learn from Gratz’s implementation of the program and
practices to consider how other campuses can learn from the areas in which Gratz excels.

The Impact
Using Data to Improve
Student Outcomes
Veronica and her team at Gratz have made databased decisions to improve Anchor's systems and
structures. They have also been able to increase
attendance and reduce the number of suspensions
for students in their program. The percentage of
students in Mastery Gratz’s Anchor program attending
school at least 90% of the time has increased by
almost 30 percentage points since 2019, before the
COVID-19 pandemic. Meanwhile, the percentage
of students in Anchor who received at least one
suspension declined by about 35 points. By using
data to drive decisions on the quality and fidelity of
program implementation, Veronica and her team have
also helped improve student outcomes that will make
a difference in those students’ lives for years.
