Preface
In Navigating Data for Multi-Tiered Systems of Support: Equity-based practices for using data at the
district, school, and classroom levels to accelerate student supports, we share a set of best practices for
leveraging data to respond to student academic and social-emotional needs with a particular focus on
Black and Latinx students with disabilities experiencing poverty. The Networked Improvement Community
for Students with Disabilities (NIC), a group of 10 public charter school networks serving more than
75,000 students nationwide, identified the best practices through their work between 2019 and 2023.
Led by Marshall Street at Summit Public Schools, in partnership with technical assistance providers
NIRN, RTI International, SRI International, and SWIFT Education Center, the purpose of the three-year
NIC was to systematically improve the way our education system serves Black and Latinx students with
disabilities experiencing poverty and share these solutions with school systems everywhere. This report is
funded by the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation. The findings and conclusions contained within are those
of the authors and do not necessarily reflect positions or policies of the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation.
The NIC used continuous improvement to translate existing research for improving learning outcomes
into best practices and to iterate the practices to ensure they worked in a variety of school contexts.

In Navigating Data for Multi-Tiered Systems of Support: Equity-based
practices for using data at the district, school, and classroom levels to
accelerate student supports, we share the results of this vital work:
A literature review summarizing the key research related to multi-tiered systems of
support
Three best practices in which schools in the NIC used data to individualize student
supports. Each best practice includes:
– Case studies highlighting how schools in the NIC implemented the practices
– Step-by-step implementation guidance
– Key prerequisites and accelerants for successful implementation in any context
Two additional case studies, which look at how network-level leaders can use data
as a team to more effectively support schools and how another network used a text
messaging intervention to engage families in reviewing students’ grades
One guidance document that provides recommendations to consider when creating
and revising data dashboards to meet the needs of school staff to implement these
practices

We share these best practices so that any school in the country can leverage the learning from the
NIC to accelerate dramatic gains in learning outcomes for their students with disabilities. We hope
that administrators and educators across the country can use the stories within this guide to build their
school cultures of continuous improvement as well.

Navigating Data for MultiTiered Systems of Support
Introduction
The Challenge
America’s public schools fall short in meeting the needs of students with
disabilities.
Students with disabilities consistently perform at levels below their peers without disabilities. On the
most recent administration of the National Assessment of Educational Progress, 8th grade students
with disabilities performed 39–41 points lower than their peers without disabilities in reading
and math,1 illustrating just how deeply schools are failing students with disabilities. These disparities
persist long after graduation, as students with disabilities are less likely than their peers to enroll in
postsecondary school, to be productively engaged in community activities, to live independently, and to
achieve financial independence.2

Implementation of MTSS is inconsistent in secondary schools.
Multi-tiered systems of support (MTSS) have been associated with improved academic achievement
and reduced incidents of suspension and other punitive disciplinary actions for students.3 Students with
disabilities who attend schools with strong MTSS implementations also experience improved academic
and behavioral outcomes and increased graduation rates.4 These findings indicate one way to improve
the poor outcomes of students with disabilities is to implement MTSS at scale. MTSS implementation
at the secondary level, however, still lags behind elementary schools, meaning that students with
disabilities in middle and high schools do not consistently receive a benefit from this structured
approach to data-based decision-making and intervention planning.5 Using data-driven early warning
systems that identify students who need additional support, and then responding to that data and
implementing appropriate interventions, is likely a key strategy for using MTSS to improve outcomes for
secondary students.6

What is MTSS?
DEFINITION
A multi-tiered system of support (MTSS) is a preventative framework that leverages data to organize instruction
and intervention to improve academic, behavioral, and social-emotional outcomes for students.7

COMPONENTS
The MTSS framework is composed of four essential components:8
1. Screening — regularly administered assessments to identify students at risk for poor academic, socialemotional, or behavioral outcomes
2. Progress monitoring — the ongoing, frequent collection and use of data to:
A. assess students’ performance
B. quantify a student's rate of improvement or responsiveness to instruction or intervention
C. evaluate the effectiveness of instruction and intervention using valid and reliable measures9
3. A multi-level prevention system — increasingly intensive instruction and intervention organized across
three tiers, with “Tier 1” referring to evidence-based,
high-quality schoolwide academic, social-emotional,
and behavioral supports for all students, “Tier 2”
referring to small-group, standardized interventions
aligned with identified student needs for some
students, and “Tier 3” referring to intensive and
individualized supports for few students with ongoing,
intensive needs
4. Data-based decision-making — the use of data to
make decisions about instruction, movement within the
multi-level prevention system, allocation of resources,
and identification of students with disabilities

HOW IT HELPS STUDENTS

Equity-Based MTSS Model by Swift Schools

Implementation of multi-tiered systems of support results in improved academic, social-emotional, and
behavioral outcomes for students.10 The features of MTSS that contribute to improved and more equitable
outcomes for students include the following:
• Decisions made within an MTSS framework are driven by data.
• MTSS emphasizes high-quality, evidence-based instruction at all tiers.
• Collaborative, data-driven decision-making and strong leadership are core to effective MTSS.
• MTSS represents a systematic approach to pursuing equitable student outcomes.
Benefits of consistent MTSS implementation include the following:
• It allows for all students to receive timely support for their unique student needs, including academic,
behavioral, and social-emotional needs.11
• Educators can more efficiently identify student needs and respond to them in a timely manner.12
• Fewer students “fall through the cracks.”13
• Students with disabilities are less stigmatized for receiving additional supports and services.14

Many schools lack the resources to build robust data systems for
instructional decision-making.
Unfortunately, many schools lack access to or funding for data systems and infrastructure that are key
to implementing MTSS well, such as progress-monitoring tools, aligned curriculum, and intervention
programs based on evidence-based practices that would support cycles of instructional improvement
as part of an MTSS.15 Building or having another company build these custom solutions in ways that
enable effective and efficient instructional decision-making requires capacity or money that most
schools do not have available. The staff training and planning time necessary to use these tools
effectively also requires additional funds. Without preplanning for the allocation of resources toward
data systems aligned to their instructional needs, schools cannot make changes that will have marked
effects on student learning and achievement.16

Existing data infrastructures are not designed to support data-driven
decision-making.
Over the last 30 years, student information systems have evolved from simply reporting student
enrollment data to including data from state standardized assessments, diagnostic and interim
assessments, and student course and assignment grades. These evolutions have not always
accounted for the different ways in which educators interact with data, making it challenging for schools
to use the data they have collected for their decision-making. As schools more regularly use data in
structured ways to reflect on practice and drive instructional decisions as part of their MTSS, they
need ways to look at data at multiple levels and disaggregate results by subgroups — that is, they
need tools designed to drive instructional change. To make the development of these tools possible for
educators to effectively review student data and monitor their progress, system-wide support for data
infrastructure, systems, structures, and training are essential.17

Effective use of data requires change to systems and culture.
Schools often train educators to interpret data from student information systems in a single
professional development but fail to build regular, structured time for educators to make use of these
skills.18 To avoid this, school leaders can reinforce a culture of continuous improvement by devoting
time for educators to participate in cycles of reviewing data, identifying
students who need additional support, creating action plans, and
assessing the effectiveness of those action plans.19 However, building
these data routines into existing systems and structures — such as
coaching sessions, regular professional development, or collaborative
time between educators — can be difficult. As a result, data routines fail
to become habits or part of the educator workflow, and knowledge built
in training is lost over time.

Our Approach
MTSS involves a host of essential components to ensure schools have a system for responding to
students’ individual needs. Keenly aware of the power of data to drive decision-making, schools within
the NIC embraced a commitment to developing the tools, routines, and systems needed to leverage
data effectively within an MTSS approach. The NIC’s commitment to focusing on data does not ignore
the other aspects of successful MTSS approaches, but it highlights how the collection and use of data is
fundamental to the other pieces of an MTSS system. This work was guided by the following principles:
EFFECTIVE DATA-DRIVEN DECISION-MAKING RELIES ON TOOLS AND
INFRASTRUCTURE.
DATA-DRIVEN DECISION-MAKING IS BEST FACILITATED IN COLLABORATIVE
STRUCTURES THAT ENGAGE THE RIGHT PEOPLE.
CLEARLY ARTICULATED AND CONSISTENTLY IMPLEMENTED ROUTINES FACILITATE
EFFICIENT AND EFFECTIVE DATA-DRIVEN DECISION-MAKING.
Together these principles help educators implementing MTSS address gaps at all levels of the school
system, from the network all the way to teachers. In turn, this pushes them to prioritize student groups
positioned furthest from opportunity, like students with disabilities who are Black, Latinx, and living in
poverty. As educators continuously review data and assess their practices to adjust instruction
to meet the needs of priority groups, improved teacher practices and new instructional strategies benefit
all students.

Data Tools and Infrastructure
To effectively implement an MTSS framework, schools need system-wide support for data collection,
analysis, and decision-making. Data infrastructure should facilitate the efficient collection of data
describing a range of student outcomes,20 allow for monitoring student responsiveness to instruction
and intervention, and support educators in analyzing data to make informed decisions about planning
content and instructional strategies.21 STEM Prep and Collegiate Academies built their own systems
to enable educators to transfer data from existing student information systems into more usable formats
for regular analysis and instructional decision-making. The experience of both networks and existing
research on MTSS demonstrate that school leaders and educators also needed training, protected
time, and resources to learn how to use data and make informed decisions about planning content and
instructional strategies to meet all students’ needs.22

Teams that Bring the Right People Together
To meet the needs of students successfully within an MTSS approach, diverse teams of educators
need to collaboratively and routinely analyze trends in student performance, including data on student
subgroups. This needs to happen at the classroom, school, and district level. To have the greatest
impact at the school level, site leaders need to bring together teams of educators with diverse
perspectives, create time and space for different types of school teams to regularly review and respond

to data, and support the use of collaborative problem-solving to find the best strategies to meet
students’ needs. Summit Public Schools and STEM Prep engaged grade-level teams to regularly
analyze student data in order to collaboratively identify potential interventions to address specific
student needs. Mastery Schools created an interdisciplinary team to address students’ postsecondary

Central office teams also need to gather leaders across the district to collaboratively take
an MTSS approach to thinking about how they use data to individualize the different levels
of support they are providing to schools. Green Dot Public Schools realized that they needed
to individualize the types and intensity of supports they gave schools rather than looking only at
aggregated data and making network-wide decisions for all schools through a one-size-fits-all lens.
Their team of network-level leaders looked at disaggregated data by school as well as by student
groups so they could devise strategies to support school leaders’ work to improve outcomes at their
schools.
Bringing the right people together also includes regularly engaging students and families in
routines that teach them how to analyze their own data, develop goals using data, and create
plans that help them meet those goals. For example, STRIVE Prep engaged families using text
messaging to give families information about students’ grades and a channel to communicate with
schools about their students’ academic progress. Additionally, KIPP Northern California and STRIVE
Prep created 1:1 student-conferencing structures to teach students how to use their data from course
and assignment grades to set goals and make plans to achieve them. These types of engagement
are particularly important for families who are Latinx, Black, and living in poverty because they have
historically been disenfranchised by the education system and disempowered from accessing student
grades, communicating with educators, or advocating for their students’ needs.

Routines and Regular Data Analysis
By creating regular, dedicated time for teams to engage in data routines, educators can respond to
students’ needs more quickly and effectively, improving student outcomes. Teams should meet at
regular intervals with clear agendas and meeting protocols that guide educators to:
1. Identify which students or disaggregated student groups need additional support on which skills
2. Choose the best strategy to address needs
3. Make a plan for how to implement the strategy
4. Reassess to determine the effectiveness of the strategy used
5. Choose to continue to use the strategy, adapt the strategy, or discontinue using the strategy
The NIC, as a community, found it necessary to develop and refine strategies for integrating the use
of data into educators’ workflow to ensure the timely response to students’ needs. Routines were
developed at the individual teacher, team, school, and network levels to support system-wide use of
data. Collegiate Academies, STEM Prep, Summit Public Schools, and Green Dot Public Schools
all created structures that provide educators with regular, dedicated time to engage in data routines.
Collegiate Academies built these routines into their co-teachers’ co-planning meetings. Green Dot

For these data routines to be effective, school leaders need to consider the types of training necessary

Collegiate Academies and Summit Public Schools found that initial professional development is
best supported with regular coaching to ensure that educators can analyze and respond to data with
confidence. Coaches can also help educators learn how to build their own regular practice of reviewing
and responding to data to ensure that all learners are mastering content and that their teaching practice
improves over time.
Data-driven decision-making processes within an MTSS can be implemented from the district level
down to the student level so that every stakeholder uses data to guide decisions in the interest of
improved student outcomes. This collection of practices highlights the ways that data is essential
to meeting the needs of students who are furthest from opportunity so that educators can
address their needs as responsively and efficiently as possible.

Enabling Conditions
Each chapter within the collection identifies the prerequisites needed to implement each specific
practice. However, a set of overarching prerequisites is shared across all practices:
• Dedicated and protected collaboration time for teachers, grade-level teams, and instructional
coaches to collect and analyze student-level data by class, course, and grade levels
• Accessible data readily available in a data tool or dashboard
• A school-wide commitment to collaborative problem-solving that is supported by a defined set of
strategies that faculty can choose from to respond to a variety of needs

Dedicated Time
Educators need dedicated time to analyze data, design interventions, and monitor the progress of those
interventions. Scheduling time in the school week for data analysis, problem-solving concerns in the
data, planning, and implementing tiered supports is a key requirement for MTSS to be successful.23
Although individuals and teams may meet at different cadences based on their purposes, protecting
time for data analysis and planning is essential, and schools must account for all stakeholders when
building schedules.

Accessible Data Systems
Most school systems have one or more data or student information systems that house student
data. Many of these tools are simply gradebooks that do not allow educators to see patterns across
assignments or standards, while others report standardized test scores but do not provide visibility
into students’ specific errors. While in the NIC, Collegiate Academies and STEM Prep found that
their existing data systems did not always facilitate targeted data analysis and intervention planning
for educators. In response, both networks built alternative data systems that gave educators different
ways to look at their student data to more easily make instructional decisions. Some tools are more
adaptable than others (i.e., they allow for individual school or network customization) or better fit the
purpose a school or team has for the data. Much of the usefulness of a data system comes down to
the accessibility of the data tool (e.g., user experience, ease of navigation, and ability to see a range of
data) and the ease with which educators can sort or filter data. When selecting a data system, feedback
from a variety of educators is essential to understanding whether a given data tool will work for a given
purpose or not.
Schools may discover a need for custom dashboards to facilitate data protocols. Excel, Google Sheets,
and Tableau are examples of platforms that can be leveraged for this purpose. To learn more about the
process for creating a data dashboard, see How-To: Design and Prototype a Data Dashboard.

Schoolwide Commitment to Problem-Solving
While it is important that schools or networks create structures and resources that help educators
determine the best strategy to meet identified needs, an underlying commitment to collaborative
problem-solving is likely to yield stronger results than problem-solving by individual stakeholders.
Educators who work together to find new solutions, especially to the most challenging situations, can
build their toolbox of strategies (a list of interventions, for example) more quickly. In addition, schools
that foster a commitment to collaborative problem-solving are likely to have teachers who engage
in informal activities to solve problems without waiting for formal structures such as grade-level or
departmental meetings.
Schools will benefit from developing a set of easily
accessible resources that educators can draw on

The Collection
This collection includes three best practices and two case studies essential to the work of
improving the use of data to devise tiered levels of support for schools, students, and families on
behalf of improving learning outcomes for all students, as well as a guidance document to use when
considering how to set up and improve data tools to put these practices into action.
Each of the practices detailed in this collection is an independently strong and valuable practice for
improving the use of data by educators and teams within an MTSS model to make gains for our priority
population. However, when implemented layer by layer, the steps described in this collection strengthen
data practices at all levels of an education system. Through these practices, schools in the NIC
embedded MTSS within every level of their school systems, with each level benefitting from the levels
above. The combined effect helped NIC schools to address the needs of students in order to improve
their outcomes.

Chapter 1: Implementing Routine Data Cycles to Rapidly Address
Student Needs
System-wide data routines are necessary to support educators to review classroom-level data at
least biweekly to determine student needs and drive instructional decision-making and interventions.
In addition, educators benefit from accessible data systems that allow them to sort student data in
meaningful ways and disaggregate data by subgroups. This chapter details the steps a network
or school can take to design regular data routines so educators can respond in a timely manner to
students’ needs. The chapter also describes how data systems and professional development can help
strengthen these data routines for schools.

Chapter 2: Establishing Effective Data Routines in School-Based Teams
While educators need to individually analyze data and make instructional decisions based on the
individual student needs shown in that data, school-based teams also need to gather and use data to
coordinate timely, targeted support for students across grade-, department-, or other site-level teams.
This chapter describes different types of school-based teams and their purposes. It also discusses
how teams can create useful structures to ensure efficient meetings and create accountability for the
resulting action items. Strong data routines used by teams of educators can help supplement the
individual and regular use of data for decision-making in schools.

Chapter 3: Student Conferencing for Life and Academic Skills
Student conferencing is a regular, consistent time during class for teachers to coach students 1:1 on
how to set goals, make plans to achieve their goals, enact their plans, and reflect on their progress.
Any school staff member can conduct conferencing with students, but the schools in the NIC that
implemented this practice piloted conferencing with special educators and students with disabilities.
This chapter explains how 1:1 student conferencing can be implemented in a variety of configurations
based on individual school capacity and student needs. The steps outline the skills students need in
order to begin conferencing as well as those they can gain by participating in conferencing.

Case Study: How Network Leaders Use Data to Respond to School-Based
Needs: Green Dot Public Schools
The use of MTSS is not confined within the walls of each school building. It can also be used at a
system level when network leaders consider how to best support each of their schools. Green Dot
Public Schools built a team of network-level leaders who meet monthly to analyze data and identify
tiered supports for schools based on their needs. This case study describes the journey that Green
Dot’s leaders went through to consistently review data through an equity lens and devise strategies to
best support the schools within their own locus of control.

Literature Review
ABSTRACT
Multi-Tiered System of Support (MTSS) is a framework for schools and districts to address
barriers to learning for students. This literature review synthesizes the existing research
base on MTSS; namely what MTSS is, why it is important, the benefits and challenges
of the framework, an overview of common implementation strategies, and what is
currently known about the efficacy of MTSS. This review is designed for school district
administrators considering adopting or improving MTSS practices at their district.

Introduction
This literature review was written for an audience of educators, schools, and districts seeking to
improve outcomes for secondary students with a focus on Black and Latinx students with disabilities
living in poverty. In this literature review, we share a concise overview of MTSS, an adaptive organizing
framework for data-informed academic and behavioral instruction and support that meets student
needs in a timely manner. The purpose of this review is to equip district- and school-level staff who
are considering adopting or improving MTSS practices with a summary of the research on MTSS so
that staff can make informed, thoughtful implementation decisions for their districts. The literature
review was conducted by Marshall Street, a coalition of educators working to systematically improve
opportunities for students across the United States.
This literature review is part of a larger collection of improvement tools for districts. The Networked
Improvement Community for Students with Disabilities (NIC), a group of 10 public charter school
networks serving more than 75,000 students nationwide, launched in 2019 to make dramatic gains in
the learning experiences, environments, and outcomes for Black and Latinx students with disabilities
experiencing poverty. Led by Marshall Street at Summit Public Schools in partnership with technical
assistance providers NIRN, RTI International, SRI International, and SWIFT Education Center, NIC
participants used continuous improvement methods, supported by this literature review of leading

What is MTSS?
A Multi-Tiered System of Support is a framework for educators for organizing, aligning, and
implementing a system of academic, behavioral, social-emotional learning, and mental health support
for all students (California Department of Education, n.d.; Choi, McCart, and Sailor, “Achievement of
Students With IEPs,” 2020; Colorado Department of Education 2017; Drury et al. 2021; Horner and
Halle 2020; McCart and Miller 2020; McIntosh and Goodman 2016; Thurlow et al. 2020). MTSS not
only integrates the service delivery of two existing support models — response-to-intervention (RTI)
for academics and schoolwide positive behavioral intervention and support (PBIS) for behavior —
but it also incorporates social-emotional learning and mental health support service delivery in order
to increase the efficiency and efficacy of support (California Department of Education, n.d.; McCart
and Miller 2020; McIntosh and Goodman 2016). The hallmarks of MTSS include multiple tiers of
student support, high-quality instruction utilizing evidence-based practices, universal screening,
decision guidelines, progress monitoring, collaborative teacher teams, and the use data for continuous
improvement practices to improve implementation (McCart and Miller 2020; McIntosh and Goodman
2016; ESSA, Sec 8101(33) in Thurlow et al. 2020; McMaster and Wagner 2007 in Drury et al. 2021).
MTSS is a prevention-based framework for systematically addressing barriers to learning in pursuit of
improved academic and social outcomes for all learners (Averill and Rinaldi, n.d.; Drury et al. 2021;
Horner and Halle 2020; Leonard, Coyne, Oldham, Burns, and Gillis 2019).

The multiple tiers offer differentiated and increasingly intensive support for academic, behavioral, and
social learning (IRIS Center, n.d.). As McCart and Miller (2020, 4) state “MTSS is a comprehensive
set of academic, behavioral, and social supports that rely on data to mobilize and coordinate diverse
resources for students.”

Why is MTSS Important?
For decades, the public education system in the United States has struggled to effectively serve
students with a diverse set of needs, particularly students with disabilities. Inclusive classrooms — that
is, classrooms in which both students with and without disabilities are served together — are both an
educational requirement and a best practice for serving students with disabilities due to governmental
mandates such as No Child Left Behind (NCLB), the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA),
and the Every Student Succeeds Act (ESSA), as well as evidence-based research showing academic
gains from such classrooms (Alnasser 2020; Brendle, Lock, and Piazza 2017; Cole et al., n.d.; Cramer
et al. 2010; Weiss, Glaser, and Lloyd 2020). Yet, many schools struggle with the school-wide cultural
shift that is required to actualize inclusive education (Sailor 2015).
MTSS is a leading strategy for advancing the school-wide cultural shift to inclusion through efficiently
and equitably allocating resources to meet the needs of all students, including students with disabilities
and traditionally marginalized students. Schools across the United States have embraced MTSS due to
multiple factors:
• The potential for all students to receive timely support for their unique student needs,
including academic, behavioral, and social-emotional needs (Gage et al. 2015; Nelson et al.
2004 in Scott et al. 2019; McIntosh and Goodman 2016; McCart and Miller 2020; Sailor 2015),
particularly those students who typically are not served well by the existing education
system, including students with disabilities and students “who live in poverty, who
experience high mobility, who benefit from an accelerated curriculum, or who struggle to
learn for other reasons” (Choi, McCart, and Sailor, “Reshaping Educational Systems,” 2020, 8).
• The potential to increase the efficiency of teachers
by reducing the time it takes to regularly identify
student needs and prepare individualized support
for those needs (Lane, Oakes, and Menzies 2019
in Choi, McCart, and Sailor, “Reshaping Educational
Systems,” 2020; McIntosh and Goodman 2016; McCart
and Miller 2020; Sailor 2015).
• The ability to use federal and local funds to
implement MTSS activities via the Individuals with
Disabilities Act and the Every Student Succeeds Act
(ESSA, Sec 2103(b)(3)(F) in Thurlow et al. 2020; IDEA
2004 in Drury et al. 2021; Sugai and Simonsen 2012).
Today, all 50 states have adopted some form of MTSS
(Zhang et al. 2023).

Limitations of MTSS
No limitations to the MTSS framework were raised in the literature surveyed, however, multiple
challenges to implementation were mentioned. Those challenges include:
• initiative change fatigue on the part of educators (McIntosh and Goodman 2016);
• lack of MTSS knowledge and training for teachers, administrators, and district staff (Drury et al. 2021);
• difficulties actually using data to inform tiered support (Drury et al. 2021; Leonard et al. 2019);
• lack of access to or funding for tools such as progress monitoring tools and aligned curriculum based
on evidence-based practices (Mason et al. 2019; Minnesota Department of Education 2019);
• limited time for implementation of tiered support (Mason et al. 2019);
• difficulties serving students with the most significant cognitive disabilities (Thurlow et al. 2020); and
• lack of systems, structures, and policies (Drury et al. 2021; Leonard et al. 2019).
Because these challenges to implementation can forestall the adoption of MTSS in schools, the
Necessary Conditions for Implementation section of this literature review examines them more
thoroughly.

Common Implementation Frameworks
The MTSS model is based on a public health approach to intervention (Brown-Chidsey and Bickford 2016
in Drury et al. 2021) and combines three, or sometimes more, tiers of student services:
• Universal Support / Tier 1: high-quality instruction and support available to all students,
• Additional Support / Tier 2: different instruction and support for students who need more in addition
to Universal Support
• Intensified Support / Tier 3: instruction and support provided with variations in intensity (such as
greater frequency and duration) in addition to Universal and Additional Supports
(Averill and Rinaldi, n.d.; Batsche et al. 2005 in Averill and Rinaldi, n.d.; Drury et al. 2021; Horner and
Halle 2020; McIntosh and Goodman 2016; McCart and Miller 2020; Thurlow et al. 2020; National Center
on Response to Intervention 2010 in Thurlow et al. 2020).
To support the appropriate use of services within the tiers, most MTSS models incorporate the elements
of teaming structures, data for decision-making, evidence-based curriculum instruction and support
materials, and continuous improvement processes (McCart and Miller 2020, 10). There are many names
for the different elements used in the research including universal screening, progress monitoring, and
problem-solving models (a term based on RTI practices) (Batsche et al. 2005; Drury et al. 2021; Fuchs
and Fuchs 2006, Gresham 2007 in Averill and Rinaldi, n.d.; Horner and Halle 2020; Hughes and Dexter
2011 in Drury et al. 2021; Leonard et al. 2019; Mason et al. 2019; McIntosh and Goodman 2016).
A commonly used visual depiction of the tiers is a triangle, “representing levels of prevention (primary,
secondary, or tertiary), types of support for students (universal, targeted, or intensive), curricula (core,
strategic, individualized), or tiers of instruction (Tier 1, Tier 2, Tier 3)” (McIntosh and Goodman 2016, 13).
Each layer is additive, meaning that all students receive Universal Support / Tier 1 services, some students
will then receive Universal Support / Tier 1 and Additional Support / Tier 2 services, and few students

receive Universal Support / Tier 1, Additional Support / Tier 2, and Intensified Support / Tier 3 services
(Averill and Rinaldi, n.d.; McCart and Miller 2020; McIntosh and Goodman 2016; Thurlow et al. 2020).
Common misperceptions of the tiers include the following:
• The perception that students need to move through all tiers in succession before receiving the
level of service that they need (Fuchs et al. 2003 in McIntosh and Goodman 2016). Thurlow et al.
(2020, 6) notes that no student should only receive Tier 3 support, as doing so would ignore the
other instruction and support in Tiers 1 and 2.
• The perception that the tiers are only applicable to some domains, such as early literacy and
behavior. In contrast, the tiers can be applied to nearly any topic in education (McIntosh and
Goodman 2016).
• The perception that the tiers are a way to label students. In contrast, the tiers are used to describe
supports that a student needs in the moment; the tiers are not a permanent descriptor of a student
(McCart and Miller 2020; McIntosh and Goodman 2016).
• The perception that MTSS is only for some students, not all students. In contrast, MTSS is
designed to provide services and resources to all students based on their needs (McCart and
Miller 2020).
• The perception that Tier 3 is the “special education” tier. In contrast, this tier is for any student who
needs intensive interventions in a particular domain (Thurlow et al. 2020).

Efficacy of MTSS and for Whom
When viewed in sum, the literature on the effect of MTSS on academic outcomes shows varied results.
Researchers typically attribute the variation in outcomes to the degree of fidelity of implementation
(Coyne et al. 2016; Fuchs, Fuchs, and Malone 2017 in Coyne et al. 2018). Generally, researchers have
found that MTSS, when implemented with fidelity, can have a positive impact on math performance
and mixed results on reading performance (Gersten et al. 2009; Montague and Jitendra 2012 in Choi,
McCart, and Sailor “Achievement of Students With IEPs,” 2020; Choi et al. 2016; Choi, McCart, and
Sailor, “Achievement of Students With IEPs,” 2020; Scott et al. 2019). One study hypothesized that
because the schools in their study had so many existing supports for reading, MTSS’s ability to impact
reading was limited as compared to math (Choi, McCart, and Sailor, “Achievement of Students With
IEPs,” 2020).
Recently, researchers have been able to more precisely measure the fidelity of MTSS implementation
to draw more definitive conclusions on the efficacy of MTSS on academic performance. Scott et al.
(2019) obtained fidelity measurements from 29 elementary, middle, and high schools in the United
States over four years. They found that schools with higher MTSS fidelity in the reading domain
were positively associated with more students at or above proficient in language mechanics and
mathematics, but not in reading. Conversely, higher MTSS fidelity in the math domain was associated
with more students at or above proficient in language mechanics, but not mathematics nor reading.
Additionally, higher MTSS implementation fidelity in the behavioral domain resulted in significantly fewer
suspension events, as compared to matched comparison schools (Scott et al. 2019, 308). As relates
to the Networked Improvement Community’s target population, schools with higher MTSS fidelity in
this study had student demographics of 14% Black, 7% Latinx, and 63% free/reduced lunch status,
though the study did not report on specific outcomes by sub-groups. The study also did not report the
percentage of students with disabilities in the sample (310).

Necessary Conditions for
Implementation
As MTSS is a schoolwide system of support and services for students, much of the research suggested
addressing the implementation challenges and preparing for successful adoption by focusing on
system-wide levers. As a brief review, the implementation challenges raised in the literature include the
following:
• initiative change fatigue on the part of educators leading to a loss of enthusiasm and willingness to
try new educational approaches (McIntosh and Goodman 2016);
• lack of MTSS knowledge and training for teachers, administrators, and district staff, particularly
around data-driven problem-solving (Drury et al. 2021);
• lack of systems, structures, and policies that support MTSS implementation and remove barriers to
implementation (Drury et al. 2021, Leonard et al. 2019);
• difficulties actually using the data that is collected to inform tiered support in a dynamic fashion
(Drury et al. 2021, Leonard et al. 2019);
• lack of access to/funding for tools such as progress monitoring tools, and aligned curriculum
and intervention programs based on evidence-based practices (Mason et al. 2019; Minnesota
Department of Education 2019);
• limited time for implementation of tiered support, particularly Additional Support / Tier 2 and
Intensified Support / Tier 3 (Mason et al. 2019); and
• difficulties serving students with the most significant cognitive disabilities (Thurlow et al. 2020).
Networks in the NIC that focused on improving
MTSS implementation found that they needed to
first find ways to create accessible data systems
that would allow educators to understand and
respond to the needs of all learners. Once that
infrastructure was built, they could focus on
training network and school leaders as well as
educators in the new data tools.
The SWIFT Education Center (2024) identifies
the system-wide levers as the MTSS framework,
administrative leadership, integrated educational
framework, family and community engagement,
and inclusive policy structure and practice. A
synthesized list of system-wide levers found in
the literature are listed on the following page as
necessary conditions.

support

The literature noted that administrators need to build consensus to commit to
implementing MTSS (Batsche et al. 2005 and Castillo et al. 2010 in Averill and
Rinaldi, n.d.); build a shared vision and supportive data-driven problemsolving culture school-wide (Batsche et al. 2005 and Castillo, et al. 2010 in
Averill and Rinaldi, n.d.; Colorado Department of Education 2017); cultivate
their instructional leadership skills in MTSS (Mellard, Prewett, and Deshler
2012 in Drury et al. 2021); foster inclusive decision-making (SWIFT Education
Center 2024); and deliver targeted professional development for staff (SWIFT
Education Center 2024).

Build a schoolwide MTSS
infrastructure

In collaboration with their districts, schools need to build a school-wide MTSS
infrastructure (Batsche et al. 2005 and Castillo, et al. 2010 in Averill and Rinaldi,
n.d.) that includes data collection methods (McIntosh and Goodman 2016); a
variety of collaborative teaming structures across systems (general education,
special education, administration, and school- and district-level support staff)
(Averill and Rinaldi, n.d.; McIntosh and Goodman 2016) to utilize the data for
problem-solving (Gersten et al. 2008; Sugai and Horner 2009 in McIntosh and
Goodman 2016); aligning and organizing the tiered support, including those
for special education services (SWIFT Education Center 2024; Thurlow et al.
2020); incorporating aligned curriculum and intervention programs based
on Universal Design for Learning and other evidence-based practices (SWIFT
Education Center 2024); and building knowledge on continuous improvement
cycles (SWIFT Education Center 2021).

Integrate the
school’s resources
and organizational
structure

Integrating resources and organizational structures includes actions such
as acquiring assessment and intervention programs (Mason et al. 2019;
Minnesota Department of Education 2019), redefining roles for paraeducators
and specialists (SWIFT Education Center 2024); hiring and training MTSS
coaches (Coyne et al. 2018); ensuring every student has a primary general
education classroom placement (SWIFT Education Center 2024); scheduling
appropriate time in the school week for data analysis and problem-solving,
planning, and implementing tiered support (Mason et al. 2019); ensuring the use
of language and practices that are based on service needs as opposed to
disability labels (SWIFT Education Center 2024) and creating a positive school
culture where all students have access to extracurricular activities and all staff are
contributing to student outcomes (SWIFT Education Center 2024).

Include families
and community
members as
partners

MTSS is a whole school transformation that requires the partnership of families and
community members. As part of the MTSS adoption process, schools and districts
should engage authentically with families for input and feedback (SWIFT
Education Center 2024), provide opportunities for families and community
members to participate in school and the education of the students (Fan and
Chen 2001 and Jeynes 2012 in Gross, Choi, and Francis 2018; SWIFT Education
Center 2024) and provide families with information needed to support their
students (Fan and Chen 2001 and Jeynes 2012 in Gross, Choi, and Francis 2018;
SWIFT Education Center 2024).

Evaluate and
adapt district
policies and
practices for MTSS

A variety of district changes are needed to support MTSS adoption and
implementation. Those changes include recalibrating district office roles
to cross-functionally support MTSS implementation (Averill and Rinaldi, n.d.),
building knowledge of district staff (including the superintendent) in MTSS
(Drury et al. 2021; SWIFT Education Center 2024), incorporating opportunities for
district staff to attend school-level meetings (SWIFT Education Center 2024),
building a regular process for reviewing and revising policies that impact
MTSS implementation (SWIFT Education Center 2024), and removing barriers to
implementing MTSS (SWIFT Education Center 2024).

Schools and districts can use tools such as the Schoolwide Integrated Framework for Transformation
Fidelity of Implementation Tool (Pollitt et al. 2019) or the Academic and Behavior Response to
Intervention School Assessment (ASA) fidelity tool (Scott et al. 2019) to track their MTSS adoption
progress.
Further, the literature noted that the order in which schools and districts implement the above
system changes matters for successful MTSS adoption. A study by Choi et al. (2018, 10) found that
administrative leadership played a critical role in school transformation and that “meaningful schoolwide change might not occur without the mediation of high-quality school leadership.” This suggests
that schools and districts in early stages of adoption focus on first building administrative leadership in
order to propel the other system changes.

Conclusion
In conclusion, MTSS is truly a school-wide approach to meeting students’ needs. When implemented
with fidelity, MTSS can be an effective approach to meeting all students’ needs, not just students with
disabilities. For successful implementation, schools and districts must understand they are committing
to a whole-school transformation and subsequently invest significant time to continuously align systemwide resources, professional development, policies, structures, and systems.
Schools implementing MTSS with high fidelity have seen strong gains in math and limited gains in
reading for all students, in addition to reduced suspensions. For staff, high-fidelity implementation
provides organizational clarity on educational practices and policies; accessible data systems;
increased whole-school collaboration; and more sustainable, efficient, and effective implementation
of student services. In short, MTSS is a framework for meeting student needs that, if implemented
with high fidelity, has the potential to improve academic and behavioral outcomes for Black and Latinx
students with disabilities experiencing poverty.
Best practices for implementing MTSS are discussed in Navigating Data for Multi-Tiered Systems of
Support: Equity-based practices for using data at the district, school, and classroom levels.

Why are Site-Based Data Teams Important?
Structured meetings that focus on analyzing student data are necessary for educators to problem-solve
how to best ensure all students receive the education they need. In many cases, schools already have
student study team (SST) structures in place to address the needs of at-risk students through a multitiered system of supports (MTSS). These types of intervention team meetings help teachers implement
high-quality instruction that focuses on the needs of all students, particularly Black and Latinx students
with disabilities experiencing poverty. This high-quality instruction can reduce the need for more
intensive intervention, allowing students with disabilities to remain in the general education classroom
as much as possible.
Site-based data teams use a similar approach, but they look at data using different lenses, which are
determined by the team’s purpose.

Why it Works
Site-based teams directly impact student learning experiences by ensuring that instruction is datadriven and based on individual student needs. They also work indirectly by strengthening educators’
knowledge and capacity to address these needs. Teams of teachers collaborating create opportunities
for peer learning among teachers to build collective expertise.1 In particular, the diverse experience and
knowledge of each member can be transferred to others as team members work together to address
challenges. Teams that also adopt and implement a data-driven decision-making process can further
increase teacher and school capacity and improve student achievement.2
Increasing teacher capacity through site-based teams is particularly important for students of color and
students with disabilities who are more likely to be placed with the least skilled teachers.3 Site-based
teams can help less experienced teachers gain important pedagogical knowledge that will help them
educate all learners.
Furthermore, site-based teams aid both newer and veteran teachers to grow their knowledge and
capacity for making data-driven decisions about student needs and to reflect on their own practices. As
a result, all students receive more targeted instruction, fewer students need additional intervention, and
more students with disabilities can be educated within the general education classroom.4

Bringing this Practice to Life
GRADE-LEVEL TEAMS USE DATA TO DESIGN
ACCELERATION OPPORTUNITIES
It can be challenging in the secondary context to find opportunities to intervene and re-teach content to small
groups of students. To meet the challenge, Summit Atlas created a weekly student support block dedicated
to targeted instructional grouping to ensure students gain skills they have not yet mastered in class. To use
this time most effectively, Summit Atlas staff need to use data to target the courses, content, and skills that
will benefit students the most.

Understanding the Challenge
During the period of remote learning caused by the COVID-19 pandemic, Summit Atlas struggled
to help students not yet mastering cognitive skills, a set of learning outcomes for Summit Public
Schools graduates. At the heart of the struggle was the difficulty of providing “just-in-time” support
to the students experiencing barriers with remote learning. Atlas’s teachers had trouble knowing
what the students’ needs were and finding time in the daily schedule to address those needs. To
make that time available, Atlas created a block each week (based on a structure developed at the

Summit Public Schools network-level) during which students could get more targeted support on

Transitioning from Virtual to In-Person Norms
During COVID virtual school, solving for “where” acceleration blocks would meet proved relatively
easy. Staff sent students Zoom links that students could use to meet, with little delay, the teachers
they needed support from. However, when Summit Atlas returned to an in-person school setting, it
became more challenging to solve for “where.”
To tackle this issue, Summit Atlas Director of Learning Experience Kristina Brawley and Summit
Atlas Principal Dan Effland reflected on their success in virtual school and wondered if they
could apply a similar approach in the school building. In the physical space, they would need to
communicate to students further in advance because the rooms for acceleration blocks would
change, and unlike virtual school, a simple click would not lead them to the correct teacher.
Additionally, as with any changes to physical space, school culture needed to be of foremost
concern for implementation. While Kristina proposed changing class lists for acceleration blocks on
a daily basis to maximize the “just-in-time” learning, Dan suggested making these changes once
every two weeks to minimize disruptions that could negatively impact school culture. Ultimately,
they agreed to start with once-a-week roster changes for acceleration blocks, in an effort to both
rapidly meet student needs and minimize disruptions to school culture.

Building a Tool to Support Rostering
To facilitate the grade-level team members’ rostering of students into classes based on data,
Kristina connected with Linh Hoang, data lead at Summit Public Schools, for help in building a
scheduling support tool. Kristina and Linh began meeting on a weekly basis to clarify the tool’s
requirements and how teachers would need to use it. Kristina communicated specifically what data
she needed to see, why she needed to see it, and how it would help to improve student outcomes
and efficiencies for teachers. Following their meetings, Linh iterated on the scheduling tool to
support those exact needs. In time, the scheduling tool developed into an early version of the
acceleration block planning tool that became a lynchpin of the grade-level team meeting.

IMPROVING THE PRACTICE BY BUILDING DATA ANALYSIS SKILLS
While supporting grade-level teams in rostering students weekly, Dan and Kristina noticed that
teachers were strong in considering the “who” of their acceleration blocks, but they were not yet as
strong in the “what” and “how” aspects of the steps needed to plan blocks. Their teachers needed
more support in analyzing student progress data to understand student needs and how to respond
to them.
Inspiration for building data analysis skills in teachers struck after Dan participated in a networkwide data protocol during a meeting of all Summit principals. At the meeting, the principals used a
protocol that involved analyzing data, setting a goal for a priority metric, identifying actions to move
toward that goal, and receiving feedback from peer principals. Dan recognized this protocol was
a modified version of a Plan-Do-Study-Act cycle to meet stated goals and could also benefit his
teachers’ development of their data analysis skills.
After the principals’ meeting, Dan suggested creating a grade-level team meeting protocol: first

School leaders and leadership teams should have a clear vision for why a team of educators is meeting.
Various site-based teams will have different purposes based on the group of students they are focusing

Step 4: Develop Meeting Resources
PROTOCOL
Draft a team data protocol aligned to the purpose and vision of the team that allows for a one-hour
meeting. The protocol needs to ensure that all team members do at least the following:
1. Review relevant data. Look for patterns, trends, and outliers.
2. Pay special attention to subgroups and equity.
3. Make sense of the data. Try to understand why the data look the way they do.
4. Plan for how to respond to the data, answering the questions:
– What will be done?
– Which students need support?
– Who will take the action?
– Where and when will this happen?
– How will you know if it worked?
5. Give and get feedback from another teammate or the whole team.
The protocol should take into account the amount of support team members will need with data analysis
and problem-solving, both individually and as a group. Some teams might develop a protocol in which
teachers make sense of their data individually and then come together as a group for problem-solving.

ACTION STEPS BANK
School leaders will need to create a resource of different action steps or interventions that team
members can choose from when developing a plan for how to respond to their data. Action steps
should be feasible, timely, and in the teacher or team’s locus of control. It is a good idea to start a
basic prepopulated list based on research. The team can expand the list over time as they brainstorm
new ideas during team meetings. Teams can also add to their bank of action steps information on how
students have responded to the actions or how easy they were to implement with fidelity. Over time,
team members will see how different interventions work for different students and for the varied needs
of those students, giving them the self-efficacy and empowerment to keep trying new options when
prior strategies have not worked.

Step 9: Enact Plans and Follow Up
The most difficult step in the process for site-based teams is implementing the action steps determined
in the meeting. This step requires team members to enact plans with fidelity and quality so that the
data eventually shows improvement as a result. Furthermore, some plans may not work, for a variety
of reasons, forcing the team to reconsider the chosen actions. And while the team needs to hold its
members accountable for enacting the plan, there should be a considerable amount of grace given
when a plan does not work out. Providing this courtesy helps all members feel like they can continue
to innovate and persevere. A certain amount of failure has to be tolerated — even celebrated — before
trying the next possible strategy for responding to data.

COLLEGIATE LEADER DATA MEETINGS
How can a school foster collaboration between special and general educators to enhance learning
outcomes for students with disabilities?
One promising approach is an emerging practice called “data teaming.” Data teaming involves regular
meetings among school stakeholders to collectively analyze student needs and develop intervention
strategies. This collaborative process can effectively break down silos and create a more inclusive and
effective learning environment for all students.
During the 2022–23 school year, Rosenwald Collegiate Academy (RCA), a Collegiate Academies public
charter high school in New Orleans, implemented data teaming after noticing a troubling trend: Special
education teachers weren’t seeing the impact of specialized instruction without teaming and alignment from
general education teachers, while general education teachers struggled to provide differentiation and reteaching to support students with disabilities. As a result, both general education students and students with
disabilities were struggling to achieve content mastery.

The Journey
Sonia Scott, the director of special education, and Samara Levy, the director of academics, had
been seeking new ways to improve content mastery for their students with disabilities.
Although RCA had implemented co-teaching—a collaborative teaching structure aimed at
enhancing teachers’ ability to provide coordinated and individualized student support—it did not
produce the desired learning outcomes the school had anticipated, especially for students with

disabilities. Sonia and Samara noticed that co-teachers tended to stick to a single co-teaching

students’ needs. Moreover, they observed that communication between general education and
special education teachers was challenging, partly due to conflicting meeting times. Although both
groups met separately with Sonia and Samara on a weekly basis, they did not have consistent joint
meetings. This lack of collaboration compounded the issues they faced.
After reflecting on the school’s co-teaching history and efforts, Sonia and Samara formulated a new

ESTABLISHING EFFECTIVE DATA ROUTINES IN SITE-BASED TEAMS

Summary
Site-based teams have the potential to provide proactive support to educator teams,
programs, or entire schools by using data to identify issues and develop action
steps. Teams should be intentional about the data and action steps they take to
ease the burden for others and function as a support system based on the level of
needs identified in the data. This can help ensure that students, especially those with
disabilities who are Latinx, Black, or living in poverty, are able to receive the appropriate
amount of academic support to make progress. To do this, formal structures and strong
data that support decision-making are essential for the team’s activities to be effective
and centered on improving outcomes for students. Including diverse team members
in these processes helps educational systems stay aligned in their school culture and
actions to improve outcomes for all students. When diverse teams work together with
the same purpose, they can strategically use their collective expertise to best support
student learning and outcomes.

Practice Overview
What is the Practice?
Student conferencing is a regular, consistent time during school days for educators to coach students 1:1
on how to set goals, make plans to achieve their goals, enact their plans, and reflect on their progress.

Why is Student
Conferencing Important?
Many Black and Latinx students with disabilities
experiencing poverty are subjected to school
cultures with low expectations, are not given
autonomy to direct their own learning, and are
not given opportunities to engage in deeper
learning.1 Student conferencing is a structure
that helps interrupt the negative cycle of low
expectations. It builds students’ skills for success
by providing a time for students to explicitly learn
habits through 1:1 reflections with educators.

Why it Works
David Yeager, Carol Dwek, Angela Duckworth, and many other researchers have demonstrated the
importance of habits and skills such as grit, perseverance, and agency for student success in school
and life. There is a strong body of evidence that underpins student conferencing as a structure that
works to build these critical habits and skills for success:
SENSE OF BELONGING AND DEEPER RELATIONSHIPS
The regular 1:1 interaction between students and teachers during student conferencing helps students
build positive relationships with adults in the school building. When students have strong relationships
with adults, as well as with other students in their school, they can feel a sense of belonging that can
translate into increased engagement and, eventually, academic success.2
GOAL SETTING
Goal setting is associated with increased
perseverance, higher aspirations, and positive
homework behavior, as well as some improved
outcomes for the lowest-performing students.3 By
explicitly teaching students how to self-monitor
grades, identify achievable goals, and reflect
on progress, students can be empowered to
drive their educational futures forward. Student
conferencing gives students the opportunity to
practice identifying their goals and to learn from
their educators how best to achieve them.
SELF-DETERMINATION

Teaching and Using
SMART Goals
At KIPP Northern California, special
education case managers begin their
conferencing work by teaching students
how to create their own SMART (Specific,
Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, and
Time-Bound) goals. By focusing first on
SMART goal setting, case managers can
easily direct future check-ins based on those
goals. Clear data on student goal attainment
also helps case managers consider what
steps are most effective in helping students
reach their goals.

Students build self-determination — the initiative
to plan, implement, and evaluate their own
learning needs and outcomes, with or without
the help of others4 — by developing a host of skills that are generally not explicitly taught to students
outside of conferencing.
Some of the skills include:
• understanding how assignment completion impacts a final grade, including how some assignments
are worth more than others;
• creating a goal to address the high-leverage assignments to get the desired grade;
• making a plan for how to study more effectively for a test;
• connecting learning to personal goals;
• advocating to teachers when extra time or additional help is needed; and
• regulating emotions in response to setbacks.

EXECUTIVE FUNCTIONING
Students with disabilities, especially those
who are Latinx, Black, and living in poverty,
particularly benefit from this practice because
many disabilities affect executive functioning
skills and thus require explicit instruction to
increase these skills.5

Summit’s Mentorship
Structure
Nearly two decades ago, Summit Public
Schools pioneered weekly 1:1s for every
individual student. Over time, Summit
has evolved the structure, content, and
process for these meetings to better meet
all students’ needs. Through long-term
mentoring relationships, teacher-mentors
become the students’ advocates, inside and
outside of the classroom. Mentors provide
guidance, support, and skill-building so
that, eventually, the leadership of weekly
meetings shifts to students. Mentors also
come together to meet and discuss individual
student’s needs in a structure called Kid Talk.
Summit’s teacher-mentors use their time with
students to address important habits and
skills, such as the following:6
• Challenge seeking: students pushing
themselves to achieve higher goals
• Persistence: continuing to work toward
a goal despite challenges or setbacks
• Strategy shifting: finding alternative
ways to accomplish a goal when their
original strategy does not work
• Appropriate help-seeking: reaching
out to others for support when they hit a
roadblock they cannot overcome alone
• Response to setbacks: regulating
their emotions in response to barriers
or negative outcomes when trying to
achieve their goals.

Bringing this Practice to Life
ACCELERATING LEARNING WITH STUDENT CONFERENCES
AT STRIVE PREP
STRIVE Prep Federal7 in Denver, Colorado wanted their students to take ownership over their short- and
long-term goals, grades, and study habits. Teachers there realized that these self-determination skills
needed to be taught explicitly for students with disabilities. To do that, STRIVE Prep Federal introduced
student conferencing structures. The team piloted student conferencing in one special education math class,
and encouraged by the pilot’s success helping students pass classes, they have since spread the practice
to multiple classrooms across grade levels. The scene described below shows a typical conference between
one student and her teacher.

Starting a Conference
Jolisa and her math teacher, Mr. Miller, get settled and organized across from each other at a table
with their laptops. Mr. Miller helps Jolisa find the right bookmark and pull up her student portal,
while he also pulls out a paper form to start their check-in.
Mr. Miller: How is your week going, Jolisa?
Jolisa: I’m yellow. [points to “yellow” on her check-in sheet]
Mr. Miller: Why are you a yellow?
Jolisa: I’m tired from staying up too late.
Mr. Miller: How do you think being tired impacts you at school? I love how much energy
you bring to my math class, and I’d love to see you be able to give the same energy to all
your classes. How can we find some ways to help you get enough sleep each night?

This initial interaction between Mr. Miller and Jolisa starts their check-in with an opportunity to build
their relationship. Mr. Miller shows Jolisa that he cares about her well-being and sees the positives
in her even when she isn’t feeling like she’s at her best.

Reflecting Using Data
From there, Mr. Miller asks Jolisa questions about her academic successes and challenges
from the last week. Her reflections naturally lead them into a conversation about Jolisa’s current
grades. Mr. Miller guides Jolisa through the process of looking for patterns in her grades. When he
sees some troubling grades in her math and English classes, Mr. Miller asks Jolisa to look at her
assignments in those classes. Jolisa acknowledges that despite turning in all the assignments, she
didn’t complete them all and doesn’t feel confident that what she turned in was correct.
Mr. Miller then spends time asking about specific, low-scoring assignments, which allows him to
work with Jolisa on regulating her negative emotions around her struggles with some assignments.
He reminds her of her past successes and tries to redirect her. Mr. Miller also explains to Jolisa that
it is helpful to look at her grades in classes to see how she did on assignments because she can
usually meet with her teachers to revise her work and get higher grades.

Planning for the Future
Key to this redirection is focusing on Jolisa’s long-term goals of going to college and studying art.
Mr. Miller reminds Jolisa that passing her classes is the way to achieve those goals. He is then able
to help Jolisa make a plan of action for how she can revise and then resubmit high-quality work.
The two create a new goal for Jolisa to revise one assignment that she needs to resubmit by their
next check-in. Mr. Miller asks Jolisa to restate her goal and then to write it on a sticky note to put on
the class’s public goal board.

Outcomes from Conferencing
The entire student conference takes Mr.
Miller about 13 minutes. These steps help
develop a culture of care, accountability,
and reflection that are reinforced every two
weeks during student conferencing.
The initiative has already benefited
students. All of the students who had an IEP
and met with Mr. Miller for student conferencing passed all of their classes. When Mr. Miller spread
student conferencing to his advisory class, 80% of students who had an IEP and met with him
for student conferencing increased their GPA that semester. They did that by using self-advocacy
skills, by learning routines for completing tasks, and by learning how to transfer good work habits
from classes they liked to use in classes they did not enjoy. Improving their grades was just the
beginning for Mr. Miller’s students, who now also feel valued, empowered, and motivated to reach
for their goals in life.

How to Implement this Practice
This section provides step-by-step guidance for implementing student conferencing, along with
accompanying resources to get started. The steps are organized into two sections for implementation:
Getting Started and Holding Student Conferences.

Getting Started
When school leaders set up school-wide systems and supports for student conferencing, the
practice thrives and student outcomes improve. Below are various system elements school leaders
can implement prior to the start of the school year to enable successful student conferencing.

Step 1: Use Data to Identify Students Who Need
Student Conferencing
While conferencing can be structured as a system-wide universal intervention, it can also be done
to provide targeted or more intensive support to a subset of students who need additional support
in building social-emotional skills, study skills, or relationships. School-based teams can use data to
identify a select number of students who need student conferencing the most or to determine whether
an entire caseload or class should receive conferencing as a universal intervention. (See Establishing
Effective Data Routines in School-Based Teams.)

Implementing
Conferencing with
Less Structure
KIPP Northern California began
implementing student conferencing with three
special education case managers. Each
case manager first conferenced with only
one student who they knew would be willing
to participate for several months, and later
added to their conferencing schedule two
additional students who had a greater need
for this time. These conferences had to be
slotted into the weekly schedule wherever
possible because the case managers did not
necessarily have the students they managed
in their advisory periods. The challenge
for case managers was to ensure they still
prioritized student conferences amid the
multitude of other tasks they were responsible
for in a week.
KIPP’s initial pilot was able to prove the value
and effectiveness of student conferencing to
the school leader, who created two dedicated
conferencing periods for special educators
the following year. By making this shift to
teacher’s schedules, the school was able to
offer biweekly conferences to more than
twice as many students.

Keeping Conferences
Meaningful While Scaling
STRIVE Prep initially implemented student
conferencing in a math class for students
with disabilities. The special educator was
able to use classroom stations during normal
class periods to conference with students.
As STRIVE Prep looked to scale its student
conferencing practice to more classrooms,
they found a way to do student conferencing
during shorter advisory periods. The
implementation meant conferences went from
12 or 13 minutes to less than 10 minutes,
but they accomplished the same purpose of
helping students set and meet goals.

responsibility for conferences and for decisions made during conferences. It may also be helpful to
have slightly different protocols based on the time of year:

By the end of the school year, some students may be able to run conferences themselves.
Educators can use various gradual release strategies to support this level of student agency. Some
strategies include:
• asking students to check and document their grades before their check-in,
• providing students a script for leading each section of the check-in,
• having students calculate their progress toward their goal, and
• asking students to write their own SMART goals and give feedback during check-ins.
Because conference meetings are very short, staying on schedule may be the biggest implementation

• Teachers gave conference pre-work to students, prompting students to review previous goals and

to conferences ready to share.

Step 4: Establish a Communication System
for Collaborating Teachers
It is important that collaborating teachers can easily document and share student goals, plans, and
progress. This information aids teacher follow-up with students and communicates the current status
with other staff. A shared spreadsheet can allow staff members convenient access to conference
documentation. School leaders can use existing structures to create a time and place for sharing
information from conferencing, such as grade-level meetings, co-planning sessions, or schoolwide
“kid talk” sessions.

Step 5: Embed Student Conferencing Supports
in Coaching Structures
Collaborating teachers benefit from coaching support and professional development focused
specifically on student conferencing. Examples of this might include the following:
• Observations of student conferencing: Ideally, instructional coaches observe student
conferencing so educators can receive constructive feedback on how to improve conferences.
• Training on school grade reporting system: By giving teachers training on using the school
grade reporting system, particularly from the student perspective, teachers are then equipped to
help students navigate the systems.
• Professional development on teaching skills: Through school-wide professional development,
teachers can gain skills to build relationships with their students, coach students on habits of
success, and help students manage setbacks.

Holding Student Conferences
Step 6: Students
Complete Pre-Work
Student conference meetings are very short,
typically 10–15 minutes. Completing pre-work
before the conference ensures the best use of that
10–15 minutes. For example, students can look
up grades or other data related to their goals and
begin filling out information on the conference
form. This pre-work can be assigned as either
homework or classwork.

Differentiating
Conference Structures
At STRIVE Prep, some students
complete their grade reflections
independently, ahead of the 1:1, once
they’ve shown that they know how to
independently review and reflect on
current grades. This practice can also
save time in cases where a teacher
needs to shorten the conference length
for some students.

Step 7: Educators Create
Independent or Partner
Work for All Students
In order to be free to conduct 1:1 conferences during class, teachers must create independent or
partner activities for all students to work on during conferencing. Additionally, teachers will need to
spend sufficient time setting expectations for students during independent work.

Step 8: Educators Set Up Conferencing Space
Because teachers conduct 1:1 conferences in the same classroom as the rest of the students, a
dedicated space for conferencing will be needed. Teachers should set up a conferencing space in
the classroom such that they can see the rest of the students while maintaining some privacy for the
student in the 1:1 conference.

Step 9: Students and Educators Hold Student Conferences
Though students need different amounts of support and time in each part of their check-in, 1:1s
typically will cover the following steps:

SAMPLE CONFERENCE AGENDA
Relational Check-in (~3 min.)
• Ask the student how they’re doing.
• Ask a follow-up question for more detail, to clarify, or to ensure they feel heard.
• Ask for a “win” the student has experienced since the last conference.
Goal Setting (~2 min.)
• Pull up the student’s goals from the prior meeting, along with data on whether or not
they met their goals.
• Ask the student what the data tells them about their progress. Help them understand
whether it shows that they got closer or further from their goal.
• Ask students which classes they want to focus on this week and why, based on their
progress, they have selected those classes.
Plan (~2 min.)
• Ask the student to consider barriers to reaching the SMART goal they just created.
Gut check whether there is a “root cause” that is going unaddressed that could inhibit
the student’s progress toward their goal.
• Help the student modify their plan based on both where they are focusing their
energy this cycle and their reflection on prior barriers. Make sure the student can
give their rationale for modifications. For example:
– “I will go to the teacher’s office hours two days a week because I’m running into
too many questions that I need the teacher’s help to understand.”
– “My ‘plan B’ for when my parents can’t give me a ride to school is to text my
neighbor by 7:30 a.m. to get a ride with them instead.”
• Ask the student if there is anything they need from you or other staff members at the
school to help them be successful in acting on their plan.
Accountability and Next Steps (~5 min.)
• Have the student set reminders, identify how they want to communicate with other
teachers or do follow-up, and publicly post their goal (to support accountability,
transparency, and relatability across students).
• Set a date for the next check-in and any intermediate steps. Provide recommendations
based on knowledge of schedule, upcoming deadlines, and the like.

Step 10: Students
Implement Their Plans
After the meeting, have students fill in any data
that will be public, whether in a shared document
or on a poster in class. Additionally, students will
be responsible for implementing the plan they
created during the conference.
There will likely be more times than not that
students struggle to enact their plans. A host
of barriers both within and outside of school
can impede a students’ ability to do what
they said they would. Educators can use

BUILDING MEANINGFUL STUDENT CONFERENCES AT KIPP PUBLIC
SCHOOLS NORTHERN CALIFORNIA
KIPP Navigate College Prep’s special educators built relationships with students while teaching them
essential skills through biweekly student conferences. The team built their conferencing program gradually
to ensure that time spent in conferences benefitted students as much as possible. Whether writing SMART
goals or advocating their needs to teachers, students who participated in student conferencing demonstrated
that KIPP Navigate created systems and structures that can benefit all students.

The special education team at KIPP Navigate noticed a trend that was negatively impacting
student performance. Students, particularly students with disabilities, were waiting until the end of
a semester to take action on missing work that was preventing them from passing courses. The
end-of-semester rush made it difficult for students failing multiple classes to catch up. Students
needed to learn the skills to help them stay on track in their classes all semester through regular
touchpoints with their special educators.
To address this challenge, the special educators wanted time to meet personally with students to
discuss their grades and to help students devise action steps to improve those grades. Holding
biweekly student conferences during advisory seemed like a feasible way for the school’s special
educators to have the types of interactions with students that they felt could have a real impact on
their attendance and grades.

Yet KIPP Navigate special educators had a logistical barrier: special educators each taught an

WHAT IS THE PRACTICE?
An interdisciplinary, cross-functional team that meets regularly with a clear purpose,
defined membership, and established routine to review data in order to identify strengths
and needs and problem-solve to take timely action to intervene for all students.

WHY IS IT IMPORTANT?
• School systems and teams are often designed to operate in silos, which limits effective
collaboration and ultimately hinders positive impacts on students. This is true at the
school level and also at the network level for many school systems across the country.
• Intentional structures are needed to break down silos and create opportunities to
analyze data across teams for the purpose of allocating resources and support where
needed.
• Network teams require a clear purpose, process, and next steps that align with existing
ways of working and organizational goals.
• This type of collaboration can reinforce a sense of shared responsibility for all students.
Students can be more fully supported in general education classrooms and achieve

How Green Dot Implemented the Practice
Over time, the Green Dot team developed a process to implement the data-to-action protocol, which they
iterate upon each year as new members join the team, priorities evolve, and schedules change. Green
Dot’s Ed Management team follows clear steps to ensure they are addressing needs across the network:

Step 1: Define the team’s purpose.
Step 2: Determine the team members.
• Make sure team members understand the purpose of the meeting.
• Define roles and responsibilities for team members.
Step 3: Create a meeting schedule.
• Reduce all possible conflicts.
• Reinforce the importance of the meeting by protecting the time on calendars.
Step 4: Create the protocol.
• Draft a team data protocol aligned to the purpose and vision of the teaming structure and routine.
– The protocol should allow for data analysis, identifying trends, setting a goal, creating an action plan,
and gaining consensus from the team.
– The protocol should align all team members on the goal of each meeting so that if teams are broken up
to focus on different data, topics, or even students, each group’s action plan meets the common goal.
• Gain consensus on the data protocol and questions used in the protocol.
Step 5: Identify the data to analyze.
• Determine what data is most applicable to the team’s purpose and goals.
• Data must be aligned with school and network priorities in order for it to be as useful as possible.
• Misaligned data will, over time, erode buy-in of team members.
Step 6: Train team members on data analysis tools and methods.
• Provide a specific step-by-step process and training on data tools that will be used by the team.
• Give examples of how to interpret the types of data that the team will look at regularly.
• Ensure understanding of how to analyze disaggregated data by subgroups based on the purpose and
goals of the team.
Step 7: Create a bank of potential action steps that can be taken.
• This can be built by the team over time and/or prepopulated with interventions.
• Action steps should be feasible, within the team’s locus of control, and timely.
Step 8: Hold data meetings.

Green Dot’s Ed Management team found success in meeting regularly and learning how to use the
protocol they had devised. Over time, the team learned important lessons about how to make these
meetings more impactful:
• They needed action plans that they could drive to solve problems for schools.
• Their solutions should not create more work for their school teams.
• They had to be very targeted in the data they looked at and keep it consistent over time. This led to
the eventual reduction in the amount of data analyzed.
• The data they looked at had to be similar to the data that individual schools were focused on
improving.
• They needed time twice a year to step back and reflect on how effectively they were working together.

The Impact
Use of the network-level data-to-action protocol
has built skills and capacity.
After about a year and a half of implementing and iterating upon their meetings, one of the biggest
improvements that the Ed Management team made was including data questions and a common data
routine during every meeting. Even if the team’s focus shifted to different data, their data analysis
skills and routines were still relevant. The team leaders started using the data-for-equity protocol in
department-level meetings to help explore more department-specific equity gaps in the data.
The team continues to meet once a month and now focuses on staff capacity data and how it relates
to professional development. The team is doing a book study and analyzing professional development
exit slip data to answer specific data questions. Because the Ed Management team is responsible
for much of the network-wide professional development, the team no longer needs to worry about
who is responsible for implementing interventions and can respond quickly to data. Green Dot plans
to triangulate student outcomes data and observational data in order to drive the focus of future
professional development.

Engaging Families after COVID-19
As students began returning to in-person learning from the COVID-19 pandemic, two of STRIVE

rates. After careful reflection, they agreed that the solution should consider several key factors:
• Families were not logging into the network’s parent portal that showed them their students’
grades and assignments.
• Sixty percent of students with disabilities were failing at least one class.
• Families needed communication available in both English and Spanish.
• Families indicated that text messaging was their preferred method of communication.
Jen and Jose decided that text message communication was the ideal family engagement strategy.

To ensure that all families had access to this critical information, the system would send messages
in English and Spanish biweekly. ThruText would also prompt families to check students’ grades on
the parent portal and encourage them to engage in back-and-forth communication with staff. They
hoped this strategy would get every family involved and aligned with the school so families could
talk to their students about their education. These conversations would allow families and students

Expanding Parental Engagement to Benefit All Students
As they expanded the text messaging strategy to all students in the STRIVE Prep network, Jen and
Jose created a semester-long plan of all text messages that would go out to families, taking into
account holidays and other important dates to reduce the burden of sending text messages biweekly.
Importantly, they also decided to hire three “family ambassadors,” family members of students at a
STRIVE Prep school who would send and monitor text messages on a part-time basis. These family
ambassadors would be integral to the text messaging process, particularly in communicating with
families.
Each school principal created a video introduction to ThruText to share with families. The first text
message families received included the introductory video and expectations for future text messages
every other Thursday. Parents could opt out of the text messaging service, but no parents chose to
do so.
Once this introduction to the text messaging service happened and the process was fully active, Jose
routinely updated student information in ThruText every Thursday using a spreadsheet of grade data
pulled from the student information system.

Family ambassadors could then send biweekly text messages to inform families about students’

TIPS FOR SELECTING A TEXT MESSAGING PLATFORM
As STRIVE Prep’s team considered various platforms they might adopt for this pilot, they needed to
balance budget considerations between platform cost and time requirements for processing data. In the
end, they chose ThruText because it was a less expensive platform than others they considered. Yet, this decision
required considerable data management on the part of the STRIVE Prep team members, especially Jose, because it
did not integrate with their student information systems.
When determining which platform is best for a family text messaging system, consider the following:
• Cost of the platform
• Integration of the platform with existing student information systems
• Skill level of team members to run reports and merge content between systems
• Capacity of team members to track engagement and response data
• Training requirements for staff members and families to navigate the system

Barriers to Solve
Even after the text messaging strategy was fully implemented, some barriers remained for the team to
solve. One challenge was the limited access family ambassadors had to students’ information beyond
ThruText. Jen and Jose made sure that family ambassadors had the information that needed to be input
into text messages, but without full access to the data systems, family ambassadors could not update
information from families into the student information system nor retrieve details they needed about
students to respond to follow up text messages from families. In the end, the limited access family
ambassadors had to student data meant most data responsibilities lay with Jose. This both limited the
impact the family ambassadors could have on family engagement with the text messaging system and
put time burdens on Jose to complete data input and address follow-up requests on specific students.
The team also ran into a logistical issue where they could only match one student per phone number,
making it impossible to send text messages for two or more siblings at a STRIVE Prep school without
applying special assignment rules. For example, in cases where there were two students in the
household and two phone numbers for parents or guardians, the team could assign one student to
each of the numbers; this did not work, however, if there were more than two students attending the
school from the same household. Where the number of students from a single household exceeded the

Introduction
When existing data tools do not meet current needs and a school or network chooses to design a
custom data tool, it is important to invest the necessary time and effort to ensure the tool is useful,
usable, and utilized. Using Excel, Google Sheets, Tableau, or another data visualization tool, teams
can customize a dashboard to import and manipulate data to suit the measurement effort. To do this
successfully, school systems typically need an experienced data team with the knowledge and capacity
to implement these technical needs.
The iterative process necessary to develop an effective data tool can be time-intensive. The following
steps may be helpful when planning for this process.

Design and Develop
First, during the design stage, identify a clear purpose for the data dashboard and define core priorities.
The purpose and priorities should connect to student outcomes (e.g., achievement, GPA, attendance,
or social-emotional skills) aligned with the school’s mission.
The following questions are important to answer
during this phase:
WHO ARE THE PRIMARY USERS? (e.g., teachers,
grade-level teams, school leaders, or central office)
AT WHAT LEVEL SHOULD EDUCATORS ANALYZE
DATA? (e.g., individual students, subgroups, class
periods, courses, teacher, or school)
WHAT WILL EDUCATORS NEED TO BE ABLE TO
DO WITH THIS DATA? (e.g., reflect on a lesson,
reflect on a unit, or identify gaps between subgroups)
HOW MUCH TECHNICAL ABILITY DO
EDUCATORS NEED TO DEVELOP TO USE THE
TOOL EFFECTIVELY?
HOW WILL DATA BE INPUT OR DOWNLOADED
INTO THE TOOL, AND WHO WILL MANAGE THAT
PROCESS?
HOW MUCH TIME WILL EDUCATORS NEED TO
DEVOTE TO THE TOOL OUTSIDE OF MEETINGS?
HOW OFTEN WILL EDUCATORS ACCESS THE
DASHBOARD? (e.g., daily, weekly, biweekly, or
monthly)

Building a Dashboard
at Collegiate
Collegiate Academies found,
early on, that their general
student information system (SIS)
did not allow teachers to view
student mastery of standards by
the types of filters they needed
(e.g., grade levels, class periods,
or subgroups) to evaluate and
respond appropriately to the
data in order to reteach skills
students hadn’t mastered. For
that reason, Collegiate opted to
create their own tool for looking at
students’ progress on standards
and subsequently developed a
spreadsheet dashboard that let
teachers easily see how students
were progressing on each
standard and devise plans for
how to teach and reteach skills.

These questions will help determine how to design the initial prototype of the dashboard. Key stakeholders
may also have valuable input into what the dashboard should look like and how it should function.

Set Up Data Dashboard
Build a prototype of the data dashboard
based on the defined purpose, priorities, and
requirements gathered in the design phase.
Using school-wide goals, determine which
classroom-level data points best measure
the goals to be used in the dashboard.
These measures need to be relatively
easy for teachers to collect with fidelity and
consistency. Examples of data include:
• engagement measures, such as
attendance or assignment completion;
• formative assessments, like exit tickets,
checkpoints, or independent practice; or
• summative assessments, including
project grades, course grades, or unit
tests.
Once the dashboard is established, test it for
usability by teachers.

Building Tools
for Every Level
Schools can choose between multiple,
discrete dashboards that each serve a
primary purpose, or nested dashboards that
provide a one-stop-shop data experience
for an entire school system. For ease of
access, a single, nested dashboard allows
teachers and district leaders alike to view the
same data at different levels of aggregation,
each using the dashboard to meet their
individual objectives. For example, a chief
academic officer can be monitoring school-byschool and student-group-by-student-group
progress, while a school leader can track that
data as well as classroom-by-classroom or
grade-by-grade student progress. Teachers
have access to this same data, but can
focus their efforts on understanding their
own students on a more granular level or
collaborate across their grade-level team.

Test and Improve
Test the prototype with teachers, who can give input on the tool’s utility and ease of use, using a small
set of representative data. Will users be able to easily disaggregate data at many different population
levels, including class periods and subgroups? Can they easily refresh data or upload new data?
Considering these factors will help set the dashboard up for successful use.
The initial testers should be key individuals whose buy-in will be important to garner. By engaging these
individuals early, you provide transparency into the process and can integrate their perspectives, so
they can champion the prototype’s use later. These individuals will likely be:
• lead teachers or department heads,
• coaches,
• school leaders, or
• other educators or leaders whose voices are important to new initiatives.
The first prototype of the dashboard will not be perfect, so it is essential to create something simple and
be prepared for feedback. Continue to iterate on the dashboard with users until it has the functionality
desired to meet the stated objectives.
Throughout the prototyping phase, give special attention to how users actually interact with the
dashboard. Anticipate challenges educators may have and training they may need to use the
dashboard effectively.

Routinely Assess Implementation
and Quality
After developing and refining the prototype,
consider establishing a team structure that allows
regular assessment of the implementation and
quality of the tool. A structured approach allows
teams to systematically evaluate how the tool
is being used and its effectiveness. Frequent
evaluation of sample data can provide insights
into the way individuals and teams are using
the tool, identify emerging trends, and pinpoint
barriers in implementation. NIC partners found a
quarterly review cadence — to review how the tool
is being used and to do a strategic set of empathy
interviews with users at the classroom, school
leadership, and district levels — allowed them to
make timely adjustments to the tool, integration
settings, and training plans for team members.
Backward-plan timelines for each of these steps
to occur based on the final target roll-out date. In
order to train leaders and educators on how to use
the tool before school starts in the fall, testing of
the tool should be complete by the end of the prior
school year.

Conclusion
Many schools utilize a Multi-Tiered System of Support (MTSS) — a comprehensive framework that
centers individual student needs within the wider scope of instruction, differentiation, behavioral
support, and intervention (see the California Department of Education definition) — to meet the
variety of needs of their students. When implemented effectively, MTSS empowers schools and
networks to meet the needs of every student at their current level. It can be a challenge, though, for
schools and networks to utilize the framework effectively. It involves:
• identifying the right data,
• disaggregating data in a way that highlights the students most in need of support,
• creating and executing tiered plans to close the gaps, and
• accurately monitoring the implementation of those plans.
Despite having an immense amount of data at their fingertips, schools often need support in creating
the systems and routines that are necessary to ensure their most marginalized students are receiving
the support that they need.
Over the course of three years, the Marshall Street Networked Improvement Community (NIC) saw
teachers and leaders from 10 participating charter networks implement, study, and adjust various best
practices for improving outcomes for Black and Latinx students with disabilities who are experiencing
poverty. This collection — itself an outcome of the work these schools have done — describes some of
the strongest of these practices, focusing specifically on how the participating schools improved their
existing MTSS structures. It highlights three best practices: routine data cycles, student conferencing,
and site-based teams. There are also two case studies, one focusing on how network leaders at Green
Dot Public Schools use data effectively at the network level and another on STRIVE Prep’s approach
to improving family engagement.
Through their work with the NIC, the participating schools honed their already-present MTSS structures,
focusing on making changes that prioritized the NIC’s target population and would lead directly to
positive outcomes for all students. At STEM Prep, introducing a gradebook dashboard to support
teachers in monitoring student grades in more frequent data cycles led to an increase in the percentage
of students with disabilities who had As or Bs in their classes, from 25% to 81%. Additionally, the
dashboard helped improve the experience of all students, as the percentage of students without

At STRIVE Prep, a special education math teacher piloted student conferences in his math class.

Based on this success, KIPP has built additional periods into their schedule so that special educators
can meet with more students to confer.

Key Takeaways
THE TYPE AND QUALITY OF DATA THAT SCHOOLS UTILIZE MAKES A BIG
DIFFERENCE.
One of the biggest challenges participating schools faced at the start of their work with the NIC was
that, although they had an immense amount of student data, including academic, behavioral, and social
data, they weren’t consistently focusing on the data that would drive impact on student learning and
growth. As schools work to improve their MTSS structures, an important first step is to consider what
their data tells them and what it doesn’t tell them. From there, they can focus on the actions needed to
either gather the right information or make a plan to solve for the problems they’re seeing.
SCHOOLS MUST DISAGGREGATE DATA TO BE ABLE TO DESIGN FOR PRIORITY
SUBGROUPS.
Disaggregating data, or looking at data for groups of students rather than the whole student population,
is an effective way to see which students’ needs are not being met by the current system. Too often,
schools miss gaps for subgroups, such as students with disabilities, because those gaps don’t show
up when looking at the school’s entire student population. To accurately identify which students need
higher-touch support services, schools and networks should ensure that they are examining data
across grade levels, schools, and student demographic characteristics such as English language
proficiency and special education status.
ALIGNMENT ACROSS ALL LEVELS ALLOWS THE SYSTEM TO FUNCTION SMOOTHLY.
In order for collaboration between educators to be effective, there must be specific time set aside
for deliberate co-planning. Not only that, but the time must be used intentionally to increase access
to learning for all students. The collaborating teachers and co-teachers in the NIC found that simply
meeting together is not enough. Rather, meeting with a plan and with support led to the growth in
practice and student impact that they were looking for.

The United States education system was not intentionally designed to respond to the varying needs
of students positioned furthest from opportunity in our society, including Black and Latinx students
with disabilities who are experiencing poverty. As a result, year after year, students with disabilities
repeatedly underperform their peer groups in schools across the country.1

Multi-tiered systems of support that are designed to fit into
the current education landscape, while nominally focused on
providing support to all students, frequently miss the mark
by focusing on the middle, rather than the edges.
This collection of practices highlights the important work that members of the NIC did to
ensure that their students – all of their students – received the targeted instructional and
behavioral support that they both need and deserve. Schools must follow the lead of the
NIC participants and begin by asking which of their students are not being served well
by the current system. The answer will allow them to consider how their practices might
change so that every student can succeed.
