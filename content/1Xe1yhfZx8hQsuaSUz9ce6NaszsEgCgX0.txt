Practice Overview
What is the Practice?
Student mentorship is a peer support structure that pairs two students in a mentee/mentor relationship
for regular formal and informal check-ins to build positive peer relationships and increase students’
sense of belonging.

Why is Peer-to-Peer Mentorship Important?
Developing strong relationships with peers and staff at school is important because it can impact
students’ sense of belonging and connection to their community, factors essential to students’ success
for persistence to and through K–12 schooling.1 Strong relationships are especially important for
students with disabilities who are increasingly included in educational and community settings, yet
may not always feel a sense of belonging and inclusion socially and emotionally with peers. Students
with emotional and behavioral disabilities may particularly struggle to connect and relate to their peers.
This struggle may be related to their disability, the way peers react to their disability, and sometimes a
combination of related factors. Peer-to-peer mentorship provides opportunities for students to develop
social bonds, create community, and learn new ways of relating to other students.

Why it Works
TEACHING AND MODELING DEVELOPMENT OF SOCIAL SKILLS
Students participating in peer-to-peer mentorship have the opportunity to work on important prosocial
skills that many students need modeled and reinforced as they develop relationships with peers.
Peer mentorship focuses on building prosocial skills that all students need to learn in school and that
specialized programs for students with emotional and behavioral disabilities work to teach, such as:
• offering to help someone,
• considering the feelings and needs of others,
• sharing and kindness,
• volunteering time,
• self-efficacy, and
• self-awareness.
These skills can be taught through explicit instruction in the classroom, but it is essential that students
have opportunities to practice skills in a supportive environment that provides feedback and allows
for them to make mistakes. Implementing skills with supportive peers can help students, particularly
students with emotional and behavioral disabilities, generalize their learning to their interactions with
other students and enhance their application of prosocial skills in other contexts.
STUDENTS BUILD CONNECTIONS AND COMMUNITY AT SCHOOL
As students build these interpersonal skills in their peer-to-peer mentorship program, they also gain a
sense of belonging with students who were not part of their typical social groups at school.2 Developing
these relationships with other students with disabilities enables students to relate to peers with similar
experiences and can also help them gain a positive identity and pride related to their disability.3 The
feeling of understanding from peers and the sense of belonging within a group of students create
important connections between students and their school, helping them feel more engaged, respond
more positively to authority, and improve academic achievement.4
MENTORS GAIN CONFIDENCE AND LEADERSHIP
The positive messages students receive about their disability from peer mentorship can help combat
negative and exclusionary social messages students may have previously internalized.5 Mentors also
have the opportunity to build new leadership skills as they learn how to coach and support their peers
with a similar disability.6 As mentors see the positive effects of their leadership, their confidence grows,
helping them in and out of the classroom. This creates a virtuous cycle, in which mentors’ growing
confidence helps them to further build up and support their mentees.

Bringing this Practice to Life
FROM PILOT TO PROGRESS: THE SUCCESS OF PEER MENTORING
AT MASTERY SCHOOLS
Mastery Schools’ Simon Gratz High School in Philadelphia, PA, began piloting a peer-to-peer mentorship
program in the 2021–22 school year to cultivate a sense of belonging among students in their school
community. The program paired older students from their Anchor emotional support program with younger
students, aiming to foster authentic relationships between them. Each week, mentors and mentees meet
formally and informally to nurture their relationship and collaboratively address any barriers to learning
experienced by the mentees. Over the course of two years, the Gratz team has expanded and refined the
program based on insights gained from the pilot, enabling them to involve more students and better address
student needs through this initiative.

Starting Small and Seeing Big Problems
Before the COVID-19 pandemic, Gratz was separated into two campuses. Vernetta Newman, one
of Gratz’s emotional support counselors, ran a meet-and-greet day bringing together the upper- and
lower-level students in the Anchor program. The meet-and-greet day was a time for celebration,
socializing, playing games, and presentations by upperclassmen about the Anchor program on the
upper campus. Vernetta and the Anchor staff found that these one-day events were insufficient to
create a sense of belonging and community connection for their students. This need for community
was particularly acute for students in Anchor who came from alternative placements, and others
who experienced so many suspensions that they did not feel connected to school or their peers.

The team also reflected that students in Anchor were not attending school often enough and
thought giving students a non-academic reason for coming to school might help. The team needed
to find a strategy to help their students make connections at Gratz that would help them feel
connected and want to come to school more regularly.

Getting Peer-to-Peer Mentorship Started
Once students returned to campus for in-person learning during the 2021–22 school year, Vernetta
began building Anchor’s peer-to-peer mentorship program as a way to connect older students in the
program with younger ones. Vernetta recruited outgoing, assertive students with strong attendance
records and demonstrated success within the program to serve as mentors. In the first term, three
students became mentors, each with one mentee. In order to pair mentees with their mentors,
Vernetta spoke with Anchor staff and, in some cases, staff at the middle schools attended by the
students, to deeply understand students’ personalities and needs. Unfortunately, Gratz continued to
experience temporary school closures due to COVID-19, and the mentorship pairs could only meet
regularly for about a month. However, during this time, Vernetta was able to learn important lessons
about matching students, logistical needs, and structures for formal mentorship sessions that she
could apply to the next school year.

Mentorship as a Way to Build Connection
For the 2022–23 school year, Vernetta took a different approach to identifying mentors for the
program. She realized that less outgoing students could learn the skills for good mentoring —
like how to communicate with new people — and that the program itself could teach those skills.
Vernetta developed a mentor training curriculum and guidelines with explicit expectations for
mentors. Twelve students engaged in peer-to-peer mentorship in the second year, and formal
sessions were able to happen more regularly from the start of the school year. Along with formal
sessions, Vernetta worked to build informal texting into mentor-mentee relationships to create less
structured interactions between students. Reworked meeting protocols and solutions for logistical
barriers meant that mentors and mentees were able to benefit considerably in the second year of
the mentorship program.
By the end of this second year, the peer mentorship program was running more consistently. In
end-of-year surveys, students expressed its value in helping them build connections:
• “I’ve learned how to control my emotions.”
• “I can build a relationship with other people. Let’s say I’ve been through a struggle, and they
are going through it now; I can tell them how to get out of it cause I’ve been there, done that.
Like having a problem with a teacher. I told [my mentee] to sit there, do the work, and follow
the teacher’s directions. You’ll be out of that class in no time.”
• “I was mad one day, and [my mentor] talked to me, ‘Do you need anything? Do you need
space to calm down?’ Then we just started talking about what happened.”
• “I always used to get in trouble, and he told me how to act over there, that fighting isn’t worth
it. Ain’t gonna get you nowhere.”

Learning to Improve
As Vernetta became more skilled at mitigating some of the barriers that hindered students from
attending all their formal meetings, students voiced the value they saw in peer mentorship and
expressed a desire for more mentoring sessions.
First, through some trial and error, she worked out how to ensure that mentoring sessions were held
when students could all attend. This meant making sure that sessions were not during classes that
students could not or did not want to miss. Finding a schedule that worked helped increase student
attendance at sessions. Second, to increase attendance, Vernetta created permanent laminated
passes for students to keep for the day and time peer-to-peer mentorship would be held. This way,
students did not have to request permission from teachers for a pass each time, and access to
passes was not a barrier for mentor pairs. In the end, Vernetta also realized she needed a backup
plan for mentors or mentees who ended up without their partners due to an absence.
Students reported that they valued their connections and interactions with peers that resulted from
mentorship even though pairs were not always perfect. Vernetta started to develop a rubric for
determining which students could make good mentors. She realized that some of the mentees in
the program could eventually transition to become mentors instead because of how their behavior
had progressed in the Anchor program. After the first year of implementing explicit mentor training,
Vernetta had insight into the amount and types of training and support mentors needed to build and
maintain relationships with their peers.
Whether they felt they were gaining communication, self-regulation, or leadership skills, Gratz
students enjoyed and benefited from the Anchor program’s peer-to-peer mentorship. From
2019 to 2023, during which time Gratz implemented peer-to-peer mentorship, the percentage
of Anchor students attending school at least 90% of the time increased by 26 points.

Mastery Gratz significantly reduced the attendance gap between student groups between 2019 and 2023.

How to Implement this Practice
This section provides step-by-step guidance for implementing a peer-to-peer mentorship program,
along with accompanying resources to get started.

Prepare Needed Structures
Step 1: Identify a Peer Mentorship Lead
Identify a staff member who can lead the mentorship program. School leaders can support this
program lead by giving them the authority to make decisions in the best interest of the peer mentorship
program (e.g., giving students permanent hall passes or creating a schedule for formal mentorship
sessions). The peer mentorship lead will also be more effective when given a clear set of roles and
responsibilities for their role that empower them to oversee the effort. Some of the tasks they will be
responsible for include:
• coordinating program schedules,
• creating student pairings,
• raising awareness and promoting the program among teachers,
• relaying expectations to mentors and mentees,
• monitoring formal mentorship sessions, and
• managing ongoing communication with families and students.
Because this role requires a considerable amount of time, it is
important to consider other staff responsibilities when assigning the
role. If existing responsibilities cannot be reassigned, consider whether
a stipend can offset the additional tasks for the program leader.
Mastery Schools provides a $2,000 stipend for their mentorship lead.
It is helpful to choose a program lead who deeply understands
students’ backgrounds and needs so that they can properly match
mentors and mentees, address any emotional or behavioral needs
during sessions, and communicate easily with students and families.
A staff member who does not know students well will likely need to
spend additional time talking to other staff members to gain insight into
potential mentors and mentees.

Step 2: Design Tools, Protocols, and Norms
In order to plan and implement a successful mentorship program, it is important to create specific
materials and resources that give mentors and mentees an understanding of the structure and
expectations for their participation, such as the following:
• Mentorship agreement form to set expectations for participation in the program
• Mentorship training materials (e.g., presentation, worksheet, readings) to help students
understand the purpose of peer-to-peer mentorship, their role, and how it can benefit them
• Mentorship protocol that gives students a structure to follow during their sessions to ensure they
are communicating and collaborating with their mentor or mentee
• Attendance logs to track attendance at mentorship sessions to identify which students need help
mitigating barriers
Establishing and communicating norms for the program’s operations can build buy-in among school
staff and minimize conflicts related to students missing class for mentorship sessions and submitting
make-up work. Consider a regular communication strategy to remind teachers about sessions and
obtain information about students’ make-up assignments. With teachers’ active support for the program,
some absences from mentorship sessions can be avoided.
Because other absences are unavoidable, have concrete plans in place for mentor or mentee
absences from formal mentorship sessions. Such plans might include alternative activities students do
independently or an alternative meeting protocol for temporary pairings of students who are not in an
official mentorship relationship.

Step 3: Recruit Students
The ideal recruitment strategy will depend upon the size of the population from which mentors and
mentees will be identified. If students come from a large set of over 100 students or even an entire
school, it can be helpful to have teachers make announcements, make school-wide announcements,
and put posters up around school. For smaller populations, such as those within one program or
classroom, it is best to use existing adult and student relationships to talk directly to students about the
opportunity to participate in the peer-to-peer mentorship program. This strategy can be effective when
recruiting students for a larger mentorship program as well. Encourage staff members to consider all
students as potential mentors who could benefit from developing self-confidence and leadership skills,
not just those who may already demonstrate those abilities.
Consider offering incentives to students to recruit their participation and retain them in the program
long-term. Effective incentives may differ for mentors and mentees. For example, in the first year of
their mentorship program, Gratz gave mentors a $25 gift card each quarter if they regularly participated
in mentorship (i.e., they attended 75% of mentorship sessions) but chose not to give incentives
to mentees. Mentors could earn a bonus $25 gift card if they met certain criteria (see mentorship
competencies in Step 7). The Gratz team found that students enjoyed the program enough that these
incentives were unnecessary going forward.

Step 4: Get Permission from Families
Engaging with families from the start of the program can be helpful in building buy-in and ensuring
they can support the program implementation, particularly to encourage their students’ attendance at
sessions. At the start of any peer mentorship program, schools should provide detailed information
about the program to families, including its goals, structure, activities, and expected outcomes.
This information can be shared through written materials, presentations, or virtual meetings. This
communication also allows staff to highlight the potential benefits of participating in the program for
both mentors and mentees and address any concerns about how the program will be implemented
and monitored. Mastery Gratz required all mentees and mentors in their program to have parents or
caregivers sign a consent form in order to participate. Once the program begins, regular communication
with families can provide updates on activities, progress, and any changes to the program as needed.

Step 5: Create Student Pairs
Creating cohesive student pairings is an essential
part of having a successful mentorship program.
Consider the factors that most often connect
students at school, such as:
• neighborhood,
• cultural factors (e.g., religion, ethnicity,
traditions)
• interests (e.g., video games, the military, hiking),
• TV shows,
• music,
• behaviors exhibited, and
• student schedules (e.g., out-of-school
responsibilities, hobbies).

Mentors and Mentees

attendance and tardy data to pair
students so that mentors and mentees
would not be alone during sessions. For
the second year of implementing peerto-peer mentorship, Vernetta met with

When creating peer mentor pairs, it can be helpful
to survey participating students to gather specific
information about them to guide their pairing.
An alternative strategy could be to allow students to meet in an informal setting and observe which
students naturally gravitate toward one another. A more structured version of this approach would
involve a “speed dating” scenario where mentees engage in five-minute conversations with each
mentor, aided by prompts, to determine which pairs interact most effectively together.
Have a plan for how to respond if pairings are not working and need to be changed. Pairings may not
work for a variety of reasons; it is a good exercise for both students in an unsuccessful pairing to meet
together with the mentorship lead to discuss why the pairing is not working. In situations where there is
a sensitive personal issue, one-on-one conversations with the mentorship lead can also be very helpful.
Empower the students by asking what characteristics they now know they need in a mentor or mentee,
as well as whether there is another student they think may be a better match. It is not recommended
to change another successful pairing to rematch students. Instead, look for a student not currently
engaged in peer mentorship to create new pairings.

Step 6: Create a Mentorship Schedule
Schedule times for formal mentorship sessions that are regular and consistent. Use student schedules
to help determine the best day and time for these sessions so they are least likely to impact high-needs
academic courses (i.e., English and mathematics). It will be important to communicate this schedule
with teachers, particularly general educators, who will need to excuse students from class to attend
mentorship sessions.
Consider what time of the day mentorship sessions will be least disruptive to student learning while
also encouraging students to attend school. Gratz strategically chose to have the formal mentorship
sessions during the last period of the day to support students’ timely arrival and consistent attendance
at sessions. Holding sessions at the end of the day also prevents students from struggling to reregulate for a return to an academic setting after the relative downtime allowed by the mentorship
check-in (e.g., eating a snack together or playing a game).

Put Peer Mentorship into Practice
Step 7: Train Student Mentors
In order to act as an effective mentor to peers, students acting as mentors will need training to help
them build competencies that will allow them to facilitate conversations with their mentees. A helpful
first step before designing mentor training is to know what competencies students should have when
they finish the training. The specific competencies will depend on the mentorship program’s purpose
and goal. Competencies might include:
• leading a conversation with a peer,
• filling out protocols for sessions,
• making eye contact,
• requesting adult assistance,
• using technology for effective informal communication,
• creating SMART goals, or
• asking probing questions.
Plan to conduct mentor training before the first peer-to-peer mentoring session so mentors are ready
when formal mentoring begins. Some mentors might need continuing training or support after the
initial session(s). Consider having mentors identify areas of growth they hope to work on. Use these
areas to coach them throughout the semester or year. This type of self-reflection and goal setting
for mentors can help them build important leadership skills that will help them as mentors as well as
outside of the program.

Step 8: Hold Pre-meetings
Every mentee should have a pre-meeting with their mentor and a staff member before mentoring
begins. Use this pre-meeting to make sure students know that participation is voluntary, relay clear
expectations for the program, have students sign their mentorship agreement form, and set up a
communication plan. Encourage students to exchange contact information, and remind the mentor
to reach out to their mentee before the first formal session. Starting the informal communication
between mentor and mentee during pre-meetings can be very helpful to the success of mentorship. For
example, using informal communication (e.g., text messaging), mentors can make sure mentees are at
school for formal mentorship sessions in the future. This can immediately lessen potential attendance
issues during formal sessions.

Step 9: Hold Mentorship Sessions
Peer-to-peer mentoring can happen through formal and informal interactions. Both types of interactions
are important for the mentor and mentee to bond, transfer skills, and create routines.
FORMAL SESSIONS
Students can be required to meet for formal sessions on a weekly or biweekly basis, depending on
the schedule devised. Set an expectation that students will all arrive on time to maximize the session.
Formal sessions can be as short as 15 minutes or as long as a class period, depending on the protocol
developed and the ability of the mentor to facilitate the protocol. Initially, mentors may require more time
to navigate the protocol during the first sessions. Regular, informal interactions can also reduce the
need for lengthy formal sessions.
Make sure students have comfortable seating in private, non-classroom spaces to meet with one
another. Mentoring spaces should allow conversations to be confidential. Consider having snacks
available for students to help them feel more at ease while talking.
INFORMAL SESSIONS
Informal interactions between mentors and mentees
can be used to help mentees move forward with any
goals or action items that come out of formal check-ins.
Ideally, mentors are informally communicating with their
mentees at least once a week through text, in-person
check-ins, or emails.
It is helpful to have a document or structure for mentors
to communicate to the mentorship lead that an informal
check-in happened. The mentorship lead can then
easily follow up with mentors, remind them to check in
on their mentees, and address any barriers they are
having with informal check-ins.

Summary
Mentorship can be a powerful way for students with common backgrounds and experiences
to support one another. Peer-to-peer mentorship programs that pair students who have similar
disabilities give participants additional common ground. This type of program empowers older
students to be leaders to younger students, to help teach skills, and to provide support as
someone who understands the mentees’ disabilities. Younger students have the opportunity to
connect to older students whom they can look up to and use as part of their support system,
increasing their sense of belonging at school. Participating in mentorship provides another
chance for both mentor and mentee to gain new social, academic, and leadership skills that
will help them navigate personal and professional relationships in the future. Mastery’s peerto-peer mentorship program was correlated with a substantial increase in the number of
students attending school at least 90% of the time. Their success shows the potential for using
mentorship as an effective intervention for improving attendance for students with disabilities,
especially students with emotional and behavioral disabilities.
