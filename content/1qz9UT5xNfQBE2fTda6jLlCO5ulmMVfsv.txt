Practice Overview
What is the Practice?
An intervention in which educators follow a
defined protocol to provide immediate feedback
to students on their application of taught
prosocial behavioral skills, enabling them to
effectively apply these skills in various contexts.

3.3

What is a Prosocial
Behavioral Skill?
A prosocial behavioral skill is a chain of
individual, discrete actions or cognitive
operations performed in specific situations
that demonstrate helping, sharing,
or caring for others.1 The teaching of
prosocial skills must be explicit and
comprehensive, and it relies on control
of the environment, reinforcement, and
generalization of skills across all settings.

Why is Behavioral Feedback Important?
When school teams provide direct instruction on the prosocial behavioral skills students need,
partner that instruction with discrete practice, and offer generalized feedback on those skills across
settings, schools can reduce the number of disciplinary consequences students experience. That
reduction can have a significant impact on students’ short- and long-term outcomes. Students
with emotional and behavioral disabilities receive, on average, seven disciplinary consequences
(suspension or expulsion) per year. Over 60% of students with emotional or behavioral disabilities
receive at least one disciplinary consequence per year. And almost three quarters (73%) of students
with emotional or behavioral disabilities are suspended or expelled at least once during their school
careers.2 Disciplinary consequences can increase negative stereotypes about students with emotional
and behavioral disabilities and reduce their opportunities for learning by removing them from their
classroom settings. Furthermore, it is unlikely that these types of consequences help change
behavioral outcomes for students with emotional or behavioral disabilities.3 Instead, exclusionary
practices likely contribute to the school-to-prison pipeline — that is, the way in which disciplinary
systems in K–12 schools increase the likelihood that students will enter the criminal justice system.4

Why it Works
INTERVENTIONS IN INCLUSIVE SETTINGS HELP GENERALIZE LEARNING.
Direct instruction on prosocial skills and how to generalize behavioral strategies, as well as feedback
on the use of strategies, can be more effective when students learn as often as possible in inclusive
settings, where they can encounter authentic opportunities to apply the prosocial behaviors they have
been taught. When students spend more time in inclusive settings, they interact with a more diverse
set of students. As they encounter a wider range of behaviors from these peers, they also learn and
have the opportunity to apply new strategies for engaging with these diverse behaviors.5 In addition,
by receiving behavioral feedback in an inclusive setting, students with emotional and behavioral
disabilities can experience education that is academically rigorous while also improving behaviors
long-term.
REGULAR FEEDBACK POSITIVELY REINFORCES PROSOCIAL BEHAVIORS.
Positive reinforcements (e.g., Positive Points, Kickboard Points, Dojo Points, WOW Points!) and realtime feedback (e.g., “Nice job walking into class on time,” “I appreciate you being in your seat”) for
even small steps of a behavior are effective interventions when working with students with emotional
and behavioral disabilities.6 Recognizing small gains helps maintain students’ motivation to continue
to improve toward the target behavior. Behavioral feedback has also been found to increase student
engagement in learning, while decreasing the number of times teachers attempt to correct behaviors.7
As these students spend more time focused on learning, and their teachers spend less time
addressing behavior, all students benefit from additional learning opportunities.

STAFF TRAINING LEADS TO BETTER FEEDBACK.
When schools implement deliberate staff training on how to deliver high-quality, timely, and targeted
behavioral feedback, staff are better equipped to respond to students in ways that reinforce prosocial
behaviors. With regular, high-quality feedback over time, students can develop skills to address areas
of need that result from their disability and can better use those skills in a variety of environments.
Increasing prosocial behaviors through equitably implemented feedback can lead to fewer of the
disciplinary consequences that can put students with disabilities into the school-to-prison pipeline.
Behavioral feedback instead increases the amount of time students spend in the classroom to learn
academic skills for future success.

3.7

Creating a Fully
Integrated Emotional
Support Program
Behavioral feedback is just one evidencebased practice in Mastery’s supplemental
behavioral support model. Mastery’s
Anchor program is a larger structure
that uses evidence-based interventions
to support and strengthen the impact that
behavioral feedback has on students.
These practices include daily conferences,
discrete skills instruction of the Student
Achievement Model (SAM) and a
CASEL-aligned curriculum, IEP goals
aligned to individual student SAM skills,
therapeutically trained education and
clinical staff, de-escalation structures, a
token economy, differentiated instruction,
and data-driven decision-making in
meetings. While research demonstrates
that behavioral feedback alone is an
effective practice for students with
emotional and behavioral disabilities, its
effectiveness is amplified by the structured
implementation of all of these practices.

Using Data to Keep Improving
Mastery uses data from the behavioral
feedback process to improve the
implementation of the practice. On a
biweekly basis, the Anchor team uses
Kickboard data to track the number
of positive and learning points each
student receives per period and per day
and analyzes how that data relates to
attendance, disciplinary referrals, and
Observation rubric for assessing a counselor's fidelity of
suspensions. They also look at student
implementing behavioral feedback
responses on daily conferencing sheets
as part of this analysis. The program lead
conducts regular observations of counselors while they are giving behavioral feedback. These
observations ensure students are receiving feedback for their behavioral skills, that counselors are
using the feedback scripts with fidelity, and that counselors are accurately identifying opportunities
for students to demonstrate SAM skills across settings. This observational data is used for biweekly
counselor training so implementation of behavioral feedback continues to improve.
The impact of the behavioral feedback practice at Gratz’s campus has been significant. Decreases
in the number of suspensions for students in the Anchor program are much larger than those seen
for other students in special education. Since 2019, the percentage of students in special education
who have at least one suspension has declined by 22 points, while the percentage of students in
Anchor who have at least one suspension has declined by 36 points.

How to Implement this Practice
This section provides step-by-step guidance for implementation of a behavioral feedback program
specific to a school and community context, along with accompanying resources to get started.

Lay the Groundwork
Step 1: Train Program Staff
Program staff will benefit from training on the following:
• The skills that they need to teach students (e.g., SAM skills, Collaborative Problem Solving)
• How to recognize opportunities for students to use their skills in different types of classroom
settings (e.g., specialized program classes, interventions classes, core general education classes,
electives)
• How to provide learning and positive feedback with the use of a script
• How to enter data into a shared system
• How to use data with students
When training staff in the practice of behavioral feedback, it is essential to identify priority behavioral
skills for the start of the year and focus training on how staff can provide targeted feedback to students
on those skills. Staff training also needs to cover the skills necessary for staff to implement the program.
For example, staff may need to know how to enter data into data systems as soon as school begins but
can learn how to use data with students later in the school year. Throughout the year, training can build
the full set of behavioral skills staff should know and continue to strengthen the implementation of the
feedback routine with students. This training should be incorporated into regularly held team meetings,
along with the review of data from feedback.
Consider how a coaching structure might also
be used to ensure that staff continue to learn
and grow in their implementation of the practice.
All school staff, particularly classroom teachers,
can benefit tremendously from training on
the basic concepts of behavioral feedback.
Teachers can also benefit from understanding
the types of skills their students may be learning
throughout the year and the scripts that program
staff use daily. Whole-school training requires
coordinating with school leadership, but training
can also be done during grade-level meetings or
even through individual coaching sessions.

Step 2: Create a Schedule
for Staff Rounds and
Manager Observations
Scheduling staff so that they can support
students both in general education classes and
in specialized program classes can be very
challenging. It is important to individualize the
amount of support provided to each student in
classrooms when creating the schedule for staff
rounds. A student’s level of need likely varies by
class period, by subject area, and with regard to
different adult and peer relationships. This might
mean, for example, that a given staff member
visits one student for 30 minutes each period
of the school day but visits another student for
only 15 minutes during one class period in which
that student has particular challenges. Over
time, data on how students respond to feedback
can help teams adjust the schedule to most
effectively meet students’ needs.

Ensuring Sufficient
Staffing
Adequate staffing ensures successful
implementation of behavioral feedback and
that students receive the necessary support
to acquire new behavioral skills. Mastery
generally recommends that for every 12
students in three consecutive grades (i.e.,
K–2, 3–5, etc.) Anchor staffing includes:
• 1 full-time emotional support teacher/
case manager
• 1 part-time clinician (20 or 29 hours
per week)
• 2 part-time classroom counselors
The team also regularly analyzes outcome
data (e.g., student infractions, amount of
behavioral feedback, teacher and staff
perceptions) to assess whether the staffing
of each role is sufficient to support students.

Within the schedule, program leads can identify
regular opportunities to observe staff enacting
behavioral feedback. Observations will allow them to provide real-time coaching and to collect data on
the implementation of the behavioral feedback practice. This data can be tracked both for individual
staff members and at the program level.

Step 3: Introduce Counselors and Behavioral Feedback
to All School Staff
It is important that classroom teachers and staff implementing behavioral feedback are aligned on what
this intervention looks like across classrooms. General education teachers may only receive minimal
professional development around how to provide students with behavioral feedback in their classrooms.
In addition, modified schoolwide behavior systems can help teachers and staff understand the
alternative ways they may need to respond to students with emotional and behavioral disabilities, as
these may be different than their standard behavioral response. These teachers can gain considerable
knowledge about behavioral skills and how feedback is implemented through regular interactions with
the program staff who implement the behavioral feedback practice.
Program staff implementing behavioral feedback can themselves be more effective when they have
the opportunity to communicate with classroom teachers. Schools may want to schedule regular time
for program staff to formally collaborate with classroom teachers. These structured meetings can
be used to discuss individual student needs, the different skills behavioral feedback addresses, and

The implementation of a behavioral feedback practice establishes
a continuous cycle in which student needs are identified, students
are taught skills, students receive behavioral feedback across
instructional settings, and data is used for progress monitoring

Step 5: Teach Students Skills and Behavioral Feedback Routines
Create structured times for explicit instruction
of behavioral skills, sub-tasks, and prerequisite
skills. Just like academic skills, explicit instruction
is a necessary step in helping students learn
new skills and behaviors. Structured times may
include specific courses, pull-out small groups,
or individual counseling sessions.
These taught skills will be reinforced by
behavioral feedback, so students will need to
learn what that feedback will look like in the
classroom, as well as the appropriate way to
respond to feedback. They should know that
when they have the opportunity to demonstrate
the skills they have learned in real time, they
will receive praise along with either positive or
negative consequences. Students will also need
to understand that through positive feedback they
can earn positive points for the program store.

Step 6: Staff Follow Rounds Schedule to Observe
and Support Students
Program staff follow the predetermined schedule to join students in their classrooms throughout the
day. Staff enter classrooms to observe specific students during parts of the school day when the
students are likely to have opportunities to demonstrate the skills they are working on. If necessary,
staff can also create opportunities for students to demonstrate skills if they do not naturally happen. For
example, staff can ask a student to work actively on an assignment until finished or to get the teacher’s
attention appropriately if they have a question. When the student correctly demonstrates their skill (e.g.,
remaining on task), behavioral feedback using positive praise language helps reinforce the skill. The
staff member gives this praise using predefined steps and an example script, such as the following:
Steps

Example Script

1. Descriptive praise

John, you are remaining on task by sitting in your seat, having still
hands, and working quietly on your workbook pages.

2. Reason

This is important so you are not disrupting others and you are able
to focus and complete your work.

3. Request acknowledgement

Does this make sense?

4. Positive consequence/reinforcer

You earned 2 points for treating others with respect.

If the student has the opportunity to demonstrate the skill but is unsuccessful (e.g., does not remain on
task), the staff member provides behavioral feedback using “learning language” to redirect the student,
explain the appropriate behavior, and prompt the student again to practice the skill. Learning language
includes the following steps:
Steps

Example Script

1. Initial praise/empathy

Hey John, thanks for listening to me.

2. Description of inappropriate behavior

I see you are not remaining on task. Ms. Johnson asked you to
quietly complete your workbook pages, but you are out of your seat
talking to your friend.

3. Negative consequence

You earned two learning points for not remaining on task.

4. Description of appropriate behavior

Make sure you are remaining on task by sitting in your seat and
working quietly on the workbook pages assigned by Ms. Johnson.

5. Request acknowledgement

Can you get back to work for me?

For progress to occur, students need multiple opportunities each day to practice skills with the support
of behavioral feedback. Ideally, the established daily rounds schedule accommodates multiple
opportunities for practice and behavioral feedback from staff.

Step 7: Students Conference and Reflect with Staff
Students’ daily reflection on their behavior and data is important for learning prosocial behavioral skills
and for changing how they respond to antecedents in the future. At the end of the school day, conduct
end-of-day conferences with students to facilitate reflections on:
• points and feedback they received,
• the reasons why they received them,
• what they should have done differently, and
• what they plan to do the next day.
After conferencing, students also have the
opportunity to add up all of their positive points
and subtract the learning points they earned
that day. With the net points calculated, students
can purchase items from the school store or
choose to save their points, which accumulate

Step 8: Staff Reviews Data and Makes Changes as Needed
Behavioral feedback data can be used to help an emotional support program continuously adapt and
improve for students. Program leads, network leaders, or teams can analyze measures like these on a
weekly or biweekly basis:
• Total number of feedback opportunities
• Ratio of positive to learning feedback
• Percent of feedback that is positive
• Percent of feedback that is learning
• Total number of opportunities for each student to practice skill(s) across settings
• Consistency of feedback throughout day (i.e., a similar amount of feedback given each
class period)

Summary
In order to implement a full continuum of services for students with more intensive behavioral needs
and to ensure these students have access to general education classrooms, schools must design and
implement high-quality behavioral interventions that are adaptable across environments. Behavioral
feedback uses the underpinnings of research on positive reinforcement and token economies to help
students recognize when they have used the skills they are working on or missed an opportunity to
respond differently. Behavioral feedback also helps students learn how to generalize their skills to a
variety of classrooms and groups of students in general and special education settings. Students also
work with staff on daily reflections of their behavior and planning for the next day, setting themselves up
for more successful days.
By investing in their school staff through initial and continuous training, schools can implement
behavioral feedback with fidelity and quality across learning environments. With these supports in
place, students can internalize their areas of growth, reflect on their actions, and see positive change
in their behaviors over time. Mastery School’s reduction in the percentage of Anchor program students
who experience a suspension demonstrates the potential that effective implementation of behavioral
feedback can have for students with disabilities in inclusive settings.
