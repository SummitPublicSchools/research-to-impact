Why are Site-Based Data Teams Important?
Structured meetings that focus on analyzing student data are necessary for educators to problem-solve
how to best ensure all students receive the education they need. In many cases, schools already have
student study team (SST) structures in place to address the needs of at-risk students through a multitiered system of supports (MTSS). These types of intervention team meetings help teachers implement
high-quality instruction that focuses on the needs of all students, particularly Black and Latinx students
with disabilities experiencing poverty. This high-quality instruction can reduce the need for more
intensive intervention, allowing students with disabilities to remain in the general education classroom
as much as possible.
Site-based data teams use a similar approach, but they look at data using different lenses, which are
determined by the team’s purpose.

Why it Works
Site-based teams directly impact student learning experiences by ensuring that instruction is datadriven and based on individual student needs. They also work indirectly by strengthening educators’
knowledge and capacity to address these needs. Teams of teachers collaborating create opportunities
for peer learning among teachers to build collective expertise.1 In particular, the diverse experience and
knowledge of each member can be transferred to others as team members work together to address
challenges. Teams that also adopt and implement a data-driven decision-making process can further
increase teacher and school capacity and improve student achievement.2
Increasing teacher capacity through site-based teams is particularly important for students of color and
students with disabilities who are more likely to be placed with the least skilled teachers.3 Site-based
teams can help less experienced teachers gain important pedagogical knowledge that will help them
educate all learners.
Furthermore, site-based teams aid both newer and veteran teachers to grow their knowledge and
capacity for making data-driven decisions about student needs and to reflect on their own practices. As
a result, all students receive more targeted instruction, fewer students need additional intervention, and
more students with disabilities can be educated within the general education classroom.4

Bringing this Practice to Life
GRADE-LEVEL TEAMS USE DATA TO DESIGN
ACCELERATION OPPORTUNITIES
It can be challenging in the secondary context to find opportunities to intervene and re-teach content to small
groups of students. To meet the challenge, Summit Atlas created a weekly student support block dedicated
to targeted instructional grouping to ensure students gain skills they have not yet mastered in class. To use
this time most effectively, Summit Atlas staff need to use data to target the courses, content, and skills that
will benefit students the most.

Understanding the Challenge
During the period of remote learning caused by the COVID-19 pandemic, Summit Atlas struggled
to help students not yet mastering cognitive skills, a set of learning outcomes for Summit Public
Schools graduates. At the heart of the struggle was the difficulty of providing “just-in-time” support
to the students experiencing barriers with remote learning. Atlas’s teachers had trouble knowing
what the students’ needs were and finding time in the daily schedule to address those needs. To
make that time available, Atlas created a block each week (based on a structure developed at the

Transitioning from Virtual to In-Person Norms
During COVID virtual school, solving for “where” acceleration blocks would meet proved relatively
easy. Staff sent students Zoom links that students could use to meet, with little delay, the teachers

became more challenging to solve for “where.”
To tackle this issue, Summit Atlas Director of Learning Experience Kristina Brawley and Summit

could apply a similar approach in the school building. In the physical space, they would need to
communicate to students further in advance because the rooms for acceleration blocks would
change, and unlike virtual school, a simple click would not lead them to the correct teacher.
Additionally, as with any changes to physical space, school culture needed to be of foremost
concern for implementation. While Kristina proposed changing class lists for acceleration blocks on
a daily basis to maximize the “just-in-time” learning, Dan suggested making these changes once
every two weeks to minimize disruptions that could negatively impact school culture. Ultimately,
they agreed to start with once-a-week roster changes for acceleration blocks, in an effort to both
rapidly meet student needs and minimize disruptions to school culture.

Building a Tool to Support Rostering
To facilitate the grade-level team members’ rostering of students into classes based on data,
Kristina connected with Linh Hoang, data lead at Summit Public Schools, for help in building a
scheduling support tool. Kristina and Linh began meeting on a weekly basis to clarify the tool’s
requirements and how teachers would need to use it. Kristina communicated specifically what data
she needed to see, why she needed to see it, and how it would help to improve student outcomes
and efficiencies for teachers. Following their meetings, Linh iterated on the scheduling tool to
support those exact needs. In time, the scheduling tool developed into an early version of the
acceleration block planning tool that became a lynchpin of the grade-level team meeting.

IMPROVING THE PRACTICE BY BUILDING DATA ANALYSIS SKILLS
While supporting grade-level teams in rostering students weekly, Dan and Kristina noticed that
teachers were strong in considering the “who” of their acceleration blocks, but they were not yet as
strong in the “what” and “how” aspects of the steps needed to plan blocks. Their teachers needed
more support in analyzing student progress data to understand student needs and how to respond
to them.
Inspiration for building data analysis skills in teachers struck after Dan participated in a networkwide data protocol during a meeting of all Summit principals. At the meeting, the principals used a
protocol that involved analyzing data, setting a goal for a priority metric, identifying actions to move
toward that goal, and receiving feedback from peer principals. Dan recognized this protocol was
a modified version of a Plan-Do-Study-Act cycle to meet stated goals and could also benefit his
teachers’ development of their data analysis skills.
After the principals’ meeting, Dan suggested creating a grade-level team meeting protocol: first
have teachers use a similar simplified goal-action-feedback protocol, and only then have gradelevel teams determine how to roster students into classrooms.
Today, the weekly grade-level team meeting protocol looks like this:
10 min.

The learning specialist shares updates about students for the week.

10 min.

Teachers identify student needs and make a plan to meet the needs:
1. Teachers use student progress data to identify the student needs that will make the
biggest impact.
2. Teachers make a plan to address the need within the following week.
3. Teachers set a goal for improved student performance.
4. Teachers give one another feedback on their plans.

25 min.
or less

Teachers roster students in acceleration blocks, according to the intervention plans. If more
than one teacher adds a student to their roster, the team discusses prioritization of needs to
determine the optimal class for the student to join.

MAKING COLLABORATIVE DECISIONS
Summit Atlas grade-level teams often have to negotiate where a student will receive the
most benefit during their acceleration blocks when they need intervention in more than one class. In
these cases, teachers balance students’ multiple needs with other factors, such as:
• content prioritization (e.g., distance from mastery, current grade, value of assignments),
• relationships with teachers,
• other services students are entitled to in their IEPs, and
• student interest in the subject.
Atlas school leaders give teams the autonomy to make these decisions using data and provide
additional support when needed.

Acceleration Blocks in Action
Every Monday morning, students receive an email from the scheduling tool that tells them which
class to attend for their weekly acceleration block. Teachers then enact the plans they created for
the “who” (students they’re focusing on), the “what” (the way students will show mastery), and the
“how” (the strategies teachers proposed to their grade-level team and received feedback on).

How to Implement this Practice
The steps below provide step-by-step guidance for implementation of site-based teams specific to
school and community context, along with accompanying resources to get started.

Lay the Groundwork for Site-Based Data Teams
Step 1: Define Team Purpose
School leaders and leadership teams should have a clear vision for why a team of educators is meeting.
Various site-based teams will have different purposes based on the group of students they are focusing
on and the problem they are trying to solve. The team’s goal needs to be clear and within the team’s
locus of control.
The table below presents examples of possible team structures and purposes:

Team

Purpose

Grade-Level

Foster collaboration among educators within the same grade, allowing them to
share insights, discuss student progress, plan across subjects, address challenges,
and coordinate efforts to ensure a cohesive and supportive learning environment
for students within that grade level. These meetings serve as a platform for effective
communication, data analysis, and collaborative decision-making to enhance the
overall educational experience for students in that specific grade.

Departmental

Collaboratively address curricular concerns within the content area, particularly
as it relates to vertical alignment across grade-levels. These meetings could also
be used to discuss types of accommodations, modifications, or interventions that
pertain to the subject area.

Intervention

Analyze student data to identify individual students who need more intensive levels
of intervention in or outside of their classes. This team will also monitor data for
students who receive these interventions to determine whether interventions are
working effectively, new interventions are needed, or additional support may be
needed from special education.

Step 2: Determine Team Members
The choice of team members should align with the purpose of the team. Ideally, teams consist of
individuals with varied viewpoints on the same issue or group of students. By leveraging diverse
perspectives in any team, schools can more holistically and effectively address student needs. For gradelevel teams, this might include a school counselor or special educator who works with that grade level.

Step 3: Train Team Members on Data Analysis
Using summer or beginning-of-year training to build necessary mindsets related to data analysis and
the purpose of the team will help create the foundation for the team throughout the year. Professional
development throughout the school year can then build team members’ common understanding,
language, and capacity for data analysis. Both alignment of staff mindsets and technical training will
help ensure consistency across the team regardless of each member’s role.

Step 4: Develop Meeting Resources
PROTOCOL
Draft a team data protocol aligned to the purpose and vision of the team that allows for a one-hour
meeting. The protocol needs to ensure that all team members do at least the following:
1. Review relevant data. Look for patterns, trends, and outliers.
2. Pay special attention to subgroups and equity.
3. Make sense of the data. Try to understand why the data look the way they do.
4. Plan for how to respond to the data, answering the questions:
– What will be done?
– Which students need support?
– Who will take the action?
– Where and when will this happen?
– How will you know if it worked?
5. Give and get feedback from another teammate or the whole team.
The protocol should take into account the amount of support team members will need with data analysis
and problem-solving, both individually and as a group. Some teams might develop a protocol in which
teachers make sense of their data individually and then come together as a group for problem-solving.

ACTION STEPS BANK
School leaders will need to create a resource of different action steps or interventions that team
members can choose from when developing a plan for how to respond to their data. Action steps
should be feasible, timely, and in the teacher or team’s locus of control. It is a good idea to start a
basic prepopulated list based on research. The team can expand the list over time as they brainstorm
new ideas during team meetings. Teams can also add to their bank of action steps information on how
students have responded to the actions or how easy they were to implement with fidelity. Over time,
team members will see how different interventions work for different students and for the varied needs
of those students, giving them the self-efficacy and empowerment to keep trying new options when
prior strategies have not worked.

Step 5: Identify the Data for Analysis
The team itself must determine what data is most applicable for the team to analyze based on its
purpose and goals. This determination should balance team objectives with school and network
priorities in order for it to be as useful as possible. While schools and networks may prioritize outcomes
such as test scores or class grades, teams can choose data that is more connected to daily instruction.
Changes will be more apparent in these more proximal measures than in less frequently collected
measures (e.g., summative assessments, state standardized assessments).

Misaligned data will, over time, erode buyin from team members. Early on, Summit

When team members are able to express expectations and agree to norms that everyone will hold,
frustrations and disagreements are less likely to occur in the future. In the process of agreeing to

Create a Schedule that
Works for Everyone
Scheduling team meetings may seem obvious, but
it can often pose an unexpected challenge. It is
important to try to reduce all possible conflicts for team
members. For instance, consider how to schedule
meetings so that special educators who support
students in multiple subjects can attend each contentarea meeting and not end up double-booked. This is
made easier when school leaders, leadership teams,
and site-based teams collaborate on the scheduling
systems to ensure that all necessary members can
attend meetings. To reduce potential conflicts, schools
can use a shared school-wide calendar to schedule
meetings so that all staff members can see when
various team meetings are held.

WHAT DO SITE-BASED DATA TEAM MEETINGS LOOK LIKE?
The following example illustrates what a site-based data team meeting can look like.
Some teams will have team members create their own individual goals and/or create
team-wide goals that all members work toward. Consider what both the team and
the students might benefit from most.
Step 1 (Independent)

Find trends in the current data.

Step 2 (Independent)

Develop a strategy to impact that student data
in the next week (e.g., small group instruction,

Step 9: Enact Plans and Follow Up
The most difficult step in the process for site-based teams is implementing the action steps determined
in the meeting. This step requires team members to enact plans with fidelity and quality so that the
data eventually shows improvement as a result. Furthermore, some plans may not work, for a variety
of reasons, forcing the team to reconsider the chosen actions. And while the team needs to hold its
members accountable for enacting the plan, there should be a considerable amount of grace given
when a plan does not work out. Providing this courtesy helps all members feel like they can continue
to innovate and persevere. A certain amount of failure has to be tolerated — even celebrated — before
trying the next possible strategy for responding to data.

Practice Snapshot
COLLEGIATE LEADER DATA MEETINGS
How can a school foster collaboration between special and general educators to enhance learning
outcomes for students with disabilities?
One promising approach is an emerging practice called “data teaming.” Data teaming involves regular
meetings among school stakeholders to collectively analyze student needs and develop intervention
strategies. This collaborative process can effectively break down silos and create a more inclusive and
effective learning environment for all students.
During the 2022–23 school year, Rosenwald Collegiate Academy (RCA), a Collegiate Academies public
charter high school in New Orleans, implemented data teaming after noticing a troubling trend: Special
education teachers weren’t seeing the impact of specialized instruction without teaming and alignment from
general education teachers, while general education teachers struggled to provide differentiation and reteaching to support students with disabilities. As a result, both general education students and students with
disabilities were struggling to achieve content mastery.

The Journey
Sonia Scott, the director of special education, and Samara Levy, the director of academics, had
been seeking new ways to improve content mastery for their students with disabilities.
Although RCA had implemented co-teaching—a collaborative teaching structure aimed at
enhancing teachers’ ability to provide coordinated and individualized student support—it did not
produce the desired learning outcomes the school had anticipated, especially for students with

disabilities. Sonia and Samara noticed that co-teachers tended to stick to a single co-teaching

students’ needs. Moreover, they observed that communication between general education and
special education teachers was challenging, partly due to conflicting meeting times. Although both

After reflecting on the school’s co-teaching history and efforts, Sonia and Samara formulated a new
hypothesis. They believed that implementing a school-wide practice of utilizing student progress
data to identify academic needs, selecting suitable co-teaching models, and fostering informationsharing between general and special educators would enhance the co-teaching process and, in
turn, lead to improved learning outcomes, especially for students with disabilities.

Setting Enabling Meeting Structures
First, Sonia and Samara changed the school’s faculty meeting infrastructure. Historically, Sonia
and Samara had met weekly with one another to understand student needs and then would meet
separately once a week with general education and special education teachers.
However, to better facilitate information sharing, Sonia and Samara changed their meeting
approach.
• First, Sonia and Samara would continue to meet weekly, often three times a week, to align on
student needs.
• Then, Sonia and Samara would systematically bring instructional coaching teams to observe
classes and review data. Instructional coaches met with the co-teaching pairs biweekly to

To introduce the new data teaming protocol and meeting structure, Sonia and Samara brought

After introducing the new data teaming protocol and meeting structure with the full staff, Sonia,
Samara, and the school started implementing.

Making Improvements to the Practice
Initially meeting once a week 1:1 as directors, Sonia and Samara noticed that they still needed to
have multiple informal 1:1 check-ins beyond the once-a-week meeting. Realizing the benefits of the
more frequent check-ins, Sonia and Samara adjusted their practice in the second semester. From
the first semester to the second semester, Sonia and Samara increased their 1:1 meetings to three
times a week, comprising two 75-minute sessions and a brief 10-minute huddle.
Reflecting on their changed approach, Sonia noted, “This semester we approached it completely
differently. At the last minute last semester, we were making sure to get students to master
standards. This semester, we are making sure that we are monitoring the progress of data together.
Now, we are already meeting student learning goals at the beginning of the semester.”

The Results
At the end of the school year 2022–23, 92% of RCA’s students with disabilities achieved
Mastery on their LEAP and/or April Dunn projects, up from 44% at the end of the school year
2020–21.
Moreover, data teaming has had a significant positive effect on RCA’s school culture. By involving
all instructional leaders and teachers in the process, RCA created an environment that fostered
collaboration, innovation, and a shared sense of responsibility for student success. Sonia reflected
on the importance that data teaming had in improving collaboration, sharing: “Collaboration was
a new system and structure that came from this teaming structure. We had different teachers
collaborating with one another to support in areas where we knew we needed the most support.
Our teaming structure brought together ownership and teamwork.”

ESTABLISHING EFFECTIVE DATA ROUTINES IN SITE-BASED TEAMS

Summary
Site-based teams have the potential to provide proactive support to educator teams,
programs, or entire schools by using data to identify issues and develop action
steps. Teams should be intentional about the data and action steps they take to
ease the burden for others and function as a support system based on the level of
needs identified in the data. This can help ensure that students, especially those with
disabilities who are Latinx, Black, or living in poverty, are able to receive the appropriate
amount of academic support to make progress. To do this, formal structures and strong
data that support decision-making are essential for the team’s activities to be effective
and centered on improving outcomes for students. Including diverse team members
in these processes helps educational systems stay aligned in their school culture and
actions to improve outcomes for all students. When diverse teams work together with
the same purpose, they can strategically use their collective expertise to best support
student learning and outcomes.
